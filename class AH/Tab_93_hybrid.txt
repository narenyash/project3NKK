In my academic pursuit, I am passionately drawn towards the creation of Machine Learning (ML) algorithms that strike a harmonious balance between data, model, and system efficiency – a symphony that elegantly solves real-world problems in Natural Language Processing (NLP) and Computer Vision (CV). As we traverse an era where Artificial Intelligence is increasingly propelled by data and computational power, the significance of mastering data representations through meticulously optimized methods cannot be overstated.

Recently, there has been a substantial increase in the magnitude and expense associated with ML models, making participation in the development of these cutting-edge models exclusive to a privileged few within resource-abundant institutions. As an independent researcher, my limited resources have honed my ability to approach experimentation with a thoughtful precision and frugality.

In the upcoming years at Carnegie Mellon University during my Ph.D., I aim to focus my efforts on designing methods that empower us to construct efficient models and systems with minimal computational and data resources, thus lowering the barrier for crafting the ML technologies of the future. Hereafter, I delve into my research experiences and outline my intended trajectory.

Data Efficiency: The most significant constraints to developing and deploying real-world ML systems in the Global South are extremely low-resourced data and compute constraints. In my first research project, I developed a sign-to-text & speech system to reduce the communication barrier between the hearing-impaired community and the mainstream populace in sub-Saharan Africa. Prior to this work, there was no available dataset for sign languages in Africa. To this end, I created the first ever large-scale and publicly available sign language dataset in Africa (with the help of a TV sign language broadcaster and two special education schools in Nigeria). After developing several baseline models using image classification and object detection (with You-Only-Look-Once and single-shot-detector) architectures, I deployed the best-performing model (accuracy up to 98%) for real-time conversion of sign words/phrases to text and speech. The work was published at IJCAI22 after a spotlight presentation at the 2021 NeurIPS ML4D workshop. However, while the work garnered awards and media fanfare for solving a critical-yet-overlooked societal problem, the pains of creating a pioneer dataset with my meager resources got me thinking about how to build data-efficient algorithms for low-resourced scenarios even beyond NLP. And in cases where some form of data is available, how do we approach the problems that arise when evaluating in out-of-domain settings or class imbalance which is prevalent in many CV tasks? I am interested in exploring optimal transport and geometric data manipulations to solve this family of problems.

In my pursuit of revolutionizing machine learning, I was captivated by the promise of Federated Learning (FL), a collaborative approach that could unlock the potential of dynamic models across multiple devices. However, I soon encountered formidable obstacles: the communication and computational inefficiencies that plagued FL, particularly the degradation of model performance due to federated averaging's struggle with heterogeneous data distributions among workers. To tackle these hurdles, I embarked on a research journey, focusing on implementing efficient evolutionary algorithms as secondary methods for FL. By harnessing the power of particle swarm optimization, I managed to significantly reduce local computational costs in federated training.

My exploration didn't stop there; I delved into various cost-cutting strategies within FL. Although many attempts resulted in failures—such as my endeavor to create faster and cheaper communication among clients using decentralized mutable torrents in decentralized FL, or my implementation of neural architectural search in a federated setting that led to new sets of problems—each failure served as a valuable learning experience. This journey fueled my curiosity to develop more efficient optimization methods beyond the conventional gradient descent approach. I found myself drawn to understanding the intricacies of neural networks, with a particular focus on how they learn and what they learn.

Moving forward, my research will concentrate on accelerating both local and global convergence in an economical manner while training neural networks. I am eager to uncover methods that can expedite this process without compromising computational efficiency or sacrificing model performance—the keys to unlocking the full potential of FL and propelling machine learning into a new era of collaboration and innovation.

In the realm of computational efficiency, my most recent endeavor delved into devising strategies to adapt Vision Transformer (ViT) architectures for deployment on mobile and edge devices, platforms where resource-intensive models would be predominantly utilized in practical scenarios. These devices are characterized by significantly limited processing power and memory bandwidth, necessitating lighter models with fewer parameters, compact sizes, and minimal inference latency. Upon examining various transformer-based architectures tailored for edge devices, I discerned recurring design patterns. Notably, these designs predominantly incorporated Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP)-based structures or sparse models; some even combined transformer and convolutional neural networks. Quantization, pruning, and other sparsification techniques are routinely employed on the trained models. Previous research has consistently established a balance between compression and model performance as a crucial factor. My passion lies in pioneering innovative methodologies that allow for the training, evaluation, and deployment of neural networks within resource-restricted environments without compromising model performance. Compression techniques such as the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis have been employed to train sparser neural networks equivalent in test performance to their dense counterparts. However, recent findings suggest that these techniques may lack robustness when faced with class imbalance and can produce less interpretable models with less confident predictions. This is an intriguing challenge I aim to address; my goal is to develop sparser models that not only exhibit the aforementioned challenges' resilience but also provide clearer insights through more reliable and assertive predictions.
