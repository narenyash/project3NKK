Nestled within the vibrant intellectual ecosystem of the Indian Institute of Technology Kanpur, I immersed myself in an undergraduate research project that sparked a passion for exploring the intricacies of complex adaptive systems. Collaborating closely with distinguished faculty from the Industrial and Chemical Engineering departments, I delved into crowd simulation, meticulously calibrating popular models against local Indian data to evaluate their resilience in the face of diverse customs and cultures. This endeavor offered a profound insight: even the simplest rules can give birth to intricate patterns of behavior.

My mentor's captivating discourses on the groundbreaking work of the Santa Fe Institute further kindled my fascination with complexity science, piquing my curiosity about how these systems function and evolve over time. This exhilarating journey through the labyrinth of complex adaptive systems has left me yearning to delve deeper, making graduate studies in this field an inevitable pursuit.

Upon graduation and securing a position as a site-reliability engineer at a prominent tech corporation, I found myself immersed in the vibrant, intellectual atmosphere of San Francisco's complexity conference. Surrounded by esteemed PhDs and industry trailblazers, an impromptu working group was formed, driven by a shared vision to leverage complexity science methodologies in analyzing physiological data derived from wearable devices publicly accessible.

The synergy of our diverse backgrounds - spanning multiple disciplines - sparked stimulating discussions that fueled our collective passion for this interdisciplinary endeavor. As we embarked on this journey, I was captivated by the intricacies and breadth of complexity research I encountered. From the labyrinthine pages of journals dedicated to fractal physiology to the vast network of citations mapping dynamical neuroscience, it became clear that my curiosity in this field would be ceaselessly rewarded with a wealth of unexplored territory.

In my industrial engineering curriculum, I found courses like operations research, systems simulation, and machine learning the most intriguing. This interest led me to work with Dr. Sergiy Butenko of Texas A&M to investigate how the ratings of 700 popular movies relate to the global structural properties of their character graphs. Beginning with initial conditions and working towards desired outcomes challenges my creativity and I enjoy applying the probabilistic, computational, and optimization techniques in my courses to real-world problems.

In the realm of my B.S. studies, an exceptional opportunity presented itself: collaborating with a professor in teaching his course. This role allowed me to offer supportive guidance during office hours and exam review sessions, fostering a sense of approachability that I found deeply rewarding. Inspired by this experience, I sought out an opportunity to give back beyond the confines of my academic pursuits. I joined Big Brothers Big Sisters, a U.S.-based program that pairs underprivileged high school students with college-educated mentors, eager to make a difference in the lives of those who may lack the resources and support I've been fortunate enough to have. As I embark on my master’s journey at Chalmers, my ambition is to continue in this vein, serving as a dedicated teacher or research assistant. My passion for education extends beyond the classroom, and I am committed to using my skills and knowledge to serve others.

In my current role, I developed a proactive reputation at Apple, creating many automation scripts that reduce labor and increase insight into the health of our clusters and technologies. With no formal training on the technologies, these skills were attained through cultivated relationships with subject matter experts and sweat equity. In both industry and academia, statistics, insight, and prediction and control are where my primary interests lie, and by eagerly seeking out areas for advancement I will be well-equipped to handle the academic and research stresses of the CAS program.

In my quest for graduate studies, I sought out a rigorous program focusing on complex adaptive systems – not merely a niche interest, but a vibrant intellectual community of researchers from diverse disciplines. I aspire to be immersed in an environment teeming with complexity-focused scholars, rather than confined to a narrow circle of faculty with only casual curiosity in this field. Chalmers University, with its vast array of active researchers and faculty spanning numerous domains, provides an ideal setting that fulfills this ambition. Moreover, the prospect of collaborative partnerships bridging the Atlantic offers not just academic alliances, but potential research collaborators, making a European education not only appealing, but strategically advantageous in my pursuit of intellectual growth and discovery.

By studying and conducting research at Chalmers, I hope to set myself up for maximal fulfillment in further studies through publishing several peer-reviewed papers. After graduate studies I plan to begin the professorship journey towards tenure. Having been fortunate to spend a year living abroad I am open to working in any culture or society where my ambitions are likely to be realized. Living and working in Sweden after graduation would certainly an opportunity.

Although I am open to a variety of research, there are several professors in the CAS program whose projects are especially appealing to me: Kristian Lindgren (information theory and agent-based economics), Kolbjörn Tunström (swarming and nonlinear dynamics), and Claes Andersson (wicked societal systems). Studying these professors’ papers has given me a sense that Chalmers’ MSc CAS program is a great match for my interests and future ambitions.
