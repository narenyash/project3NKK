Statement of Purpose
Yash Mehta

It was only after my first taste of research, close to the end of my undergraduate program, that I really found my spark. During my final year thesis at NTU Singapore, I worked on deep learning-based personality prediction [6-9] with Dr. Erik Cambria, and fell in love with the research process. I had always enjoyed the logic of code, but now I could see the adaptability and broad applicability of the tool. I realized that research was my true calling and left the cushy confines of my position as a software developer at Amazon to dive head-first into a life of research. I aim to become a group leader, working alongside passionate researchers leveraging advances in deep learning to uncover fundamental principles that drive learning in the brain.

Enthralled by the intricate workings of neural networks' foundational learning mechanisms, I sought out Dr. Peter Latham at the Gatsby Computational Neuroscience Unit to delve into biologically feasible credit assignment algorithms. During my 18-month tenure at Gatsby, in partnership with Dr. Tim Lillicrap from DeepMind, I embarked on an exploration of perturbation-based algorithms' potential as brain learning candidates. My endeavors culminated in the development of an efficient node perturbation algorithm implementation and a comparative analysis of its learning dynamics against backpropagation during object classification tasks.

In collaboration with Drs. Latham and Lillicrap, we pioneered a novel method that harnessed the autodifferentiating capabilities of contemporary deep learning libraries for large-scale experiments on convolutional architectures with node perturbation—a first in the field. Our efforts also unearthed an intriguing training failure mode, characterized by a sudden and challenging performance drop in the network, which we thoroughly investigated.

Our findings, published in NeurIPS'22 [4,5], revealed that node perturbation may not be swift enough to facilitate effective learning of certain complex goal-oriented tasks mastered by animals. Simultaneously, I contributed to a project on biologically plausible alternatives to convolutional networks, which was also published at NeurIPS'21 [3]. Our research demonstrated that incorporating lateral connections and enabling local plasticity led these networks to deliver nearly convolutional performance while providing a better fit for ventral stream data. This work underscored the significant impact that neural network architecture has on learning algorithms.

Engulfed by the intricate labyrinth of underlying network topologies, my curiosity ignited as I pondered over the possibility of identifying neural architectures optimally tailored for specific tasks. Could we engineer intelligent algorithms capable of evolving their own optimal neural circuits? This question fueled my journey, leading me to join Dr. Frank Hutter's Machine Learning Lab at the University of Freiburg for a 1-year, 3-month tenure. There, I delved into the development of efficient algorithms designed to automate the creation of neural networks, seeking answers to questions that fascinated me.

My time at the university provided me with an invaluable understanding of cutting-edge advancements in deep learning. Leveraging this knowledge, I spearheaded the creation of an open-source library for researchers to innovate novel evolution-based Neural Architecture Search (NAS) algorithms. This initiative not only fostered new discoveries but also facilitated fair benchmarking comparisons among various existing NAS algorithms, culminating in a first-author publication at ICLR'22 [2].

To bridge my extensive background in deep learning with neuroscience research, I spent the past year immersed in Drs. James Fitzgerald and Jan Funke's labs at the HHMI Janelia Research Campus. Now, I am capitalizing on cutting-edge meta-learning advancements to decipher the hidden synaptic plasticity rules within neuronal activity patterns during learning. Utilizing an artificially constructed neural network guided by the Drosophila Mushroom Body connectome, I am developing a model that will be fine-tuned with data from Kenyon Cells, Mushroom Body Output Neurons, and cross-compartment dopamine measurements, as flies undergo first-order conditioning. In collaboration with Drs. Yoshi Aso and Glenn Turner's labs, I am initiating this process with preliminary studies using simulated data, aiming to reconstruct the Oja rule as a proof of concept.

To ensure the robustness of our approach against real-world variations, I have partnered with Dr. Larry Abbott's lab at the Zuckerman Institute as a visiting researcher. Together, we are exploring factors like noise, sparseness of neuronal recordings, length of measured trajectories, number of trainable parameters, weight initialization, regularization, and network size. Our preliminary results on synthetic data have been submitted for publication in Cosyne [1].

In both academic and non-academic settings, I enjoy teaching, mentoring, and collaborating with others. During my undergraduate studies, I was the head Teaching Assistant for the Design and Analysis of Algorithms course where I was responsible for creating and teaching tutorials for a class of 250 students. At the University of Freiburg, I volunteered as a Teaching Assistant for the Deep Learning Lab and Deep Learning courses. Together with Dr. Frank Hutter, I designed and created a new Attention and Transformers course module from scratch. I also co-supervised 2 Msc. thesis and 3 research technicians. Presently, I teach an introductory machine learning class at a local high school close to Janelia, that I helped design. I believe science is best done when researchers teach and learn from each other, building fruitful interdisciplinary insights.

I’m excited by the vast potential of the interdisciplinary field of “NeuroAI”. During my graduate studies, I would like to make strides toward reverse engineering the brain to uncover the fundamental principles that underpin learning and sensorimotor intelligence. By garnering a deeper understanding of biological learning, I believe we can bring machine-learning algorithms closer to those in the brain – leading to better AI systems as well as more powerful theoretical paradigms to understand human cognition. My goal is to work at the interface of academia and industry to construct fruitful collaborations while taking an active role in mentoring the next generation of scientists. I’m confident that the Cognitive Science Programs’s focus on interdisciplinary approaches in concert with my active collaborative style and enthusiasm for research would provide an environment where I would thrive.
