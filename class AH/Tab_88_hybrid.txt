What I Like to Research

In the realm of software and systems analysis, my passions are centered around formal methods, specifically static verification and run-time monitoring. I am particularly captivated by projects that allow me to redesign or scrutinize a software system using a robust and adaptable formal model. Crafting such a model can lead to the validation of powerful system attributes, like being 'correct by construction', significantly enhancing our understanding of its behavior. The creation of a versatile model often necessitates the development of a bespoke language or tool, designed to facilitate comprehensive description and maintain an intuitive interface for ease of use. Tackling such challenges in a project offers me immense satisfaction - it's rewarding to design a system with unwavering guarantees about its behavior, or to develop a language that simplifies the specification of such a system.

As the clock ticks down, I find myself captivated by the intricate world of run-time verification and monitoring in software systems. This realm presents unique challenges; its transient nature limits us to a singular, finite run of the system, making it impossible to monitor all properties with this sole instance. However, crafty manipulations can make certain properties more manageable—by intelligently combining the original property with an astute assumption about the system's structure, we expand our monitoring capabilities.

In quantitative monitoring, statistical techniques such as modeling with random variables and employing confidence bounds prove invaluable. Yet, efficiency is paramount; the run-time verifier must incur minimal overhead to the existing system configuration, disqualifying many exact solutions that are either time- or memory-intensive.

The allure of this field lies not only in its complexity but also in the diverse set of tools and strategies required to design a robust monitoring mechanism. I relish the challenges posed by these problems and look forward to exploring them further during my college years.

My Previous Research Experiences

My first research experience was working with Dr. Hossein Hojjat and his graduate student Amir Hossein Seyhani. In this work, we studied a notion of causality, primarily introduced by Halpern and Pearl, and applied this notion to the context of a concurrent system, modeled by a transition system or event structure. Applying a robust framework of causality to real systems can help in analyzing the root causes of failure in complex settings, and building systems that are more secure and less error-prone. In this project, I implemented event structure composition and translation to a causal model in a prototype tool for defining and verifying causes in event structures. I also translated some of the classic causality problems (priorly studied by Halpern and Pearl), as well as simple computer network configurations, into our desired models. I am currently working on optimizing our cause-verifier module, which checks whether a given cause, accompanied by a given witness, can satisfy the Halpern-Pearl causality criteria. For this problem, I am using ideas from previous publications in this direction (such as search space reduction and special-case solutions).

During my summer at the Institute of Science and Technology Austria, I immersed myself in cutting-edge research on formal methods as part of their prestigious ISTernship program. Working alongside Prof. Thomas Henzinger and two of his brilliant group members, we embarked on a fascinating project to design run-time monitors for verifying fairness properties within complex black-box systems.

In this endeavor, we approached the underlying system as a Markov chain, developing monitors capable of providing a confidence level regarding the satisfaction of a fairness property, even with an error threshold in place. Our innovative approach employed both frequentist and Bayesian statistical methods to achieve our objectives.

To validate our progress, we tackled two well-known issues of fairness—bank lending and college admissions—using equal opportunities and social burden as our respective metrics for fairness. I spearheaded the development of a robust Rust codebase to bring our monitors to life, and I also played a crucial role in translating these classic problems into Markov chains and refining our theoretical framework.

One of my most significant findings was the discovery that Bayesian monitoring becomes computationally efficient and can be simplified from evaluating factorial expressions to updating counters when our fairness expression is in polynomial form. Additionally, I demonstrated that monitoring fixed-size regular expressions could be effectively implemented using our existing monitors. The results of this groundbreaking research have been submitted for publication at TACAS 2023.
