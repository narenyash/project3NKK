I aim to perform fundamental research both in the general machine learning and natural language processing fields. I am inclined to understand how learning takes place, and how to leverage such information to make robust and efficient models in curiosity-driven research. In contrast to the mainstream direction, research work that focuses on scaling down instead of up fascinates me, as it is not only technically demanding but also pushes the field to have an understanding of how to control for a given behavior. My research taste was built mainly due to experiences acquired during my master’s degree at UNICAMP and my time as a Research Scholar at Cohere For AI.

These collaborative ventures have ignited a passion within me for the intricate intersection of data-centric modeling, interpretability, and safety in artificial intelligence research. The forthcoming academic year promises to further fuel my exploration and commitment in this dynamic field.

During my tenure as a Research Scholar at Cohere For AI, I was captivated by the intriguing questions that arose from our Data Pruning project [Marion et al., 2023]. Particularly, I pondered over the role of individual data points in shaping training dynamics and the surprising revelation that models trained on disparate subsets of data could attain strikingly similar downstream performances. Our findings were astonishing; with merely 30% of the initial dataset, our model could match or even surpass the performance of a fully-trained counterpart. This consistency across different datasets is contingent upon an equal number of training steps. This observation suggests that within a given data domain, model efficiency might be more directly linked to the number of gradient update steps rather than the sheer quantity of unique instances in a dataset. This resonates with previous research that hinted at language data points potentially being processed multiple times before learning is affected. This discovery sparked further inquiries: can we quantify the "bottleneck capacity" of a dataset prior to training? How can we intelligently select data for learning, and synthesize reliable data so that smaller models perform comparably to their larger counterparts? Lastly, at the intersection of data efficiency and generalization, how can we leverage a minimal number of data points to learn generalizable features that defy common scaling laws?

In the inaugural year of my master's program, my fascination with generalization was kindled, particularly in the context of reinforcement learning algorithms within Contextual Markov Decision Processes (CMDPs), also known as zero-shot policy transfer. Although I was unable to delve deeper into this subject during that period, the complexities and ambiguities surrounding out-of-distribution generalization and uncertainty estimation continue to captivate me, given their challenging nature in various academic domains beyond reinforcement learning.

The realm of Language Modeling is experiencing a nascent phase where definitions, evaluations, and classifications of types of generalization are gradually emerging. As data sets expand exponentially, I find myself grappling with the elusive essence of generalization within this context. Moreover, I perceive uncertainty estimation as an under-researched area in Natural Language Processing (NLP).

Recent preliminary work in self-improvement and self-correction within NLP indicates a burgeoning interest in these fields, with expectations of increased attention in the coming years. Integrating effective synthetic data could potentially pave the way for high-performing models with reduced reliance on annotated datapoints, which are currently prohibitively expensive to generate. This approach could significantly impact the world, offering a viable and cost-effective solution for advanced machine learning model development.

Interpretability & Safety. Still on language modeling, a question that interests me is in the property of in-context learning. It has been deemed as an “emergent ability” of large language models that enables zero or few-shot transfer. I wonder, though, if that is truly an emergent property or if is it possible for us to have a smaller model with the same properties as a larger one. This relates to the previously mentioned interests of efficiency but is a question of model understanding at its core. I believe this type of research, to investigate unexpected model behavior, to be one of the most exciting. With a few peers from my masters, I worked on understanding how generative methods for image synthesizing behaved when given datasets with varying domain distributions. Would they obey the frequency of each domain in the training data when generating images unconditionally? We found that Diffusion models and certain flavors of GANs do obey the training distribution, but some don’t. This indicates how certain algorithmic choices might lead to these undesired behaviors. Similarly, while in the Scholars Program I discovered unexpected behavior from black-box APIs, backed by ML models [Pozzobon et al., 2023a]. The unexpected behavior in this case was simply the non-reproducibility of research that these APIs cause when changes are not well-informed to users. This is a trivial but highly influential finding as ML algorithms are increasingly coupled to general applications and unexpected behavior leads to systems that eventually fail. I discovered this while working on toxicity mitigation with retrieval-augmented models [Pozzobon et al., 2023b] and both works were accepted to EMNLP 2023. Currently, I’m working on expanding this work to multilingual toxicity mitigation. Harms and bias in a multilingual setting are extremely underexplored and the few works that pursue this path do so only for high-resource languages and classification tasks. I aim for this work to be a starting point to measure harm before deploying multilingual models.
