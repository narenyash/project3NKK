Machine Learning (ML) has made great advances in recent years, but adopting it in real-world applications imposes challenges beyond statistical methods. These challenges call for better system and infrastructure support for real-world performance and usability requirements in every stage of the ML pipeline. For instance, training systems need to consider scalability for large models, as well as heterogeneity for in-house clusters, while serving systems must minimize latency and protect users' privacy. Many challenges need to be addressed in the next few years, and I want to pursue a Ph.D. degree to tackle them.

In particular, I study large-scale Machine Learning in the intersection of ML and distributed systems. My goal is to codesign ML models, algorithms, and systems to support performant models at scale, and develop easily usable frameworks for people, to facilitate ML deployment in the real world. In my pre-doctoral research, I worked on democratizing distributed ML training, privacy-preserving ML serving, and large-scale ML algorithms. My research has been published in ICLR’23(spotlight), NeurIPS’22, CVPR’21, submitted to MLSys’23, and recognized by Amazon Research Awards. In my Ph.D. study, I hope to gain a more holistic view and work on the most impactful problems. Below I will summarize my research development.

Embarking on an exploration of Large-scale Machine Learning algorithms, my undergraduate research journey with Professor Zhuowen Tu unearthed a compelling challenge: the limited versatility of existing generative models, their specialization confining them to a narrow spectrum of tasks within Machine Learning. During our experiments, I discovered that incorporating an instance-level contrastive loss could potentially yield comprehensive performance improvements. Yet, its effective utilization demanded a substantial quantity of negative samples, far exceeding the storage capacity of a single GPU. To surmount this hurdle, I delved into scalability literature and harnessed data-parallel distributed training to expand our model's capabilities. The result was a groundbreaking generative model capable of concurrently synthesizing images at the level of Generative Adversarial Nets (GAN), while also showcasing image reconstruction and representation learnability akin to Variational Auto-Encoder (VAE) standards. This pioneering work was recognized with acceptance at CVPR 2021 (co-first author). Reflecting on this experience, I realized that alongside innovative model designs, robust system support can unlock fundamentally novel avenues for progress.

Democratizing distributed ML training. With the curiosity of exploring ML Systems, I joined Prof. Eric Xing’s group during my master’s study at CMU. Through study of contemporary ML training systems, e.g. DeepSpeed, I found that they required experts to design model-parallel strategies but only provided solutions to a limited industry scenario. In practice, people are system outsiders but are training beyond these setups. To develop a general solution, I proposed a framework that can automatically find good model-parallel strategies, especially for heterogeneous models and clusters.

In my pursuit of automating complex parallelism strategies for machine learning models, I recognized the challenges posed by the vast number of exponential trials needed for a thorough evaluation. To tackle this issue, I embarked on a two-pronged strategy. Initially, I constructed a cost model serving as a swift surrogate for time-consuming real-world trials. I dissecting model parallelism into its constituent parts, meticulously analyzing each element's impact on wall clock execution time. For example, I demonstrated that communication within tensor model parallelism remains asymptotically constant with the number of workers.

The second phase of my approach involved devising a dynamic programming-based optimization procedure to drastically reduce the number of trials from exponential to polynomial. The outcome of this endeavor is AMP (Automatic Model Parallelization), an innovative automatic method applicable across data parallelism, tensor model parallelism, and pipeline model parallelism domains. AMP returns strategies that outperform manual approaches by 1.54x and 1.77x on heterogeneous clusters and models, while matching industrial-standard strategies in performance. This research is under the guidance of esteemed professors Prof. Eric Xing and Prof. Hao Zhang, and has been accepted for presentation at NeurIPS 2022, with me serving as the first author.

When validating the cost model in AMP, I found that communication is a major bottleneck in model parallelism. I further studied communication compression as a potential solution with Prof. Shivaram Venkataraman. In this project, my collaborators and I conducted the first empirical study on model-parallel compression for a wide range of real-world setups, including different families of compression methods, hyper-parameters and hardware. We discovered that only learning-based compressors exhibit acceptable tradeoff between ML performance and the system throughput. Based on the throughput data, I further developed a cost model to help users predict the speed-up and decide whether to apply learning-based compressors for their training needs. This work is submitted to MLSys 2023 (co-first author).

In my quest to develop next-generation machine learning solutions, I found myself particularly drawn to projects that prioritize both performance and user-friendliness – a balance I believe is crucial for widespread adoption. This realization propelled me towards exploring usability within the realm of serving systems, a less-trodden path in my academic journey.

In this pursuit, I encountered an intriguing conundrum: the underutilization of GitHub Copilot, a transformative Transformer-based code generator with vast potential. The issue? Users were hesitant due to concerns over cloud data transmission. Recognizing the importance of privacy in today's digital landscape, my team and I embarked on devising an innovative solution – an inference framework grounded in Secure Multi-party computation (MPC). By employing MPC, we aimed to address privacy concerns and make GitHub Copilot, and similar services, more appealing and accessible to a broader audience.

In my pursuit of privacy-preserving machine learning, I encountered a significant challenge when employing Multi-Party Computation (MPC): real-world applications revealed that intricate functions, such as Softmax in Transformers, necessitate excessive communication within MPC, resulting in painfully slow inference speeds. Moreover, the disparate serving systems adopted by various service providers demanded a universal and efficient solution for multiple MPC systems. To bridge this gap, I devised an approximation abstraction, replacing sluggish functions with their MPC-compatible counterparts. This innovation accelerated Softmax computation by 12.5 times, albeit at the cost of a minor decline in machine learning performance.

To mitigate this trade-off, I proposed a Knowledge Distillation (KD)-driven solution, positioning the original Transformer as the knowledgeable mentor. The culminating framework, MPCFormer, harmoniously designs the ML model, training algorithm, and MPC system, delivering balanced performance in privacy, latency, and machine learning efficiency. Notably, it boasts a 5.93x speedup compared to contemporary BERT models operating within MPC, while maintaining comparable machine learning performance.

This research, under the guidance of Professors Eric Xing and Hao Zhang, was submitted to ICLR 2023 and accepted as a spotlight (top 25% of accepted papers). It also garnered recognition from the Amazon Research Awards, securing $80,000 in funding.
