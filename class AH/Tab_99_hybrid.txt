The natural language processing (NLP) community has recently seen an exponential increase in the size of language models (LMs), already stepping into the trillion-parameter realm (Fedus et al., 2021). Despite their “superhuman” performance on many benchmarks, they are often brittle in domain and compositional generalization. Moreover, the correlation of model size and quality leads to an inflated cost of high-quality models, hurting the inclusiveness in NLP. These issues motivate my two primary research interests: to better analyze and improve the generalizability and interpretability of NLP models, and to increase their efficiency to be more accessible and green. I am fortunate to have conducted some initial research in these areas under the guidance of Prof. Noah Smith and Prof. Fei Xia alongside many wonderful collaborators, and I am passionate to continue studying these problems in my Ph.D.

Generalizability and Interpretability

My training in linguistics has led me to appreciate generalizable and interpretable theories and systems that robustly predict with arbitrary linguistic input consistently with human judgments, and with a traceable reasoning process such that they can be easily amended when the predictions are wrong. Current models, however, usually work best on well-edited Wikipedia text in Standard American English and fail to generalize to new domains, languages, and compositional structures. I grew interested in generalization when I discovered pretrained LMs’ significant degradation in the biomedical domain for the text entailment task (Wu et al., 2019; BioNLP Workshop @ ACL). In response, I proposed unsupervised LM-finetuning using documents in the same domain, which helped bridge the gap. I also intend to contribute to developing representations that can easily adapt to many languages with little supervision. Existing multilingual models show substantially variable adaptability for languages with various amounts of resources (Wu and Dredze, 2020), and they do not generalize well to the world’s thousands of languages unseen during pretraining (Chau et al., 2020). While data availability affects adaptability, studies have also shown that even unsupervised models exhibit biases toward features of English (Dyer et al., 2019). I am curious to explore efficient models and training methods that can robustly adapt to low-resource or even unseen languages by capturing some notion of “linguistic universal.”

The opacity of deep models also obscures a thorough understanding of their true linguistic capability and generalizability. Motivated by this challenge, I proposed to analyze the mainstream coreference model, a complex task that still requires task-specific architectures in addition to pretrained transformers (Wu and Gardner, 2021; CRAC Workshop @ EMNLP). I designed analyses such as oracle experiments to inspect the interaction of model components. They allowed us to identify factors key to a high performance yet often overlooked, such as mention detector precision and anaphoricity decisions. Having seen how model analysis can reveal its shortcomings and lead to possible improvements, I proposed and am leading an ongoing project to study the limits of current LM objectives that underlie prevalent pretrained models. An often-unstated assumption of these objectives is that meaning can be acquired via training on linguistic forms alone, whose possibility has been challenged (Bender and Koller, 2020; Traylor et al., 2021). Nevertheless, Merrill et al. (2021) proved its possibility on strongly transparent formal languages. Curious to see how their theoretical analyses realize empirically, I investigate if existing LM objectives attain this capability. Inspired by my linguistics background, I also study their understanding of “referential opacity,” a closely related natural language phenomenon. Our early results suggest that current LM objectives can only capture the meaning of restricted classes of formal languages but not natural language with its full complexity. I am excited to further probe the limits of LMs and leverage the key language properties revealed in this study to ground LMs to expand their capacity.

In my quest for representations that are both versatile and comprehensible, I am drawn to utilizing linguistic structures as a powerful, inductive bias. My exploration into this area began with Wu et al. (2021b; TACL), where I sought to delve deeper into LMs' understanding of semantics, a realm closer to true language comprehension. Building upon previous studies that uncovered the emergence of syntax in pretrained LMs (Hewitt & Manning, 2019), my curiosity was piqued by the potential for these models to grasp semantic structures more effectively.

To satiate this curiosity, I designed a controlled probe and discovered that LMs struggle to surface predicate-argument semantics compared to syntax. This finding led me to explore the integration of explicit semantic structures, which not only improved their task performance but also demonstrated increased compositional generalizability. In contrast to relying on standalone parsers in this project, I am intrigued by learning with latent structures, a method that eliminates the need for expert annotation, resists cascading errors, and has historically shown superior performance, particularly with previous RNN models (Yogatama et al., 2017; Bisk & Tran, 2018, et al.).

However, this approach is often complicated by the inherent discreteness of structured prediction. To address this challenge, I immersed myself in related research and authored a comprehensive survey on latent structure learning, meticulously categorizing various approaches to surmount this formidable optimization hurdle (Wu, 2021). This newfound knowledge equips me to blend ideas that foster more robust compositional and domain generalization by minimizing reliance on superficial correlations and enhancing interpretability through the provision of inspectable structures.

Efficiency

Amidst the awe-inspiring expanse of Google's halls during my internship, I was taken aback by the extensive utilization and acceptance of TPUs, a revelation that left an indelible impression. When my Facebook Meta colleagues casually suggested that the image captioning system I had meticulously crafted could be effortlessly human-evaluated, it served as a stark reminder of the burgeoning financial strain, inequality, and environmental impact associated with participating in NLP research and leveraging NLP technology.

Recent advancements in Natural Language Processing (NLP) models have exacerbated these issues, with an increasing cost to participate, increased disparity, and a growing carbon footprint. For researchers with limited resources, fine-tuning massive pretrained models is often financially prohibitive. In response to this challenge, prompt tuning - the efficient alternative that trains only a few embeddings - has emerged as a viable solution. Although promising, it still lags behind traditional finetuning in terms of performance. This disparity sparks my determination to spearhead another ongoing project that employs meta-learning to develop a more adaptable model capable of robust generalization across diverse tasks.

The proliferation of massive NLP models has also given rise to exclusive consumer technologies, posing barriers to accessibility. While model shrinking methods exist, the maintenance of multiple shrunk models tailored for varying device capacities incurs significant overhead costs. To optimize resource utilization on edge devices, I proposed dynamic sparse models for Google Assistant, a system where a single trained model can dynamically adjust its sparsity based on available resources (Wu et al., 2021c; ICASSP). My past accomplishments in this field have not only demonstrated their practical utility but also paved the way for a more equitable and sustainable future in NLP technology.

Amidst the cutting-edge landscape of modern machine learning, one stumbling block remains: the asymptotic complexity of models, particularly transformers. These powerful innovations, at the heart of today's state-of-the-art models, demand an exorbitant consumption of both time and memory, making them cumbersome to employ for lengthy sequences such as documents. In a groundbreaking study published in Wu et al. (2021a), I presented a solution that harnessed the power of random feature attention (Peng et al., 2021b). This linear-time and space approximation to the transformer not only expedited document-level machine translation by an impressive 40%, but also bolstered the BLEU score. In another significant breakthrough, detailed in Peng et al. (2021a), we devised a unifying abstraction for efficient transformer variants, likening their attention module to accessing a bounded memory. This abstract framework enabled us to introduce a learnable memory organization, offering a more favorable balance between quality and efficiency, and enhancing interpretability.

My interest in incorporating structural inductive biases into NLP models also faces efficiency challenges. For example, many inference algorithms for structure learning are expensive, such as the cubic-time CKY algorithm, if not intractable. This inefficiency often precludes large-scale pretraining with latent structures. I am excited to address these challenges by seeking methods to efficiently combine recent advances in representation learning with previous structurally-informed models such as (Unsupervised) Recurrent Neural Network Grammars (Dyer et al., 2016; Kim et al., 2019) and Structured Attention Networks (Kim et al., 2017). This direction, along with prompt tuning and many other methods, has the potential to improve generalizability and interpretability efficiently, all of which I would be passionate to pursue in my Ph.D. study, equipped with my past knowledge and experiences.

Nurturing a profound fascination for Natural Language Processing (NLP) research, particularly the pioneering work spearheaded by groups at MIT, I envision this prestigious institution as the optimal crucible for my intellectual growth. My academic curiosity is especially piqued by the groundbreaking investigations of Professors Jacob Andreas and Yoon Kim. The former's exploration into compositionality and language grounding resonates deeply with me, while the latter's work on structured models offers a promising avenue for advancing NLP model development. I am convinced that an intersection of structural approaches could significantly enhance compositional reasoning and linguistic anchoring within these models.

Their collective wisdom and diverse perspectives would enrich my understanding of NLP research.

Ultimately, I aspire for MIT to serve as a launchpad propelling me towards an exciting journey of exploring and contributing to the dynamic world of NLP research.
