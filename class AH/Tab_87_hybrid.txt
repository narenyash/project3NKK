Evolving notions of ML privacy

As ML methods, systems, and problem settings evolve, so should how we define and implement privacy. My first exposure in this direction was through my work on distributed differential privacy at Google, published at ICML’21 [4] and NeurIPS’21 [5] and deployed at scale for applications like Gboard. Distributed DP asks the following: can we achieve central DP-like guarantees under a local DP setup, where n clients each noise their vector locally to minimize their trust in the central aggregator? Prior work bridges the O(√n) local/central utility gap assuming trusted shufflers or execution environments, but it remained open whether we could do so leveraging cryptography, specifically secure aggregation (SecAgg). With my collaborators, I put together the first working system for distributed DP with SecAgg using distributed discrete Gaussians (DDG)—client vectors are appropriately clipped, scaled, rotated/reflected, rounded, locally noised, and securely summed—and I showed that it matches the performance of the central, continuous Gaussian even under low bitwidths [4]. However, discrete Gaussian noise is tricky to sample and not closed under summation, and tuning the discretization factor γ that simultaneously touches on privacy, communication, and utility can be challenging. To address these issues, we also analyzed the Skellam noise as a practical alternative [5]: it is closed under summation, easy to sample, and has an RDP overhead over Gaussians shrinking quickly at 1/poly(variance)—a small compromise from DDG’s 1/exp(variance) in practice—and it thus has a matching performance in production. Moreover, I proposed a simple method leveraging private quantiles of client vector `2-norms to automatically tune γ, substantially simplifying the deployment of our distributed DP algorithms. These experiences meddling with cryptography, compression, empirical heuristics, and engineering issues showed me that there is meaningful headroom for better privacy tools if we see privacy as a multi-faceted problem rather than a standalone statistical discipline.

In the realm of cutting-edge research at Carnegie Mellon University, I delved into the innovative application of Differential Privacy (DP) within cross-silo federated learning. Traditional approaches to differentially private federated learning, including my distributed DP initiatives, have primarily focused on client-level DP. However, in cross-silo learning, there are significantly fewer clients with vast local datasets and disparate privacy requirements. More crucially, the entities requiring protection—institutions—often diverge from those we intend to safeguard—individuals. My groundbreaking paper at NeurIPS'22 [6] introduced a novel relaxation we termed silo-specific sample-level privacy, where each client tailors its privacy protections to the specific samples it contributes to federated learning protocols. I meticulously explored the intriguing implications of this approach, such as the elimination of trust in the server, the abolition of local fine-tuning for effective personalization, and the emergence of a trade-off between privacy heterogeneity costs and the role of personalization in navigating it. I demonstrated that mean-regularized multi-task learning offers a remarkably robust baseline under these conditions, yet also investigated its practical complexities related to hyperparameter tuning. This project underscored my conviction that privacy is intrinsically linked with multiple facets of learning and that effective privacy algorithms should not be developed in isolation.

In the realm of machine learning (ML), the widespread implementation of differential privacy (DP) encounters a significant hurdle: its practicality, primarily due to the glaring privacy-utility tradeoff it presents. Currently, I spearhead a project at Carnegie Mellon University that delves into alleviating the per-example clipping bias in DP training through various generalization techniques. My work has led me to an intriguing discovery: the empirical success of [10] seems to underscore the crucial role of data augmentation in enhancing the performance of DP-SGD, which correlates with smaller augmentation-averaged gradient norms. Drawing parallels with the extensive body of research on sharpness-aware optimization, which proposes (albeit contentiously) that generalization is linked to flatter minima, I am examining whether these generalization methods remain equally efficacious across private and non-private optimization landscapes. A striking observation arising from this exploration is that while data augmentation and gradient norm penalty both diminish the magnitude aspect of clipping bias, only the latter appears to mitigate the directional component, which materializes as a smaller drop in utility due to clipping. Unraveling and counteracting the clipping bias could be a pivotal step toward making DP more pragmatic, and I am eager to delve deeper into this complex issue through both empirical and theoretical analysis.

In the realm of private adaptive optimization, my passion lies particularly with the evolution of DP2 [7], a groundbreaking family of adaptive optimizers that harness delayed preconditioners to revolutionize privacy-utility tradeoffs. The underlying philosophy of DP2 is that in numerous tasks, the loss landscape remains relatively stable throughout training, allowing for the generation of less noisy, albeit slightly outdated, preconditioners through averaging historical private gradients.

DP2 showcases remarkable practicality and efficiency, as it operates independently of public data or second-order computation. In crafting our submission for ICLR'23 and delivering the keynote talk at OPT'22 [7], I was privileged to present empirical evidence demonstrating DP2's exceptional performance in real-world scenarios. This journey led me to a serendipitous discovery of an accompanying interest in the development of efficient private machine learning systems.

To actualize this ambition, I handcrafted our code from scratch, integrating auto-vectorization and Just-In-Time (JIT) compilation techniques, which yielded over 10x speedup compared to our initial prototype. The subsequent acceleration of our research has only fueled my curiosity, prompting me to delve deeper into unexplored territory, such as higher/lower-level efficiency methods, with the goal of further optimizing privacy-preserving optimization algorithms.
