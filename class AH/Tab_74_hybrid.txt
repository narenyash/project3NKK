1 HOLISTIC EVALUATION WITH FINE-GRAINED CRITERIA

Given two different language models, how can we systematically determine which one is better than the other in various scenarios? The ability of language models to generate fluent long-form text makes marginal improvements on classification benchmarks less significant for detailed inspection. On the other hand, evaluating long-form responses poses a unique challenge, as it’s exceptionally difficult to assess the quality of a given text. Conventional methods, such as employing reference-based evaluation metrics (e.g., Rouge, BERTScore), or using language models as judges based on coarse-grained criteria (e.g., Helpfulness, Harmlessness) often fail to capture the depth and granularity that human evaluation offers.

In the realm of appraising lengthy compositions, I contend that the challenge lies in delineating the parameters of an excellent piece. Unlike their human counterparts, artificial intelligence systems struggle to discern subtleties such as creativity, tone, and cultural nuances. However, in a pair of my publications [1, 2], my team and I have meticulously crafted an evaluation framework that not only provides precise criteria for each composition but also utilizes language models as objective judges. This innovative approach has proven instrumental in identifying weaknesses in the language models, particularly when assessing the strategy of a FinTech startup. Rather than merely labeling such responses as innocuous, it is far more insightful to scrutinize overlooked details concerning critical aspects like regulations and compliance.

By focusing on these omitted elements, we can better understand where the language models may falter and devise strategies for improvement. This methodology not only offers valuable insights but also serves as a stepping stone towards developing AI systems that can match human prowess in discerning the intricacies of written works.

However, scaling up fine-grained evaluation poses a significant challenge, as it requires extensive effort to tailor criteria to specific instances. Furthermore, evaluation benchmarks tend to become saturated over time due to contamination issues, necessitating continuous development. One interesting direction I want to explore is creating a benchmark that includes a compositional hierarchy of language model capabilities. The community has already identified several capabilities (e.g., instruction following, chain-of-thought reasoning, tool usage, self-refinement, theory of mind, etc.). We could categorize each evaluation instance, which demands specific abilities, into related sub-tasks with similar criteria. This compositional hierarchy would also streamline community contributions by enabling the addition of new prompts for testing, while still adhering to a unified, fine-grained evaluation framework.

Moreover, it would be an interesting research direction to expand the fine-grained evaluation framework into different modalities. For instance, I am currently exploring how vision-language models could be utilized as judges to assess text outputs given an image input [3]. The approach of employing fine-grained criteria can also be effectively applied to evaluate image and video outputs generated by diffusion models, as well as audio outputs. In general, evaluating AI-generated outputs using interpretable, detailed criteria will be crucial for gaining a sophisticated understanding of the model’s behaviors and its ability to generalize.

2 INDUCING DESIRED CAPABILITIES WITH SYNTHETIC DATA

My fascination lies in harnessing generative models to create exceptional data, thereby encouraging language models to adopt precise generative behaviors. The recent triumph in enhancing synthetic data to enhance instruction-following capabilities in language models presents a tantalizing prospect. This development sparks my curiosity, as it suggests that synthetic data could unlock further potential. I am drawn towards investigating how this data can be strategically employed to address the precise deficiencies of language models, uncovered through thorough evaluation. This parallels human learning from errors, where the language model undergoes continuous improvement by being exposed to targeted data that addresses its specific shortcomings. More specifically, I am intrigued by delving into continual Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF), focusing on the progressive alignment of models with user interactions and utilizing these interactions as a foundation for generating simulations of similar engagements.

One particular area I have explored is the induction of step-by-step reasoning abilities in language models. In my prior publications [4, 5, 6], I have demonstrated that incorporating synthetic rationales and commonsense inferences significantly enhance a model’s reasoning abilities in various settings. However, an notable finding was that despite the trained model excelled at constructing logical flows, it often hallucinated and made simple calculation mistakes. Therefore, as a future direction during my doctoral studies, it would be interesting to identify which capabilities can be acquired through high-quality, machine-generated data and which skills are inherently unattainable, possibly due to constraints such as insufficient parameter size or lack of essential knowledge.

In my ongoing research, I've become captivated by a pivotal question: how can we seamlessly integrate multiple capabilities within a language model without compromising its existing proficiencies? Recently, my colleague and I pioneered a modular approach in our works [7, 8], employing specialized checkpoints for each task and user-defined criteria. By leveraging post-hoc weight merging, this innovative method not only demonstrated impressive performance on novel, unforeseen tasks but also resonated with diverse user preferences. A striking aspect of this system lies in its ability to incorporate new expert models without the peril of catastrophic forgetting, accomplished through maintaining differential ('diff') weights relative to the original model's weights. This finding implies that such a system has the potential to assimilate new experts and adapt to shifting user interests effectively. However, weight merging poses its own hurdles, such as identifying optimal coefficients for each expert and the demand for substantial storage to retain all the weights. Exploring a practical solution for sustaining a suite of desired behaviors using 'diff' weights, like enhancing tool-usage abilities, offers an enticing research prospect.
