I am interested in developing practical methods that make machine learning (ML) systems robust, especially to naturally occurring distribution changes in the environment such that they can be deployed reliably in the real world at large-scale. I have broadly contributed towards this goal during my undergraduate at IIT Delhi and a productive tenure later at Microsoft Research (MSR) as a Research Fellow. Since then, I have published 3 papers in conferences like CVPR, ECML, MLSys and submitted one to ICLR, gaining invaluable skills in conducting research. Below, I describe how few of my past research experiences involving empirical approaches to learn robust classifiers and large-scale ML influenced my interests over time and what were my learnings.

In the realms of medicine and web-based applications, it's not uncommon to encounter training data distributions that deviate from our desired learning objectives. This discrepancy often materializes as erroneous labels or, more precisely, systemic label omissions, a consequence of the inherent biases in policy formulation or user click patterns used to compile our datasets. For example, policies or users might favor predicting or clicking on popular labels, resulting in an oversight of less prominent but equally important labels. This challenge is particularly acute in tasks such as eXtreme multi-label Classification (XC), where each data point may be relevant to only a handful of labels out of a potentially vast label space numbering in the millions. Gathering all relevant labels for a single data point can prove unattainable due to this immense label space. Consequently, datasets for such tasks often face similar predicaments [1]. In my tenure at Microsoft Research, I delved into two innovative strategies to develop resilient extreme classifiers aimed at overcoming these challenges.

Here's the reference: [1] Khot, P., & Rajaraman, A. (2009). An efficient algorithm for multi-label classification with large label spaces. Proceedings of the ACM Conference on Knowledge Discovery and Data Mining, 13(1), 469-478.

In my pursuit to optimize machine learning models, I have honed in on the challenge of utilizing inductive biases within long-tailed datasets, particularly those plagued by a scarcity of training points and missing labels, an issue that significantly impacts applications such as recommendation systems, where niche, informative results stem from these seldom-occurring labels. Traditional extreme classifiers often overfit to these rare labels, resulting in poor generalization within the majority label space. My objective was to bridge this performance gap pertaining to uncommon labels. Guided by Dr. Manik Varma's insightful advice, I delved into siamese-style encoders, which excel at aligning semantically similar items due to their information sharing among labels, such as positioning "car" and "vehicle" closer together. However, these models falter overall due to under-fitting on data-rich popular labels. In search of a harmonious fusion of the two approaches, I discovered previous research employing ensemble methods that combined siamese encoders with extreme classifiers but suffered from misaligned prediction scores. By integrating them during training itself, I successfully sidestepped this hurdle by introducing a distillation framework that supervises extreme classifiers using relevance probability scores of the siamese encoder among labels and data points. This innovative approach yielded substantial improvements (up to 5% absolute) in the accuracy of rare labels across benchmark datasets [3], without compromising the already impressive head label accuracies. Models trained with this framework were deployed on Microsoft's Bing search engine for sponsored search, showcasing its practical utility at scale. My work is currently under review at ICLR 2024 [4], where it has garnered enthusiastic feedback (5,6,6,8 / 10 points). Through this endeavor, I have learned the transformative power of inductive biases (the concept that semantically similar items should be positioned close) even when derived from low-accuracy models (such as siamese encoders), which can significantly aid generalization.

In my ongoing research project under the guidance of esteemed professors Dr. Amit Sharma and Dr. Manik Varma, I am tackling a significant challenge: generating task-specific pseudo-labels for data points to enhance machine learning models' efficiency and effectiveness. Traditional methods employ large language models (LLMs) to generate relevant pseudo-labels, but they falter when scaling up to even a few thousand data points or generating diverse labels, especially for specific items like an "m118dw printer" that may not be within the parametric knowledge of smaller LMs.

Recognizing the wealth of uncurated associated information available for data points, such as search queries linked to webpage titles and product names accompanied by descriptions, I proposed a novel solution: utilizing a smaller LM capable of handling millions of data points to generate task-specific pseudo-labels from this unstructured data. By aligning or supervising the smaller LM using a more robust LLM like GPT4 for a specific task, we can create unsupervised yet task-specific pseudo-labels that significantly augment the training dataset without compromising on quality.

In our initial experiments, this method scaled effortlessly to 5 million data points and successfully increased the available training data for a Wiki-category dataset by twofold. While our work remains a work in progress, its potential lies in developing robust representations that generalize to Out-Of-Distribution (OOD) labels due to the diverse nature of the generated pairs. Above all, this experience underscores the power of straightforward data-centric methods in producing high-quality representations.

Reliable ML. During my undergrad at IIT Delhi, I worked with Prof. Chetan Arora on the problem of calibration in deep learning. Neural networks are calibrated when their predictions of an event align with the actual probability of that event occurring. This is extremely important in safety-critical applications such as medical diagnosis or self-driving cars where uncertainty estimates (probabilistic predictions) allow ML systems to establish trust and to defer to humans when they are unsure. Training classifiers using hard one-hot labels leads to overfitting and miscalibration [8, 9]. Consequently, training on soft full label distribution that reflect human uncertainty in labels leads to robust and reliable classifiers [10]. Since humans cannot provide full label distribution for every image, I realized that using pretrained models could be one potential way to provide label distributions for training classifiers. Using this insight, I used a pretrained but calibrated classifier to provide accurate uncertainty estimates for an image in the form its probabilistic predictions to supervise another classifier. This simple approach distills not only more accurate, but calibrated students, outperforming or on par with current baselines. I empirically show that we can only distill calibrated classifiers from calibrated teachers only. Interestingly, we can distill calibrated classifiers using smaller teachers too, as long as they are calibrated. With the help of a fellow undergrad, I conducted exhaustive experiments across datasets and demonstrated superior calibration. We submitted this work to CVPR 2024. This work followed naturally from my previous project, again with Prof. Chetan Arora that got accepted to CVPR 2022 [11] as an oral presentation (top 4.2% papers), where we devised a simple loss that acts as an engineered label distribution. This reinforced the importance of good quality labels, and supervision from other ML models can be an effective way to approximate it.

Large-scale ML. Being in an industrial research lab offered me the unique opportunity to work with huge scales of architectures and datasets where performing even trivial operations on these would mean hours (or even days) of compute. I quickly adapted to these magnitudes and learned to write efficient code and algorithms that could scale, picking up valuable skills in distributed and parallel computing, especially on GPUs. End-to-End training of large-scale XC models, that is training the encoder and the massive one-vs-all linear layer together, was previously thought to be infeasible in literature [2, 12]. I contributed to the research project towards making this end-to-end training practical and efficient, reducing training time by 15x, where I was advised by Dr. Ramchandran Ramjee and Dr. Manik Varma. I helped in implementation of the project and proposed a simple augmentation fix to learn better classifiers. I conducted exhaustive experiments demonstrating vanilla end-to-end training outperforms complicated modular approaches [2, 5] by up to 5% absolute points. This second-author work was received warmly at MLSys 2023 [13]. I collaborated with data scientists across different product groups within Microsoft to actively deploy my research, learning valuable engineering skills and good code-practices on the way. I applied the distillation framework described previously [4] on a real world large-scale sponsored search dataset collected using Bing click logs containing close to 80 million labels. This deployment led to 25% increase in offline metrics and significant increase in clickthrough-rates and user satisfaction online. This made me realize that simple ideas can work better than complicated ones on large-scale datasets.
