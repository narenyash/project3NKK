Pioneering a Transformative Solution: Unraveling the Annotation Conundrum

In my quest to revolutionize the field of Natural Language Processing (NLP), I am convinced that we can shatter the barriers imposed by the annotation bottleneck, a predicament birthed in low-resource scenarios. By crafting NLP models more adept at knowledge efficiency, we can transcend these limitations. This can be achieved through the strategic application of neural networks with distinct inductive biases and judicious utilization of supplementary supervision data.

My introduction to this bottleneck was during my exploration of annotation transfer for semantic frames across parallel texts. While BERTology's investigation into transformer-based NLP models revealed their remarkable ability to grasp high-level semantics within sentences, my summer research unveiled a shortcoming: these advanced models, despite fine-tuning, struggled to discern cross-linguistic nuances in frame labels when annotated English data was translated into Brazilian Portuguese and German.

Upon investigation, I discovered that the main culprit was the language-specific nature of semantic frames—lexical units and their equivalents across parallel texts do not always evoke identical frames. Furthermore, the dearth of multilingual frame-semantic resources hindered models from learning generalizable semantic differences in frames under low-resource conditions.

This revelation sparked my interest in linguistically-informed NLP models. I proposed harnessing graph neural networks with robust relational inductive biases to construct lexico-semantic representations of frames, capitalizing on the wealth of multi-frame relationships found within FrameNet. My experiments yielded promising results—the approach successfully grouped frames that depicted similar scenarios, even though they were not directly linked in FrameNet. In essence, merging linguistic structure into model structure aided the learning process under resource-scarce conditions.

The primary challenge of low-resource scenarios is that overparameterized neural networks, such as transformer-based language models and graph neural networks, easily overfit on small training datasets. In my paper recently submitted to NAACL 2021, I came up with a form of data augmentation technique that randomly perturbs existing annotation labels to boost sample sizes, and the model learns to reconstruct the original labels through auxiliary learning. This technique is useful for training neural networks under limited resources as it creates synthetic in-domain data. I also found that weak supervision from the auxiliary task facilitates learning cross-linguistic frame-semantic information.

My research experience motivates me to take an interest in making models more knowledge-efficient. I intend to build on initial work on multi-task learning, adding structure and prior domain knowledge to models, and transfer learning. For instance, I am excited about studying how linguistic inductive biases affect multi-task learning so we can create linguistically-informed models that overcome negative transfers that jeopardize model inference in a low-resource setting.

Transforming Language Landscapes: A Top-to-Bottom Journey

The insatiable appetite of deep learning models for data has led me to explore solutions that alleviate the annotation bottleneck, particularly in the realm of linguistic resources. My ardor for this quest originated from my previous research endeavors in augmenting the FrameNet lexicon at the University of California, Berkeley. The Berkeley FrameNet (BFN), the most expansive lexico-semantic dataset grounded in frame semantics theory, still exhibits restricted lexical and frame coverage. In an attempt to automate the acquisition of lexical units, I challenged the prevailing assumption that existing frame labels could encapsulate all words currently missing from FrameNet.

I proposed a novel representation learning method employing an autoencoder to screen out lexical units that did not align well with BFN's framework before incorporating them. This approach not only expanded the breadth of BFN at both the lexical and frame levels but also ensured a higher caliber for the automatically enlarged lexicon. As a result, semantic resources within FrameNet became more valuable for subsequent Natural Language Processing tasks, contributing significantly to the advancement of linguistic research.

My research begs the question of whether we can automatically create new fine-grained task-specific labels without an active learning scheme involving linguists. Label acquisition is powerful because recent work has shown that diverse, fine-grained knowledge about labels helps pre-trained neural networks better adapt to a new domain. Hence, I am interested in learning the representations of the cross-linguistic, semantic relationships between word entities and their labels. The purpose is to augment linguistic resources horizontally (e.g., acquire new lexical items) and vertically (e.g., acquire new labels) to enable NLP systems for low-resource languages.
