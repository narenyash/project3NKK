Research Interests

In the realm of academia, my passion lies with optimizing machine learning for efficiencies that democratize research, ensuring that groundbreaking discoveries are not confined to a select few. The advent of billion-parameter models has proven successful in navigating vast data repositories amassed from the internet's depths. Yet, as we strive to expand these techniques, crucial efficiency matters often fall by the wayside during the training process. Upon deployment, models require compression for optimal performance, tailoring towards individual end-users, and continuous updates to counteract distribution drift - a challenge that plagues many applications. The growing disconnect in open research practices exacerbates this issue, making it increasingly difficult for researchers to access essential artifacts. With this understanding, I am eager to dedicate my Ph.D. studies towards addressing one of the following efficiency aspects:
• Enhancing model and sample efficiencies
• Implementing model compression strategies for inference efficiency
• Developing efficient methods for updating models to accommodate distribution drift and personalization needs.

I am privileged to be a contributor to OLMo, an open-source initiative at the Allen Institute for AI (AI2) aimed at developing a cutting-edge large language model (LLM). My role encompasses participating in architectural discussions, conducting experiments, and crafting the online zero-shot evaluation suite - experiences that have deepened my understanding of model structures, training dynamics, and the nuances of NLP assessment, all crucial components in shaping insightful research inquiries.

Moving forward, my primary focus is on achieving stable and widely adopted low-precision training methods. Preliminary studies [19, 12, 16] have delved into this domain using 8-bit precision training; however, several unanswered questions persist. One such question revolves around the necessity of higher precision representations for components like optimizer states or gradient synchronization, despite evidence suggesting that stochasticity boosts generalization [17]. To address this challenge, I aim to investigate the signal-to-noise ratio in a low-bit gradient synchronization setting and develop strategies to mitigate noise-induced training instability [7].

In the long run, my ambition lies in transitioning from low-bit floating point operations to low-precision training in the integer space. The objective here is to create novel optimizer update rules and gradient propagation methods. Widespread adoption of stable low-precision training techniques would open up avenues for research on currently impractical ideas due to resource constraints, thereby propelling advancements in model efficiency research.

Model compression
After pretraining, large models power applications via APIs and thus need to be deployed on inference servers with throughput and memory considerations. While the commonly available models today are autoregressive and trained on massive amounts of data, the model compression literature primarily focuses on BERT-style encoder models trained on a limited token budget. In Jha et al. [8], we propose one of the first comprehensive studies of model compression for autoregressive models trained on a large pretraining corpus on a compute vs. performance curve, finding new trends for existing compression methods in the setup of modern LLMs. Overall, the broad scope of our work opens up many new short and long-term research questions on compression efficiency, sample efficiency and inference efficiency.
We analyze trade-offs between compression and inference efficiency across different strategies for model pruning. Comparisons against state-of-the-art [18] pruning strategies identify unstructured pruning as sample-efficient and structured pruning as inference-efficient. In addition, many recent results for compression in smaller data regimes, like combining distillation with pruning [10], do not extend to the large data regime and the evaluation setup of modern LLMs, which we define in our work. As a next step, I want to make structured pruning more sample-efficient via influence functions [6] or the Rho-loss [15]. In the long term, I want to understand from a theoretical perspective why learning from data is better than learning from a teacher via distillation when a vast amount of data is available.
We continue to train pruned models for an extended token budget and compare their loss curves to that of a randomly initialized model of the same size. This comparison demonstrates an optima shift for the pruned models, where we initially observe them following a monotonically decreasing training dynamic, diverge for a bit, and then start following the loss curve of the randomly initialized model. I want to study this behavior from the perspective of the lottery ticket hypothesis [4], where we observe a non-winning ticket transition to a winning ticket with extended training. Furthermore, from the lens of compression efficiency, I want to explore mode connectivity [5] to design pruning strategies that lead to a faster optima shift. Finally, the last conclusion from our work is about the prunability of attention outputs that makes us believe we can further speed up flash-attention [3] for inference via sparse-kernels.

In the realm of artificial intelligence, the potential of retrival-augmented models has been underscored by their demonstrated ability to diminish hallucinations [1], elevate performance in the tail end of distributions [11], and operate effectively with protected data [13]. Yet, the applications of these models extend beyond efficient generation, continuous updating, and personalization – areas that remain largely unexplored.

My current endeavors revolve around the development of an innovative retrieval-only causal text generator, building upon the concepts of retrieval-only infilling from NPM [14] and long-phrase retrieval-based generations from COG [9]. My approach involves employing a suffix automaton [2] to enhance contrastive loss, thereby facilitating superior quality long-phrase generation per timestep compared to existing methods.

In the future, I envision delving into research on parallel retrieval from multiple data stores. This innovative direction promises a synergy between language models and classical information retrieval concepts. This framework would foster efficiency through parallelized retrieval, personalization by offering an abstraction to re-rank candidates from various sources, and continuous model updates to mitigate distribution drift via dynamic data store swapping.

Why a PhD?
Before AI2, I worked at PyTorch Lightning, a startup creating open-source libraries to simplify model training. At Lightning, I co-authored TorchMetrics, an API to implement distributed metrics for research reproducibility. Until then, my contributions to open science came from an engineering perspective, making me a confident engineer. But, the experience taught me that research and engineering go hand-in-hand in deep learning. The predoctoral stint at AI2 gave me an excellent platform to develop research ideas and build scientific rigor. Now, I am motivated to get a Ph.D. to develop my research skills further and continue contributing toward open and reproducible science from a research perspective.
Open science is critical for democratic control of the community’s research direction by a diverse group of people to benefit everyone. However, more labs are moving towards proprietary research to benefit individual entities. Working toward efficient machine learning ensures that academia remains at the forefront of research, which is one way to ensure research democratization. A Ph.D. at UW is a step toward my goal to stay in academia and be one of the voices advocating to keep research open and democratic. During my Ph.D., I am interested in working with Noah Smith and Luke Zettlemoyer on model and sample efficiency. I am also interested in working with Hannaneh Hajishirzi on retrieval models and personalization.
After a Ph.D. I plan to become a professor and lead a research group. As a PI, I want to bring diverse perspectives into the group, ask questions, and mentor people according to a style that fits them. I want to establish a culture of scientific rigor and slow science, make claims only with the proper evidence, and not put out minimum publishable units.
