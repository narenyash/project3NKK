Nestled within the heartbeat of a bustling Chinese restaurant, my childhood culinary landscape was an intricate tapestry woven with the rich aromas of spices, the sizzle of oil, and the hum of family camaraderie. Amidst this symphony of flavors, however, there emerged an unlikely adversary: potato skins. As the restaurant's stove cooled each evening, I would find myself by a bucket of water, meticulously stripping each potato of its rough, unyielding exterior with an apple peeler. The peeler, once pristine, gradually transformed into a testament to my daily battle against the bruised epidermal crust of these tubers. Yet beneath this tough facade lay the delightful, creamy-golden treasure that my family and I have found to be enduring. This early exposure to the culinary world instilled in me not only an appreciation for fresh produce but also a determination to conquer even the most challenging kitchen tasks.

In most American families, dessert consists of sweet pastries and chilly confections, but in mine, our dessert tradition revolves around the loaded baked potato. Indeed, on sleepy Monday evenings, we hopped in the family Nissan Pathfinder, winding our way through the hilly middle Tennessee landscape to the nearest O’Charley’s, which had the most delightful baked potatoes.

In the realm of culinary artistry, few dishes boast the versatility and charm of a perfectly baked potato. Each bite becomes a harmonious symphony of flavors, as an array of toppings—from the earthy tang of butter, the crisp freshness of chives, to the unexpected delight of dried pork floss—come together in a dance on my taste buds. Not only does this dish offer infinite possibilities for personalization, but with my generous heap of chives, I can proudly claim that I am not only satisfying my cravings, but also meeting my daily vegetable requirements with gusto.

Gradually, my palate yearned for a more robust taste experience. Perhaps excessively so, teetering on the edge of overwhelming. The additional economic benefit of six more generous dollops of crispy bacon bits was minimal at best. Drenching the humble potato in butter sparked an internal debate: was I truly there for the potato or its sumptuous accompaniment? This age-old adage, 'simplicity is beauty,' seemed to be a distant memory as I found myself oblivious to the fact that all the toppings in the world are incomplete without the core ingredient - the very potato itself.

Nestled within the turbulent years of adolescence, a maelstrom of emotions swirled within me - discontentment, fueled by the relentless barrage of social media comparisons; resentment, stirred by the ceaseless migrations of my youth; and frustration, stoked by the financial constraints that shackled our family's aspirations. This perfect storm threatened to capsize my academic journey, as I found myself drifting aimlessly through days marred by oversleeping and a general lethargy. My grades began to falter, not out of apathy or indifference, but as a symptom of an underlying malaise - a desire to compensate for the lost opportunities of my past.

But in the ugliness and resentment emerged growth. Each potato that I placed against my peeler was pockmarked with gnarly blemishes and mole-like protrusions on the skin, but on the occasion I would stumble across one that had sprouts. These sprouts — or “potato eyes” — fascinated me. From one potato comes another. Those eyes were a reminder that I can eat many baked potatoes in a lifetime, and that I don’t need to top my potato like it’s the last. I’m reminded of the fact that the potato was a New World commodity globalized by the Columbian Exchange. Although the tuber was slow to acclimate to European conditions, potatoes finally found their grip in the cool, continental lowlands. I have lost my way, but I have found it.

Despite its potential decadence, potatoes come from humble beginnings — and I must remember how I sprouted. I was born feet-first, didn’t speak until three, and taught to put the toilet seat down. I still love to sift through worn pages of National Geographics, mesmerized by geometric cuneiform etched in clay and the scale of the Mesoamerican temples. I love where my journey has taken me, from Coney Island to the palmettos of the Carolinas to strawberries of the Middle Tennessee — even the potholes along the way. Chowing on a simple plate of orange chicken and white rice after rush hour at the family restaurant reminded me of what’s important, what’s immutable.

People often joke about those who order the same thing off the menu, or those who choose level one on a five-point scale of spiciness at the local Thai restaurant. Yes, I am that person. But I’ve learned to appreciate the nuance of simplicity. Sweet potatoes are tempting, but that familiar love and warmth will remain in my baked Russet potato.
