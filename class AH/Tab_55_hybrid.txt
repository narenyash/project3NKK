I am eager to unravel how computer science and mathematics can craft a precise lexicon for defining and manipulating these abstractions in the field.

At the University of Washington, I envision a collaborative haven where my academic exploration can address the tangible needs of the open-source community, fostering an environment ripe with interdisciplinary innovation.

In my academic journey, I've honed a unique skillset that intertwines research, community involvement, and addressing tangible user requirements, primarily through my immersion in open-source development, notably with JupyterLab and Vega. My foray into this realm began with a self-driven initiative to bolster the collaborative dynamics within my research group. I collaborated closely with Dominik Moritz and Brian Granger, the creators of the project, to integrate Vega-Lite visualization tools seamlessly into JupyterLab, a feat that has significantly enhanced the data science community's Python interactive visualization capabilities.

This collaboration extended beyond integration, as I developed an open-source tool tailored to optimize interactive visualizations for Python data science users based on their specific database client needs. This innovation bolstered Vega's debugging capacities and significantly contributed to published research on visualization profiling. As a core developer of JupyterLab, I've had the privilege of assisting clients in integrating with the Jupyter ecosystem and shoulder the shared responsibility of maintaining and advancing the project.

A notable achievement was securing a Chan Zuckerberg Initiative grant to ameliorate the collaborative experience within Jupyter, culminating in the incorporation of conflict-free replicated data types into JupyterLab. The process of merging research and user needs within intricate multi-stakeholder projects has been both challenging and rewarding, offering me invaluable experiences that I eagerly bring to a college setting.

My engagement in the open-source Python data science community motivates my research on the interrelated technical and social challenges it faces. The shift toward heterogeneous hardware has increased the complexity of developing numeric libraries and led to fragmentation in the ecosystem, centralizing control in single-stakeholder projects like Jax, PyTorch, and Mojo. The "exilic spaces," as defined by O’Hearn and Grubačić, of decentralized democratic open-source projects based on mutual aid and cooperative relationships, are a powerful living alternative to the market economy. By protecting them through tools that promote a bottom-up ecosystem to meet user needs and facilitate innovation, we can engage in the vital practice of navigating the difficulties of democratic governance. I hope my work can play a role in increasing the diversity and accessibility of the ecosystem by building tools that strengthen its independence.

My programming language exploration journey, aimed at benefiting the PyData community, commenced at Quansight. Collaborating with Travis Oliphant, NumPy's inventor, I embarked on a quest to forge a fresh basis for Python's array ecosystem. Inspired by Lenore Mullin's groundbreaking 1980s work on the 'Mathematics of Arrays' and her application of the lambda calculus to APL, we joined forces to integrate her pioneering concepts into Python. This partnership birthed metadsl, a versatile framework for encoding and optimizing Python expressions.

However, funding this innovative experimental technology proved elusive. While at Quansight, I also contributed to the Data APIs consortium, scrutinizing Python's array API patterns. This experience granted me an unparalleled insight into the challenges of crafting universal interfaces for users. Post-Quansight, I joined Linea, a startup bridging database and programming language theory to enhance Python data science. As one of their pioneering engineers, I spearheaded the development of Linea's abstract analysis engine, translating Python code into a functional dataflow graph to enable program slicing and lineage analysis.

Following Linea, I refocused on creating an abstract interpretation system in Python. Enlightened by the University of Washington's work on e-graphs—an approach rooted in equational reasoning—I crafted a Python library, democratizing research software for a wider audience. This creation was showcased at the E-GRAPHS workshop during PLDI, and I also developed an integrated interactive visualizer to make the project more user-friendly. To foster deeper academic collaborations, I secured an NSF fellowship, paving the way for my pursuit of a Ph.D. in computer science.

My ongoing work with e-graphs in Python gives me a jumping-off point for my overall research goals in pursuing a Ph.D. In egglog, the framework building e-graphs on top of datalog, I am interested in studying the addition of parameterized types and higher-order functions to improve expressivity and the relationship to existing compute engines, such as SQL and differential dataflow. I also want to explore how existing functional abstractions like the Mathematics of Arrays and the RSVDG can be implemented within the e-graph framework to provide a general-purpose compiler framework for numeric computation, AI, and data science. More broadly, I am interested in studying, through the lens of human-computer interaction, how frameworks for type-driven programming can assist in cross-domain and language translation to assist inter-group collaboration between library developers, as well as how concepts from the foundations of mathematics can help give us the language to reason about the semantics of programming languages.

More specifically, at the University of Washington, I am interested in working with Zachary Tatlock, Gilbert Bernstein, Dan Grossman, and Luis Ceze on studying the abstract interpretation and compilation of machine learning code in Python. Moreover, I want to connect the academic programming language community closer with the Python ecosystem, a high-impact field that would benefit from a foundational approach to studying abstraction. By enrolling in your graduate program, I could develop longer-term working relationships with other researchers in an environment supportive of experimentation and thus connect abstract research with the needs of existing open-source communities.
