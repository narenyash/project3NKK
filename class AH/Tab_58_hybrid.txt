In the pulsating digital landscape, my ambition lies in crafting and implementing solutions that revolutionize resource management, scheduling, and scaling within operating systems, while optimizing memory and storage for maximum efficiency. As a diligent Master's student at UC San Diego, I have been indoctrinated in the art of asking incisive questions, an inquisitiveness that has propelled me towards unraveling complex challenges that may have otherwise eluded my attention.

During my first quarter at UC San Diego, I enrolled in Prof. Yuanyuan Zhou’s Graduate Operating Systems course. This was a transformative experience for me as it diverged from the traditional approach of asserting “X is right and Y is wrong” and instead focused on understanding various authors’ rationale behind choosing X over Y. I was intrigued when other authors opted Y rather than X, for a reasoning that best suited their respective systems. I came to realize that computer systems are quite philosophical, where absolute right or wrong is replaced by numerous tradeoffs.

In the realm of groundbreaking research, my formative project was under the guidance of esteemed professor Amy Ousterhout, where our focus converged on augmenting CPU efficiency by offloading datacenter burdens — encryption, compression, and copying — to advanced SmartNIC accelerators. To delineate performance variances, I pinpointed applications that consistently taxed CPUs with these operations and executed comprehensive benchmarking analyses, both with and without offloading the datacenter burdens.

Our journey commenced with the Synthetic Web Service application developed by AIFM (Application-Integrated Far Memory). Given the necessity for single-machine performance testing, I segregated the application's data structures from inherent far memory usage and subsequently transposed the Shenango-based application onto Caladan — a pioneering CPU scheduler that superseded Shenango. Post the migration of compression tasks to a Mellanox ConnectX-5 SmartNIC, we witnessed staggering performance enhancements: a 3.5x spike in throughput and a 20% decrease in latency.

The allure of this work lies in its practicality; in contemporary datacenters, there is an unabating surge in server demands, exacerbated by the waning influence of Moore's Law. By offloading datacenter burdens to hardware accelerators, we can unlock a significant potential for CPU efficiency improvement and mitigate costs associated with scaling computer systems.

My collaboration with Professors Amy Ousterhout, Alex Snoeren, and Anil Yelam, a doctoral candidate at UCSD, culminated in the development of Brim - an innovative far-memory system designed to overcome the limitations and overhead associated with kernel-based paging. As part of this groundbreaking project, we integrated a hint API that allows the pager to anticipate cold memory accesses, while maintaining a familiar page-based memory interface. My primary contributions involved evaluating Brim's performance against memory-intensive applications like DataFrame and AIFM's applications, comparing it with other far-memory systems on the market. Our findings revealed that Brim outperformed Fastswap by up to 150%, a result that is currently under review at a prestigious systems conference. This experience offered me a fascinating glimpse into the world of far-memory systems and illuminated their importance in modern computing. Additionally, I gained an appreciation for the power of collaboration, as I witnessed how individuals from various disciplines could synergize to create an effective solution. The professors' insistence on rigorous result reproduction underscored the significance of accuracy and reliability in scientific research.

I founded SudoLMS to commercialize this project, which garnered over 600 active users at its peak. This was when I first realized how creating novel solutions excites me.
