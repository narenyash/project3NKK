Brown University Computer Science PhD Program: Personal Statement

In the rapidly evolving landscape of artificial intelligence, I have observed a concerning disconnect between our legislative systems and the advanced technologies they are intended to govern. During my undergraduate studies in computer science and mathematics at a prestigious institution, this feedback loop between technology and law became glaringly apparent, offering ripe opportunities for manipulation and subversion. This realization sparked my passion to delve deeper into this intersection, leading me to pursue a master's degree in Technology and Policy at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT).

As I now embark on a PhD journey in Computer Science at Brown University, I am excited to explore how we can harness the power of computation and law to shape the development and design of our technical systems towards equitable and intended outcomes. My ambitions lie in three key areas:

1) Collaborating with legal experts to co-design models that not only support but reinforce robust legal processes and frameworks, ensuring technological advancements align with societal values.
2) Identifying and dissecting failures within computational systems and their development cycles, aiming to correct these weaknesses and minimize potential harm.
3) Advocating for and championing system standards and technology regulation, fostering an environment where ethical considerations are integrated into the very foundation of our technological infrastructure.

Research Motivation. During an engineering internship at Google in 2020, I worked on open-source differential privacy (DP) libraries under Chris Jones, Raine Serrano, and Miraç Basaran. Differential privacy strategically noises data to achieve privacy guarantees while retaining information. For data in histogram publication, however, adding noise is not enough. Consider a health survey where a rare and sensitive response is linked to a single participant. The appearance of that response as a histogram partition, regardless of noise to the partition’s contribution, would leak the presence of the participant in the database. This violates DP guarantees. Thus, δ-thresholding was used throughout Google’s codebase to prevent partition leakage. However, with thresholding, partitions with small contributions that are predetermined and public were also needlessly dropped. I designed and modified the aggregation functions in the codebase so that developers could use the library to specify and keep public, predetermined partitions beforehand. This involved changing the logic of how noise was distributed across partitions in the library and ensuring that updated function changes would not break existing function calls. Through this added functionality, developers could also achieve (ε, 0)-DP, a stronger privacy guarantee.

While developing features for anonymization libraries, I also became curious about the laws and policies that drove this work. The EU GDPR’s carve-outs for anonymized data were enacted in 2020, the very same year that I was hired onto Google’s anonymization team. Moreover, anonymization protections, such as differential privacy, are not typically run to increase privacy protections for already-collected data. Rather, they are viewed as techniques to access new forms of data. Then, the very laws that created incentives for the widespread corporate development of anonymization libraries also provide opportunities for anonymized data to violate, rather than preserve, privacy. This potential misalignment between technical and policy values frustrated me, and I began to consider how intended legal values could be more meaningfully incorporated into technical systems.

Navigating the Intersection of Law and Technology: My Undergraduate Research Journey

In my pursuit of understanding the intricate relationship between legal values and technological systems, I embarked on an undergraduate research project that modeled ethical dilemmas within strategic games. This project sought to address complex moral quandaries faced by autonomous vehicles, a relevant and timely issue in our rapidly advancing world.

I was responsible for defining the system's structure, articulating its purpose, and designing the sacrifice function—a crucial component that enabled the logical system to simulate various ethical and policy frameworks. These efforts culminated in a co-authored publication at AAAI 2021 titled "Ethical Dilemmas in Strategic Games."

While our model successfully captured significant aspects of this multifaceted issue, it necessarily omitted others due to its inherent limitations. For instance, it forced a binary decision-making process where multiple actions could potentially coexist. Additionally, we oversimplified the incorporation of legal and ethical frameworks by assigning values to each action and arranging them in order—a necessary simplification to render the system computable.

Despite these simplifications, law places great emphasis on contextual considerations, aspects that our technical approach may have overlooked. This realization fueled my curiosity about the broader realm of AI and technology's relevance and significance within the legal sphere. I am eager to explore how these fields intersect, collaborate, and ultimately shape each other in the years to come.

Exploring the Complexities of Legal Conflicts in the Emergence of Artificial Intelligence Systems: A Research Journey with Senior Research Scientist Alice Xiang at Sony AI.

In an effort to delve deeper into the technical nuances critical for legal evaluations, I embarked on a research project investigating technology case law. My exploration led me to a striking observation: almost all U.S. litigation concerning facial processing technologies (FPT) was centered around a specific Illinois state law—the 2008 Biometric Information Privacy Act (BIPA). This revelation prompted me to delve into the intricacies of BIPA's legal properties and the technical characteristics of FPT that fueled an unprecedented surge in litigation.

In some instances, the implementation of BIPA on FPT underscored the shortcomings of technological means in upholding privacy standards. Yet, the policy often fell short in grasping the core of technical methodologies as well. For example, irrespective of application, BIPA safeguards the acquisition of face geometry scans but omits facial photographs from privacy protections. This oversight could potentially discourage the adoption of privacy-preserving face blurring techniques in image datasets that collect facial landmarks for identifying and obscuring faces.

These discrepancies are among the many tensions discussed in my first-author paper, "Regulating Facial Processing Technologies: Tensions Between Legal and Technical Considerations in the Application of Illinois BIPA," published in the proceedings of ACM FAccT 2022. Post publication, the American Civil Liberties Union leveraged the policy to sue Clearview AI, while Meta faced BIPA lawsuits concerning its augmented reality filters. These legal actions resonated with several points raised in my paper, and I felt encouraged by BIPA's role in curbing the proliferation of surveillant and discriminatory FPT deployment.

Simultaneously, this research project revealed the policy's technical misconceptions, fueling a newfound motivation within me to explore how written regulations could be refined through a more comprehensive integration of technological counsel.

Motivating and Proposing Technology Regulation and System Standards. I began conducting research on regulatory design for AI systems in the first year of my master’s program advised by Professor Dylan Hadfield-Menell at MIT CSAIL. The AI research community recognizes the importance of transparency regarding potential harms caused by AI systems. However, creating legal and organizational incentives to prioritize investigating these harms remains an open and urgent problem. I discovered the potential of regulatory penalty defaults to address this problem as I synthesized several bodies of literature: AI-related policies in the United States, the legal literature on regulatory design, and domain-specific properties of recommender systems and autonomous vehicles. My main contributions were in the proposal of regulatory penalty default approaches for AI systems. I am the lead author on the corresponding AAAI/ACM AIES 2022 publication A Penalty Default Approach to Preemptive Harm Disclosure and Mitigation for AI Systems. The domain-specific, top-down approach I took to this policy problem led me to complementarily explore bottom-up approaches such as developing technical standards. I am currently conducting this work through a part-time research internship at the Center for Trustworthy and Responsible AI within the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), where I am developing and comparing measurable standards for the AI Risk Management Framework.
