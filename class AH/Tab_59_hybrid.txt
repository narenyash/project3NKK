My research interests are at the intersection of data privacy and machine learning (ML), focusing on federated learning (FL) and differential privacy (DP). I’ve always wanted to study this area with greater depth and pursue a career as a professor, and I’ve been working hard towards this career, via research, teaching, and service to the academic community. I’m applying for the EECS PhD program at MIT as it presents an unparalleled opportunity to collaborate with experts, further my research, and lay the groundwork for a fulfilling career in academia.

In the sweltering summer of 2021, my voyage into the dynamic realm of data privacy and machine learning commenced at the Institute for Mathematical Science (IMS). Here, I spearheaded a research initiative as part of an esteemed team, working closely with Google as our sponsor. Our ambitious goal was to construct a vertical federated learning (FL) system tailored specifically for dual-party financial settings. This cutting-edge system aimed to facilitate collaborative model training among organizations such as banks and fintech companies, while preserving the privacy of their shared user base.

To ensure unwavering data security, I implemented homomorphic encryption within our FL system, a technique that encrypted all exchanged information and allowed calculations to be performed on ciphertext. This innovative approach not only solidified my passion for federated learning but also served as the catalyst for further exploration in this burgeoning field under the guidance of Prof Xiaokui Xiao during my junior year.

In pursuit of uncovering the intricacies of split learning (SL), an emerging alternative to FL, I embarked on a groundbreaking project as a research assistant. My work critically analyzed existing attacks on SL, exposing their reliance on impractical assumptions and weaknesses in less complex models. By developing realistic attacks, I was able to achieve significantly improved results, offering fresh insights into the privacy concerns surrounding this promising technology.

My dedication to this project was rewarded with the Outstanding Computing Project Prize from the NUS School of Computing. Furthermore, my findings have been transformed into a first-authored paper currently under review for WWW '24, garnering generally positive feedback and high marks from esteemed reviewers.

Research in differential privacy. After my junior year, I found my research interests leaning towards theoretical problems and in particular, DP as a rigorous framework to quantify information leakage. Supervised by Professors Xiaokui Xiao and Vincent Tan, I completed my honors thesis on graph neural networks (GNNs) with link local privacy. We aimed to safeguard privacy in GNN training via local DP, where each node/user perturbs its data with noise before transmission to the server, ensuring that the server receives only differentially private information. To address the inherent privacy-utility trade-off, I innovatively proposed to use Bayesian estimation to estimate the graph topology, striking a privacy-utility balance with theoretical guarantees. This work has a broader potential impact, including privacy-preserving contact tracing systems. An abstract of this work was published and presented in SIGMOD '23, where I was awarded first-place in the SIGMOD Student Research Competition (SRC), and qualified to compete in the ACM SRC grand finals. Additionally, a full research paper first authored by me was published and presented in CCS '23. This work has also earned me the University-level Outstanding Undergraduate Researcher Prize, recognizing exceptional undergraduate research contributions.

In the meantime, I’m curious about the limitations of DP as a notion to quantify information leakage on individual data points. Driven by such curiosity, we studied the data distribution leakage in differentially private FL. We found that, despite DP guarantees, it's still possible to infer the data distribution of individual organizations, a concern in scenarios where such information could divulge sensitive business insights. This exploration opens new avenues in data privacy and underscores the need for stronger protection. The paper, co-authored by me, is currently undergoing revision at TKDE.

In the culmination of my academic journey at the National University of Singapore, I emerged with a dual honor's degree, achieving the highest distinction in Computer Science and Mathematics. Transitioning seamlessly, I immersed myself full-time as a Research Assistant, where my initial focus on Functional Languages (FL) and Distributed Programming (DP) evolved to encompass Machine Learning (ML) theory. Presently, my research is centered around kernelized bandits, with the ambition of crafting innovative Gaussian process optimization algorithms in a federated environment. The ultimate objective is to theoretically constrain both the accumulated regret and communication expense, pushing the boundaries of our understanding in this fascinating field.

As a budding researcher, my aspirations lie in the realm of advanced study as a graduate student, where I aim to pioneer innovative advancements in privacy-preserving machine learning. Specifically, I am captivated by the groundbreaking work of Professor Srini Devadas, particularly his recent contributions: the enhancement of DP-SGD through novel optimizer designs that mitigate clipping bias (S&P 2023), the addition of anisotropic noise across principal subspaces (CCS 2023), and the exploration of PAC privacy (CRYPTO 2023), a groundbreaking concept that enables automatic privacy measurement and control.

I have long held the conviction that current privacy protocols, such as DP, can be overly pessimistic, focusing on worst-case scenarios that may not be statistically common. PAC privacy represents a significant leap forward by considering the prior distribution that generates data points, offering a more nuanced and effective approach to data privacy. Although my academic background is grounded in algorithmic (differential) privacy, I am eager to delve into the cryptographic aspects of privacy as well, an area where Professor Devadas has demonstrated exceptional expertise through his work on homomorphic encryption, multi-party computation, private information retrieval, and beyond.

In discussions with Professor Devadas and interactions with his students, Simon and Kevin, at CCS, I have been captivated by the group's vibrant research atmosphere. My academic passion extends to the exploration of fundamental privacy-utility trade-offs in machine learning, a field where Professor Martin Wainwright's work on information-theoretical bounds of local DP and his recent contributions on federated optimization (NeurIPS 2020) and adversarial attacks (S&P 2020) have left an indelible impression.

Finally, I recognize the importance of data privacy in the context of database systems, a field where Professor Samuel Madden's recent project, ATLANTIC (VLDB 2021), on building efficient and accurate database systems with differential privacy guarantees, has piqued my interest.
