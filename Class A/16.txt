
When I was younger, I thought being intelligent meant knowing the answer.

If someone asked a question in class, I admired the student who raised their hand immediately.

If I solved a difficult problem before everyone else, I felt successful.

If I knew something that another person did not, I felt confident.

Over time, however, my understanding of intelligence changed.

I began to realize that some of the most important questions do not have immediate answers.

Some do not even have known answers.

And sometimes, asking the right question is more valuable than knowing a quick answer.

This realization has shaped the way I learn.

My interest in computer science began with questions.

How does a computer know what to do?

How can information travel across a network?

How can a machine recognize an image?

How can software understand language?

How can a system learn from examples?

At first, I searched for answers.

But every answer created another question.

That pattern became addictive.

I learned how a program communicates with a database, then wondered how databases handle enormous amounts of information.

I learned how machine learning models classify images, then wondered how they behave when the image looks different from anything in the training data.

I learned how language models generate text, then wondered what it actually means for a machine to "understand" language.

The questions became more interesting than the original answers.

That changed my relationship with learning.

Instead of seeing knowledge as a destination, I began seeing it as a map.

Every new concept revealed another part of the map.

The more I learned, the larger the unknown became.

At first, this could feel discouraging.

If learning more meant realizing how much I did not know, perhaps I was not making progress.

Eventually, I understood the opposite was true.

Recognizing what I do not know is evidence that my understanding is becoming more sophisticated.

A beginner may not know that a problem is complicated.

An experienced person can see the complexity.

That difference matters.

I experienced it most strongly when I began learning artificial intelligence.

At first, AI seemed straightforward.

Give a model data.

Train it.

Evaluate it.

Improve it.

But the deeper I went, the more questions appeared.

What exactly is the model learning?

Why does it make one prediction instead of another?

What happens when the training data is biased?

How does the model behave outside its training distribution?

Can a model produce a correct answer without understanding the underlying concept?

How should uncertainty be represented?

These questions made AI more interesting to me than simply achieving a high benchmark score.

I wanted to understand the system rather than merely operate it.

That curiosity influenced the way I approached my projects.

When something worked, I began asking why.

When something failed, I asked why.

Both questions were equally valuable.

Success can hide assumptions.

Failure can expose them.

Suppose a machine learning model performs extremely well.

The obvious reaction is to celebrate the accuracy.

My reaction increasingly became:

"What caused this performance?"

Maybe the dataset is genuinely representative.

Maybe the model learned a useful pattern.

Or maybe the dataset contains an unintended shortcut.

The number alone cannot tell me.

I need to investigate.

That habit of questioning results has become an important part of how I work.

I have learned not to trust a result simply because it looks good.

I want to understand the conditions under which it was produced.

This applies to software as well.

A program that works once is not necessarily reliable.

An application that looks good on my computer may behave differently on another device.

An API that succeeds with one input may fail with another.

A system that works for a few users may behave differently at scale.

Each successful test therefore creates another question:

"Under what conditions does this continue to work?"

That question is one of the foundations of good engineering.

It is also one of the reasons I enjoy testing.

Testing is a structured way of asking questions.

What happens if the input is empty?

What happens if the input is much larger?

What happens if the network fails?

What happens if the user does something unexpected?

What happens if a dependency becomes unavailable?

Instead of assuming the system will behave correctly, testing asks it to prove itself.

I find that mindset valuable.

It turns uncertainty into something that can be investigated.

This way of thinking has also changed how I approach academic subjects.

When I encounter a difficult mathematical concept, I no longer immediately search for a shortcut.

I try to understand what the concept is describing.

What problem was it created to solve?

Why does the formula have this structure?

What happens if one assumption changes?

Can I construct a simple example?

Can I explain the idea without using the formal notation?

These questions often help me understand the subject more deeply than memorization would.

The same approach works in computer science.

If I learn an algorithm, I want to know why it is useful.

If I learn a data structure, I want to know what trade-off it provides.

If I learn an optimization technique, I want to understand what problem it is solving.

If I learn a machine learning architecture, I want to know why it was designed that way.

The questions give the knowledge context.

Without context, information can become a collection of disconnected facts.

With context, it becomes a system.

This is one reason I am attracted to research.

Research is essentially structured uncertainty.

You begin with something you do not fully understand.

You examine what is already known.

You identify a gap.

You formulate a question.

You design an experiment or analysis.

Then you discover what happens.

The result may support your hypothesis.

It may contradict it.

It may reveal an entirely new problem.

I like that uncertainty.

In a normal assignment, the teacher knows the answer.

In research, nobody may know.

That changes the role of the student.

You are no longer simply demonstrating that you understand existing knowledge.

You are participating in the process of creating new knowledge.

That is something I want to experience.

I want to work on questions where the answer is not available in a textbook.

I want to read research papers and ask what remains unresolved.

I want to build experiments that can distinguish between competing explanations.

I want to become comfortable with results that do not match my expectations.

This last part is particularly important.

It is easy to become attached to an idea.

When I have spent time developing a project, I naturally want the result to support my original assumption.

But science and engineering require something more important than attachment.

They require honesty.

If the evidence contradicts my idea, I need to change the idea.

That can be difficult.

It can also be liberating.

I do not have to protect an assumption simply because it was mine.

I can replace it with a better one.

That is one of the reasons I value feedback.

A good criticism can save weeks of work.

Someone may notice an assumption I never questioned.

Someone may identify a simpler approach.

Someone may show that my proposed solution does not actually address the problem.

My first reaction may be disappointment.

My second reaction should be curiosity.

Why did they see something I did not?

What can I learn from their perspective?

This mindset has improved the way I collaborate.

Instead of treating disagreement as a threat, I try to treat it as information.

Different people have different mental models.

When those models conflict, the disagreement can reveal something worth examining.

This is particularly useful in technical teams.

One person may focus on performance.

Another may focus on usability.

Another may focus on security.

Another may focus on reliability.

A solution that satisfies only one of these perspectives may be incomplete.

The strongest designs often emerge from asking questions across all of them.

My interest in questions has also influenced how I think about artificial intelligence.

Modern AI systems can answer an enormous range of questions.

That capability is impressive.

But it also creates an interesting paradox.

If machines become increasingly good at answering questions, humans may need to become better at deciding which questions are worth asking.

An AI system can generate ten possible solutions.

A human still needs to decide which problem actually matters.

A model can summarize thousands of documents.

A researcher still needs to determine what conclusion is justified.

A system can produce code.

An engineer still needs to decide whether the code should exist.

As AI becomes better at producing answers, human judgment may become more important, not less.

This is one of the areas I want to explore.

I am interested in building AI systems that support human reasoning rather than simply replacing it.

A useful AI assistant should not always answer immediately.

Sometimes it should ask for clarification.

Sometimes it should identify an assumption.

Sometimes it should show competing possibilities.

Sometimes it should explain why it is uncertain.

That kind of system would help people think rather than simply consume outputs.

I find that possibility exciting.

It also connects to education.

A student who receives every answer instantly may finish assignments faster without necessarily learning more.

An AI tutor that asks the right question at the right time could be more valuable.

Instead of giving the solution, it might provide a hint.

Instead of correcting an error immediately, it might ask the student to examine a particular step.

Instead of explaining everything, it might determine what the student already understands.

This approach reflects something I have learned from my own experiences.

Sometimes the most useful help is not an answer.

It is a better question.

If someone tells me exactly what to do, I may complete the task.

If someone asks me the right question, I may understand the problem.

The second experience stays with me longer.

That is why I value teachers and mentors who do not always solve problems for me.

They force me to think.

They allow me to struggle.

They ask questions that expose gaps in my understanding.

That process can be uncomfortable.

But it creates independence.

I want to become that kind of learner.

Someone who does not need an answer immediately.

Someone who can remain with a difficult problem long enough to understand it.

This is particularly important because many of the problems I want to work on do not have simple solutions.

Artificial intelligence raises technical and ethical questions.

Climate change involves scientific, economic, and social systems.

Education involves technology, psychology, culture, and access.

Large-scale software involves reliability, security, performance, and human behavior.

These problems cannot be solved by one formula.

They require questions.

What is actually happening?

What assumptions are we making?

What information are we missing?

Who is affected?

What alternatives exist?

How can we test them?

These questions create a path through complexity.

That is the kind of thinking I want to develop in college.

I want to study computer science deeply.

I want to learn algorithms, artificial intelligence, machine learning, systems, and mathematics.

But I also want to develop the ability to formulate research questions.

I want to understand how scientists identify gaps in knowledge.

I want to learn how engineers translate vague problems into measurable objectives.

I want to explore how interdisciplinary teams approach questions from different perspectives.

Most importantly, I want to remain curious.

Curiosity is easy to lose when education becomes focused entirely on answers.

A student learns what will appear on the exam.

A professional learns what is necessary for the current task.

Eventually, it can become easy to stop asking why.

I do not want that to happen to me.

I want to preserve the habit that started my journey in technology:

"What happens if I try this?"

That question has led me to programming.

It has led me to artificial intelligence.

It has led me to projects.

It has led me to research ideas.

And it continues to lead me toward subjects I have not yet explored.

The future will bring questions I cannot currently predict.

That is exciting.

I do not need to know every answer today.

I need to become capable of investigating tomorrow's questions.

That requires technical knowledge.

It requires patience.

It requires critical thinking.

It requires humility.

And it requires the willingness to admit:

"I don't know yet."

Those two words are not a weakness.

They are an invitation.

They mean there is something left to discover.

I want my college education to give me the tools to accept that invitation.

I want to encounter problems that initially seem too difficult.

I want to ask questions that lead to more questions.

I want to work with people who challenge my assumptions.

I want to conduct experiments whose results surprise me.

I want to build systems that make me reconsider what I thought I understood.

And I want to leave college with more questions than I entered with—not because I failed to learn the answers, but because I learned enough to recognize deeper problems.

Perhaps that is the clearest way I can describe what I want from education.

I do not want to become a person who knows everything.

I want to become a person who knows how to keep learning.

The answers I know today will eventually become foundations for new questions.

The technologies I use today will eventually be replaced.

The projects I build today will eventually become stepping stones.

But the habit of questioning can remain.

That habit is what brought me here.

It is what keeps me learning.

And it is what I hope will guide me wherever I go next.

I used to think intelligence meant having the answer.

Now I think intelligence begins with knowing which question to ask.
