
I am a student who believes that mistakes and failures are a normal part of learning. During my academic journey and while working on technical projects, I have faced situations where my first approach did not work as expected. These experiences have taught me to be more patient, analyse problems carefully, and use mistakes as opportunities to improve.

When I started learning programming, I initially expected that writing code would be a straightforward process. I thought that if I understood the syntax and followed the examples correctly, the program would work. However, as I started building my own projects, I realised that errors are a regular part of software development.

Sometimes a program does not run because of a small syntax error.

Sometimes the code runs but produces the wrong result.

Sometimes the problem is not in the code itself but in the way different components interact.

These situations initially frustrated me, but over time I learned to approach them differently.

Instead of immediately trying random solutions, I started looking at the error carefully and trying to understand what was actually happening.

This changed my approach to debugging.

I learned that solving a technical problem requires identifying its cause before deciding on a solution.

For example, if an application is not displaying the expected information, the problem could be in the frontend, backend, API, database, or even the data itself. Simply changing the user interface may not solve the actual issue.

I therefore started breaking problems into smaller parts.

I check whether the input is correct.

I check whether the backend receives the request.

I check whether the database returns the expected information.

I check whether the response is being sent correctly.

I then check whether the frontend is handling the response properly.

This process has made debugging more systematic.

My experience with artificial intelligence projects has taught me a similar lesson.

Machine learning models do not always produce the expected results.

A model may have good performance during training but perform poorly on new data. Sometimes the dataset may not be large enough. Sometimes the data may contain inconsistencies. Sometimes the model may not be suitable for the problem.

Initially, I thought that improving an AI model mainly meant changing the model architecture.

Later, I understood that there are many other factors involved.

The quality of the data is important.

The preprocessing method is important.

The training process is important.

The evaluation method is important.

The choice of metrics is important.

This helped me understand that failure in a machine learning project can provide useful information.

If a model performs poorly, the result can help identify where the problem is.

Perhaps the data needs to be improved.

Perhaps the model is overfitting.

Perhaps the problem needs to be defined differently.

Perhaps more testing is required.

Instead of treating the poor result as the end of the project, I try to understand what it is telling me.

This approach has also influenced my academic learning.

There are subjects and concepts that I do not understand immediately.

When this happens, I try not to assume that I cannot learn the subject.

I first identify what I am missing.

Sometimes I need to review an earlier concept.

Sometimes I need to solve more examples.

Sometimes I need to find another explanation.

Sometimes I need to ask someone for help.

This has helped me become more comfortable with difficult topics.

I have realised that understanding a subject can take time.

Learning is not always a straight process.

There may be periods where progress feels slow.

However, consistent practice can gradually improve understanding.

I also believe that mistakes can reveal weaknesses that would otherwise remain unnoticed.

For example, if I repeatedly make mistakes in a particular programming concept, it indicates that I need to spend more time understanding that concept.

Similarly, if I struggle with a particular type of problem, I can use that experience to decide what I should practise.

This makes mistakes useful.

I have also learned that it is important to distinguish between making a mistake and repeating the same mistake without learning from it.

Making a mistake is normal.

Ignoring the reason for the mistake is less useful.

I try to understand what caused the problem and what I can do differently next time.

This approach has helped me improve gradually.

Another important lesson I have learned is that asking for feedback can help identify mistakes earlier.

When working on a project, I may become familiar with my own approach and fail to notice a problem.

A teammate or another student may look at the same project differently and identify something I missed.

I have learned to value such feedback.

Feedback does not always mean that the original idea was bad.

It can simply show that there is another way to approach the problem.

I try to consider feedback carefully and use it when it improves the project.

This is particularly important when working in teams.

A team project can involve disagreements about design, technology, features, or implementation.

Sometimes one approach may fail after implementation.

Instead of blaming the person who suggested it, the team can focus on understanding why it did not work and what should be done next.

I believe this creates a better learning environment.

My participation in hackathons has given me several opportunities to experience failure under time constraints.

During a hackathon, there may not be enough time to completely redesign a project after discovering a problem.

The team needs to decide whether to fix the existing approach, simplify the feature, or change the plan.

This teaches practical decision-making.

Sometimes the best solution is not to continue improving a difficult feature.

It may be better to remove it and focus on the main functionality.

I have learned that changing direction is sometimes necessary.

This does not mean giving up.

It means using the available time and resources more effectively.

Another lesson from technical competitions is that not every idea will work.

An idea may sound useful when discussed but become difficult when implemented.

There may be technical limitations.

The required data may not be available.

The solution may take too much time.

Users may not find the feature useful.

These situations have taught me to evaluate ideas more realistically.

I now try to think about feasibility before investing too much time in a particular approach.

I also believe that failure can improve creativity.

When the first solution does not work, I have to think about alternatives.

This may lead to a simpler or more effective approach.

For example, if a particular model is too complex to run efficiently, I may need to explore a smaller model or a different method.

If an application has too many features, I may need to simplify the interface.

The limitation can therefore encourage a different solution.

This is one reason I do not want to avoid difficult projects.

Difficult projects often teach more than projects that work immediately.
