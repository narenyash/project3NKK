
Mistakes are often treated as things that should be avoided. In academic environments, they may appear as wrong answers, low marks, failed experiments, incomplete assignments, or incorrect decisions. Naturally, students want to avoid them. However, as I have progressed through my education, I have realised that mistakes can provide something that success sometimes cannot: a clear view of what I do not yet understand.

This has changed the way I think about learning.

When I was younger, I often considered a correct answer to be the end of a problem. If I reached the expected result, I moved on. A wrong answer, on the other hand, felt like evidence that I had not understood the subject.

Over time, I began to see that the situation is more complicated.

A correct answer can sometimes come from memorisation.

A wrong answer can reveal a misunderstanding.

A failed experiment can reveal an incorrect assumption.

A programming error can expose a gap in reasoning.

A disappointing result can show that a particular method is ineffective.

In this way, mistakes can become a form of feedback.

My experience with programming made this especially clear.

When I first started writing code, errors were frustrating. A program that looked correct could refuse to run because of a small mistake. Sometimes the error message seemed unrelated to what I believed I had done wrong.

My first instinct was often to fix the visible problem as quickly as possible.

Later, I became more interested in understanding the cause.

Why did the error occur?

What assumption had I made?

What did the computer interpret differently from what I intended?

What would happen if the input changed?

These questions transformed debugging.

An error was no longer simply something standing between me and a working program.

It became information about my understanding.

This way of thinking has influenced how I learn computer science.

Programming requires precision, but it also requires experimentation.

A programmer must try approaches that may not work.

They must test assumptions.

They must examine unexpected results.

They must modify their solutions.

The process is inherently iterative.

A first attempt is rarely the final one.

This is very different from the idea that good students should always know the correct answer immediately.

In engineering, uncertainty is normal.

The goal is not to eliminate every mistake before beginning.

The goal is to identify mistakes early and learn from them.

This has become one of the principles I want to carry into my academic life.

Mistakes also reveal how a person thinks.

Suppose two students arrive at the same wrong answer.

Their mistakes may have completely different causes.

One may have misunderstood the question.

Another may know the concept but make a calculation error.

Another may use an incorrect assumption.

The final answer alone does not explain the problem.

The reasoning behind it does.

This is why I have become more interested in understanding the process rather than simply checking the result.

When I make a mistake, I want to know where my reasoning changed direction.

That point often contains the most useful lesson.

This approach is especially valuable in mathematics.

A mathematical error is not always simply a matter of calculation.

It may reveal that a concept has not been fully understood.

If I only memorise the correct formula, I may repeat the same mistake later.

If I understand why the formula works and where it applies, I can recognise when my reasoning goes wrong.

Therefore, mistakes can push learning from memorisation toward understanding.

This is one reason I want a strong mathematical foundation for artificial intelligence.

Machine learning involves mathematics, statistics, probability, and optimisation.

A small misunderstanding can affect the interpretation of an entire model.

I want to understand these foundations deeply enough to recognise and analyse mistakes rather than simply rely on libraries.

The same principle applies to machine learning experiments.

A model may perform poorly.

At first, this may appear to be a technical failure.

But poor performance can have many causes.

Perhaps the data is insufficient.

Perhaps the data contains noise.

Perhaps the training process is inappropriate.

Perhaps the model is too simple.

Perhaps the model is too complex.

Perhaps the evaluation method does not reflect the actual objective.

The poor result therefore creates a question.

Why?

Investigating that question can teach more than achieving a good score immediately.

This is one reason I find research interesting.

Research involves uncertainty.

Researchers often begin with an idea about what might happen.

They then test it.

The results may support the hypothesis.

They may contradict it.

They may produce something unexpected.

A result that contradicts the hypothesis is not automatically useless.

It may reveal something important.

It may suggest a new explanation.

It may lead to a different experiment.

It may show that the original question needs to be reconsidered.

This makes mistakes and unexpected results part of scientific progress.

I want to develop this mindset.

However, I do not believe mistakes should simply be accepted without responsibility.

There is an important difference between learning from mistakes and becoming careless about them.

An engineer should test carefully.

A researcher should follow appropriate methods.

A student should prepare.

A professional should take commitments seriously.

The goal is not to make mistakes deliberately.

The goal is to respond intelligently when they occur.

This distinction is important to me.

I want to reduce avoidable mistakes while remaining willing to experiment.

Preparation can prevent many problems.

Testing can identify errors.

Documentation can reduce misunderstandings.

Communication can prevent assumptions.

Review can catch weaknesses.

But no system is perfect.

Unexpected situations will still occur.

The ability to recover becomes important.
