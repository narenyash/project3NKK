
India is often described through its diversity of languages, cultures, traditions, regions, and communities. For me, diversity is not simply a characteristic of the country. It is an everyday experience that has influenced the way I observe people, communicate with them, and understand problems.

Growing up in India has taught me that there is rarely one way of seeing the world.

People can live in the same city and have very different experiences.

They can speak different languages.

They can have different educational backgrounds.

They can use technology differently.

They can have different expectations from the same service.

This understanding has become particularly important to me because of my interest in computer science and artificial intelligence.

Technology is often designed with the assumption that users will behave in predictable ways.

Real people are rarely that predictable.

Their needs depend on their circumstances.

Their choices depend on their experiences.

Their access to technology depends on their resources.

Their understanding of an application can depend on language, education, age, and familiarity with digital systems.

Therefore, building useful technology requires more than technical knowledge.

It requires an understanding of people.

My academic interest in technology began with programming.

Initially, I was fascinated by the technical side.

I wanted to understand how applications worked and how software could be built.

As I developed projects, however, I began to notice something important.

A program can be technically correct and still be difficult for people to use.

An application can contain many useful features and still fail to solve the user's actual problem.

A system can be efficient for one group and inconvenient for another.

This made me realise that technology cannot be separated from the people who use it.

India's diversity makes this especially visible.

Consider language.

A large amount of digital content is available primarily in English.

For students and professionals who are comfortable with English, this may not create a significant barrier.

For others, it can make technology less accessible.

India's linguistic diversity means that digital systems need to consider more than one language if they are intended to serve large populations.

This is an interesting technical challenge.

Natural language processing can potentially help bridge linguistic barriers.

Machine translation can improve access to information.

Speech recognition can support users who are more comfortable speaking than typing.

Conversational interfaces can make technology easier to interact with.

But technology alone does not solve the problem.

Language is connected to culture and context.

A translation that is grammatically correct may still sound unnatural.

A system trained primarily on one linguistic variety may perform differently on another.

A voice interface may struggle with accents or regional variations.

Therefore, developing language technology requires understanding the diversity within languages themselves.

This is one of the reasons artificial intelligence interests me.

AI provides technical methods for working with complex patterns.

But those methods must be developed and evaluated carefully.

A model that performs well on one dataset may not work equally well across all users.

This is not merely a technical issue.

It is an issue of inclusion.

If an AI system works well for some people and poorly for others, the consequences can be significant.

This has made me more interested in responsible AI.

A system should not be evaluated only by its average performance.

Developers should also consider who is represented in the data and who may be underserved.

This principle applies beyond language.

India contains significant differences in digital access.

Some users have fast internet connections and modern devices.

Others may rely on older smartphones or limited connectivity.

Some people are highly familiar with digital services.

Others may be using them for the first time.

A technology designed without considering these differences can unintentionally exclude people.

This has changed how I think about application development.

When designing a system, I want to ask practical questions.

What happens if the internet connection is slow?

What happens if the user has limited storage?

Can the important functions work without unnecessary complexity?

Is the interface understandable to someone unfamiliar with the system?

Are instructions clear?

Can the system be accessed through different devices?

These questions may not appear in the initial programming requirements, but they can determine whether the product is genuinely useful.

Diversity therefore encourages better engineering.

It forces designers to question assumptions.

It makes them consider edge cases that may actually represent large groups of users.

It encourages flexibility.

It can also improve creativity.

When people with different experiences work together, they can identify problems that a homogeneous group may overlook.

One person may notice a usability issue.

Another may identify a cultural consideration.

Another may suggest a different technical approach.

Another may understand the needs of a particular community.

Together, these perspectives can produce a stronger solution.

This is one reason I want to work in diverse teams during higher education.

I want to learn from people whose experiences differ from mine.

University provides an opportunity to meet students from different regions, educational backgrounds, and areas of interest.

A classroom can contain many perspectives.

A technical project can bring together different skills.

A research group can include people approaching the same problem from different disciplines.

I believe this environment can strengthen both technical and personal development.

Diversity also teaches adaptability.

When communicating with people who have different experiences, I cannot always assume that my preferred way of explaining something will be effective.

I may need to listen more carefully.

I may need to explain a technical idea using a different example.

I may need to adjust my communication style.

These are valuable professional skills.

A good engineer must often explain technical concepts to non-technical people.

The ability to communicate across differences can determine whether an idea is understood and adopted.

This is particularly important when developing technology for public use.

A technical team may understand every detail of a system.

The end user may not care about the architecture.

They care about whether the system solves their problem.

Therefore, engineers must translate technical complexity into practical value.

I want to become better at this.

India's diversity also provides opportunities for innovation.

The country's large population and varied environments create many different types of problems.

Agriculture presents challenges that differ across regions.

Healthcare access varies between communities.

Education systems operate under different resource constraints.

Urban and rural environments have different infrastructure needs.

The context may be different.

The infrastructure may be different.

The users may be different.

The economics may be different.

The cultural expectations may be different.

Therefore, adaptation is important.

I want to develop the ability to take technological ideas and adapt them to local needs.

An educational platform may need to work with varying levels of connectivity and digital access.

AI cannot simply be deployed without understanding these conditions.

This is why I want to develop interdisciplinary knowledge.

Computer science can provide the technical foundation.

A strong engineer should know when to seek expertise from other fields.

I want to become comfortable doing this.

Diversity also affects teamwork.

People from different backgrounds may have different approaches to deadlines, communication, problem solving, and decision-making.

At first, these differences can create misunderstandings.

But they can also create learning opportunities.

Instead of assuming that one approach is correct, team members can discuss their preferences.

What works best for this project?

What does each person need?

How can responsibilities be divided?

How can communication be improved?

These conversations can create stronger teams.

I want to learn how to participate in such discussions respectfully.

Listening is especially important.

When people have different experiences, it is easy to respond from one's own perspective.

But effective collaboration requires understanding the other person's perspective first.

I want to develop that habit.

Diversity has also taught me that assumptions can be dangerous.

A person may assume that everyone understands a particular cultural reference.

A developer may assume that users understand a particular interface convention.

A teacher may assume that students have access to the same resources.

A company may assume that every customer has similar digital habits.

These assumptions can create barriers.

The solution is not to avoid assumptions entirely.

That would be impossible.

The solution is to identify important assumptions and test them.

This is good engineering practice as well.

Requirements should be validated.

Prototypes should be tested.

Users should provide feedback.

Data should be examined.

Assumptions should be revised when evidence contradicts them.

Thus, diversity and engineering share an important principle:

Do not assume.

Investigate.

This principle is something I want to carry throughout my education.
