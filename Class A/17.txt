
The first user I designed for was myself.

That was my first mistake.

When I started building software, I naturally imagined how I would use it. I knew what each button meant. I knew why each screen existed. I understood the sequence of actions because I had created it.

So when something seemed obvious to me, I assumed it would be obvious to everyone else.

Then I built something for someone who was not me.

It was a small project, but it changed how I think about engineering.

The person I was building for did not care which programming language I used.

They did not care how elegant my code was.

They did not care how many hours I had spent designing the architecture.

They cared about one thing:

Could the software make their task easier?

That question was simple.

My answer was not.

I had spent so much time thinking about the system that I had forgotten to think about the person.

That experience introduced me to a lesson that has stayed with me:

A product is experienced from the outside, even though it is built from the inside.

As a developer, I naturally see the inside.

I see components.

I see functions.

I see APIs.

I see databases.

I see requests moving through a system.

The user sees none of that.

The user sees a screen.

They see a button.

They see a message.

They see whether something works.

They see whether it makes sense.

That difference between the developer's perspective and the user's perspective became one of the most important things I have learned.

The project itself began with a straightforward goal.

I wanted to create a digital system that would simplify a repetitive process. I had already imagined the basic architecture before speaking to the person who would use it.

I had chosen the technologies.

I had thought about the interface.

I had already decided how the workflow should operate.

Then I asked the user to try it.

Almost immediately, I saw problems.

They paused at a screen that I thought was obvious.

They looked for information in a place I had not expected.

They ignored a feature I considered important.

They asked why they needed to complete a particular step.

None of these things were programming bugs.

The application was doing exactly what I had designed it to do.

That was the problem.

I had designed it according to my understanding of the task rather than theirs.

My first reaction was to explain the interface.

I told them where to click.

I explained why the steps existed.

Then I realized what I was doing.

I was teaching the user how to use my software instead of asking why the software required teaching.

That was the moment I started changing the design.

I removed unnecessary steps.

I simplified labels.

I reorganized information.

I changed the order of actions.

I watched again.

The second version was better.

Not because I had written better code.

Because I had listened better.

That distinction became important to me.

I began understanding empathy as an engineering skill.

Before this experience, I thought empathy belonged mostly to interpersonal relationships.

I did not think of it as part of programming.

But software is an interaction between people and systems.

If I do not understand the person using the system, I cannot fully understand the problem I am trying to solve.

Empathy therefore becomes practical.

It affects design decisions.

It affects language.

It affects accessibility.

It affects error messages.

It affects how much information a user should be expected to remember.

It affects what happens when someone makes a mistake.

It affects whether a system feels supportive or frustrating.

This realization changed how I approached every project afterward.

I began asking different questions.

Who will use this?

What do they already know?

What do they expect to happen?

What will confuse them?

What will they do when something goes wrong?

What information do they actually need?

What can the system do automatically?

The questions often led to better designs.

They also made me more interested in human-computer interaction.

Computers are incredibly precise.

People are not.

People forget.

They make assumptions.

They misunderstand instructions.

They change their minds.

They use systems in ways developers did not anticipate.

Good software has to accommodate this reality.

It cannot assume that every user will behave exactly as the developer expected.

This became particularly clear when I worked with applications that involved forms and data.

From the developer's perspective, a form can look simple.

A few fields.

A submit button.

Some validation.

From the user's perspective, however, each field creates a question.

What should I enter?

Why do you need this?

What format should I use?

What happens if I leave it blank?

What does this term mean?

If the interface does not answer those questions, the user is forced to guess.

I began realizing that every interface communicates assumptions.

A label assumes the user understands a term.

A menu assumes the user knows where to look.

A warning assumes the user understands the consequence.

A button assumes the user knows what will happen after clicking it.

Good design makes those assumptions visible or removes them.

This idea has influenced the way I write software.

I try to make error messages useful rather than merely technically accurate.

I try to avoid unnecessary terminology.

I try to make important actions obvious.

I try to think about what a first-time user would experience.

These changes may appear small.

But small changes can determine whether someone trusts a system.

That became especially important when I started thinking about artificial intelligence.

AI systems introduce a new layer of complexity.

Traditional software usually behaves according to rules written by developers.

AI systems can produce outputs that are difficult for users to predict.

A person may not know exactly why a model produced a particular answer.

That creates a new responsibility.

The system needs to communicate uncertainty.

It needs to provide useful feedback.

It needs to make clear when an output may require verification.

The user should not have to understand the model's internal architecture to use the system safely.

This is where human-centered design becomes essential.

An AI system can be technically sophisticated and still be poorly designed.

It may generate excellent outputs but provide them in a confusing interface.

It may answer questions without explaining its limitations.

It may perform well for experienced users but frustrate beginners.

The technology can be powerful while the experience remains poor.

I want to work on the other side of that problem.

I want to understand how intelligent systems can become easier to use without becoming less capable.

This interest has led me toward questions about conversational interfaces.

Natural language can make technology more accessible because people do not need to learn complicated commands.

They can simply describe what they want.

But natural language also introduces ambiguity.

A user may say something that could mean several things.

An intelligent system should not always guess.

Sometimes it should ask.

That sounds simple, but deciding when to ask a clarification question is an interesting engineering problem.

Ask too often, and the system becomes frustrating.

Ask too rarely, and it may perform the wrong action.

The ideal system needs to balance confidence, convenience, and safety.

This is an example of the kind of problem I enjoy.

It has a technical side.

It has a design side.

It has a human side.

And it has no single perfect answer.

The same principle applies to accessibility.

If I build an application for someone who has different needs from mine, I cannot assume that my preferred interaction is universal.

People may use keyboards differently.

They may have different levels of technical experience.

They may rely on screen readers.

They may have limited connectivity.

They may use different languages.

They may interact with technology primarily through mobile devices.

Designing for these differences does not make a product weaker.

It often makes the product stronger.

A clear interface benefits everyone.

A responsive application benefits everyone.

Simple language benefits everyone.

Good error handling benefits everyone.

Accessibility therefore changed my understanding of what "good design" means.

It is not about making technology look sophisticated.

It is about reducing unnecessary barriers.

This lesson has also influenced my interest in technology for education.

Students are not identical users.

They learn at different speeds.

They have different prior knowledge.

They become confused by different concepts.

They ask different questions.

A traditional digital learning platform may provide the same material to everyone.

AI creates the possibility of something more adaptive.

An intelligent educational system could potentially identify where a student is struggling and adjust its explanation.

But to do this well, the system must understand the learner.

It cannot simply provide more information.

Sometimes the student needs less information.

Sometimes they need a different example.

Sometimes they need a hint.

Sometimes they need to try the problem themselves.

Sometimes they need encouragement.

This is why I am interested in AI systems that support people rather than simply giving them answers.

The goal should be to increase capability.

Not dependence.

That principle comes partly from my own experiences helping other students learn programming.

When someone asks me how to solve a problem, I can give them the solution.

That is the fastest approach.

But it may not be the most useful.

If I instead ask them what they think is happening and help them trace the problem, they may learn a method they can use again.

The same principle could apply to AI.

An intelligent system should sometimes guide rather than simply solve.

This is one of the reasons I am interested in the future of human-AI collaboration.

I do not want to think of AI only as a replacement for human work.

I am more interested in how humans and intelligent systems can complement each other.

A person may provide judgment, context, creativity, and values.

An AI system may provide rapid analysis, pattern recognition, summarization, or computation.

The combination can be more powerful than either alone.

But the interface between them matters.

If the human does not understand what the AI is doing, collaboration becomes difficult.

If the AI cannot understand what the human needs, its capabilities become irrelevant.

The quality of the relationship depends on communication.

That is why I keep returning to the user.

Behind every technical system is a person with a goal.

The system exists because someone wants something done.

Remembering that prevents technology from becoming an end in itself.

I have sometimes been tempted by technology for its own sake.

A new framework looks exciting.

A new AI model seems powerful.

A new architecture appears elegant.

But the question I now try to ask is:

"Who needs this?"

If I cannot answer that question, I become skeptical about whether the technology belongs in the project.

This does not mean every project needs an immediate social impact.

Sometimes learning is the purpose.

Sometimes experimentation is the purpose.

Sometimes building something simply because it is interesting is worthwhile.

Curiosity matters.

But when I claim that a project is meant to solve a real problem, I want to make sure there is a real person behind that claim.

This mindset has changed my definition of innovation.

Innovation is not simply using newer technology.

It is creating a better way of doing something that matters.

Sometimes that requires advanced AI.

Sometimes it requires a simple interface.

Sometimes it requires removing a feature.

Sometimes the most innovative decision is realizing that the original problem was misunderstood.

That realization has made me more patient when designing systems.

I no longer want to rush into implementation.

I want to observe first.

Ask questions.

Build a small version.

Watch someone use it.

Listen.

Then improve it.

This cycle feels natural to me now.

It combines my interest in technology with my interest in people.

It also gives me a way to evaluate my own work.

Instead of asking only whether the code is correct, I ask whether the user succeeds.

Instead of asking only whether the model performs well, I ask whether the output helps.

Instead of asking only whether the interface looks good, I ask whether someone can understand it without explanation.

These questions are harder than technical tests.

They are also more meaningful.

I want college to help me develop this way of thinking further.

I want to study computer science deeply.

I want to learn algorithms, artificial intelligence, machine learning, databases, and systems.

But I also want to explore human-computer interaction, design, accessibility, and responsible technology.

I want to work on interdisciplinary projects where engineers have to understand people rather than simply specifications.

I want to participate in research that asks how intelligent systems can support human goals.

And I want to learn from people who approach technology from perspectives different from mine.

My long-term goal is to become an engineer capable of building intelligent systems that are genuinely useful.

Not merely powerful.

Not merely impressive.

Useful.

That distinction matters to me.

A powerful system that creates confusion is not successful.

A technically elegant system that nobody wants to use is not successful.

An accurate AI model that cannot be trusted in real-world conditions is not successful.

Success requires connecting technical capability to human need.

That connection is what I want to spend my career exploring.

The user who struggled with my first interface taught me something I could not have learned from a programming tutorial.

They showed me that the person using the system does not see the architecture behind it.

They experience the consequences.

That realization made me a different kind of developer.

I became more willing to question my own decisions.

More willing to observe.

More willing to simplify.

More willing to ask for feedback.

And more aware that good technology requires understanding people.

I still care about algorithms.

I still enjoy programming.

I still get excited about new AI models and technical possibilities.

But now, when I build something, I try to imagine the person on the other side of the screen.

What are they trying to accomplish?

What do they know?

What are they worried about?

What might confuse them?

What could make their experience easier?

The user is often invisible in the code.

But they are the reason the code exists.

That is the lesson I want to carry into college and eventually into my career.

I want to build from the inside with the perspective of someone standing outside.

I want to understand systems deeply without forgetting the people who use them.

And I want every technical decision I make to answer a simple question:

Does this make someone's life better?

Because the best software is not the software that makes the developer feel clever.

It is the software that makes the user feel capable.
