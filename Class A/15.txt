
There is a particular moment in every competition when the excitement becomes pressure.

The clock is running.

The problem is no longer theoretical.

People around you are working quickly.

An idea that seemed obvious an hour ago suddenly has three unexpected problems.

Someone asks whether a feature is ready.

Someone else discovers that an assumption was wrong.

And there is no time to start everything again.

I have experienced that feeling through hackathons and technical competitions.

At first, I thought competitions were mainly about winning.

The goal seemed simple: build something better than everyone else.

But the more competitions I participated in, the more complicated my understanding became.

Winning was rewarding.

Losing was disappointing.

But neither outcome fully represented what I had learned.

The most important lessons happened in the middle.

They happened when an idea had to become a working prototype.

They happened when teammates disagreed.

They happened when time ran out.

They happened when the simplest solution turned out to be the strongest one.

And they happened when I realized that a team could accomplish something that none of its members could have built alone.

My first experiences with technical competitions were exciting because they gave me a reason to build quickly.

Normally, a project can take weeks or months.

A hackathon compresses that process into a short period.

You have to understand the problem, choose an approach, divide responsibilities, build a prototype, test it, prepare a presentation, and explain why the solution matters.

The first time I experienced that pressure, I focused almost entirely on the technology.

I wanted the project to look impressive.

I wanted to use advanced tools.

I wanted to demonstrate as many features as possible.

I eventually learned that this approach can be dangerous.

Complexity consumes time.

Every additional feature creates another potential failure.

Every new integration creates another dependency.

Every technical decision creates consequences elsewhere in the system.

Under normal circumstances, these problems can be handled gradually.

Under competition pressure, they become much more serious.

I learned to prioritize.

What is the core problem?

What is the minimum functionality needed to demonstrate the solution?

Which features genuinely improve the product?

Which features can wait?

What can be simplified?

These questions became more important than the desire to make the project look sophisticated.

This was one of the most valuable lessons competitions gave me.

A good idea is not enough.

A good idea that cannot be implemented within the available constraints remains an idea.

Engineering is the process of turning possibility into something that actually works.

That requires decisions.

And decisions require trade-offs.

I began to understand the difference between an ambitious concept and a realistic plan.

This did not make me less ambitious.

It made my ambition more practical.

Instead of asking how many features we could build, I began asking how much value we could create within the time available.

That change improved the way I approached projects outside competitions as well.

Another lesson came from working with teams.

When a competition begins, everyone can be enthusiastic.

Ideas come quickly.

People want to contribute.

But enthusiasm alone does not create a functioning team.

Someone has to organize the work.

Someone has to make decisions when two approaches conflict.

Someone has to communicate what is happening.

Someone has to recognize when the plan is no longer realistic.

I learned that teamwork is not simply dividing a project into equal pieces.

It is coordinating different strengths.

A teammate may be much better at frontend development than I am.

Another may understand machine learning more deeply.

Someone else may be better at presenting an idea.

Another person may notice problems that everyone else has missed.

The strongest teams do not require everyone to be identical.

They make differences useful.

This changed my understanding of leadership.

I used to think leadership meant being the person with the answers.

Now I think leadership often means creating the conditions for other people to contribute their answers.

A good team leader does not need to control every decision.

They need to keep the team moving toward a shared goal.

This can mean asking for opinions.

It can mean resolving disagreements.

It can mean accepting that someone else's approach is better.

It can also mean telling the team that an exciting feature should be removed because it is consuming too much time.

That last decision can be particularly difficult.

People become emotionally attached to their ideas.

I have experienced this myself.

When I spend hours building something, I naturally want it to remain part of the final product.

But sometimes the project is better without it.

Learning to let go of work became an important part of my growth.

A feature is not valuable because I spent time building it.

It is valuable if it improves the final solution.

This distinction sounds obvious.

Under pressure, it is not.

Competitions taught me to separate effort from value.

That lesson has influenced how I evaluate my work.

I try not to ask only:

"How much work did I put into this?"

I also ask:

"Did the work improve the result?"

Sometimes the answer is yes.

Sometimes it is no.

Sometimes the work taught me something even if it did not improve the final product.

That last category is important.

A failed implementation can still be useful if it teaches me what not to do next time.

This is another reason I value competitions.

They create concentrated learning.

A few intense hours can expose weaknesses that might otherwise remain hidden for months.

Perhaps I am not communicating clearly.

Perhaps I underestimate development time.

Perhaps I rely too heavily on one technology.

Perhaps I do not test early enough.

Perhaps I am trying to solve too many problems simultaneously.

A competition reveals these weaknesses quickly.

That can be uncomfortable.

It can also be incredibly useful.

One of the most important things I learned was how to work under uncertainty.

In a normal academic assignment, the requirements are usually clear.

In a competition, they may not be.

The problem statement can be broad.

The available technologies may be unfamiliar.

The users may be hypothetical.

The best solution may not be obvious.

You have to make decisions before you have complete information.

That is similar to real engineering.

Real projects rarely begin with perfect specifications.

Requirements change.

Users provide unexpected feedback.

Technologies behave differently from what was expected.

New constraints appear.

The ability to make reasonable decisions with incomplete information is therefore an important skill.

Competitions gave me practice in it.

They also taught me how important communication is.

A technically brilliant idea can fail if it cannot be explained.

Judges, users, teammates, and potential stakeholders need to understand what the system does and why it matters.

This forced me to think beyond code.

What problem are we solving?

Why does the problem matter?

Who experiences it?

How is our solution different?

What evidence do we have that it works?

What are its limitations?

These questions became part of every project.

I began realizing that communication is not something added at the end of engineering.

It is part of engineering itself.

If you cannot explain the problem, you may not understand it.

If you cannot explain the solution, it may not be clearly designed.

If you cannot explain the limitations, you may not have tested it thoroughly.

Competitions therefore strengthened both my technical and communication skills.

They also changed my relationship with failure.

When the result of a competition is announced, the ranking is clear.

Someone wins.

Someone does not.

That can make failure feel final.

But I learned that a ranking captures only one moment.

A team can lose a competition and still leave with a better understanding of technology than it had before.

A team can win and still have many weaknesses to improve.

The most meaningful outcome is often what happens afterward.

What did we learn?

What would we change?

Which part of the project should continue?

Which idea deserves further development?

What problem did we discover that we had not considered?

These questions matter more to me now than the ranking itself.

That does not mean I stopped caring about competition.

I still enjoy trying to win.

Competition creates urgency.

It pushes me to work harder.

It gives me a reason to test myself against difficult challenges.

But I no longer believe that winning is the only evidence that the experience was worthwhile.

I want to compete seriously while remaining curious.

That balance matters.

If I focus only on winning, I may avoid risky ideas because they might fail.

If I focus only on learning, I may not push myself hard enough.

The ideal is somewhere between the two.

Compete with ambition.

Learn with humility.

That is the attitude I want to carry forward.

My experiences in competitions also influenced my interest in entrepreneurship.

A hackathon provides a compressed version of the startup process.

You begin with a problem.

You form a hypothesis.

You create a prototype.

You test assumptions.

You present the result.

Then someone evaluates whether the idea is valuable.

The process is incomplete, but the underlying logic is similar.

It taught me that building something is only one part of creating value.

You also need to understand whether people want it.

A technically impressive product that nobody needs is not a successful solution.

This brought me back to a lesson I had encountered in other projects: technology should begin with a problem.

The best competition ideas are often not the ones with the most complicated technology.

They are the ones that identify a meaningful problem and use technology appropriately.

That perspective has shaped the kind of projects I want to build.

I am interested in artificial intelligence, but I do not want to add AI simply because it sounds advanced.

I want to ask whether AI provides an actual advantage.

Can it reduce a repetitive task?

Can it identify patterns humans would struggle to find?

Can it make information more accessible?

Can it personalize an experience?

Can it help someone make a better decision?

If the answer is no, then another technology may be more appropriate.

Competitions taught me to make that distinction quickly.

Time pressure has a way of revealing what matters.

When you have limited hours, unnecessary complexity becomes obvious.

You cannot afford to build everything.

You have to choose.

That ability to choose is one of the skills I want to strengthen in college.

University will give me opportunities to work on larger and more complex projects.

I want to participate in research groups.

I want to collaborate with students from different disciplines.

I want to take part in technical competitions.

I want to build products.

But I also want to understand the deeper principles behind what I am building.

Competition taught me how to move quickly.

College can teach me when moving quickly is appropriate and when a problem deserves slower investigation.

Some problems require a prototype in a weekend.

Others require months of research.

Some require experimentation.

Others require mathematical proof.

Some need an immediate solution.

Others need careful consideration before implementation.

I want to learn to recognize the difference.

My long-term goal is to work in artificial intelligence and software engineering, developing systems that solve practical problems.

I want to combine technical depth with the ability to work effectively in teams.

I want to become someone who can contribute ideas, listen to others, make decisions under pressure, and take responsibility when things go wrong.

Competitions have shown me that technical skill alone is not enough.

A brilliant programmer who cannot collaborate can become a bottleneck.

A great idea without execution remains unfinished.

A fast team without direction can waste time.

A careful team without urgency may never finish.

Success requires balance.

That balance is something I continue to develop.

There is one moment from competitions that I value more than the final announcement.

It is the moment when a prototype finally works after hours of uncertainty.

Someone runs the application.

The feature responds.

The data appears correctly.

The system behaves as expected.

For a few seconds, everyone on the team stops talking.

Then someone smiles.

That moment is not about winning.

It is about creating something that did not exist before.

The idea has become real.

I think that is what attracts me most to engineering.

The transformation from possibility to reality.

An idea begins as a conversation.

Then it becomes a sketch.

Then code.

Then a prototype.

Then a system that someone can actually use.

The process is messy.

It involves disagreements, bugs, compromises, and unexpected problems.

But when it works, even briefly, there is something deeply satisfying about it.

Competitions gave me many of those moments.

They also gave me failures, unfinished features, difficult decisions, and lessons I did not expect.

I value all of them.

Because I have learned that the finish line is not the most important part of a competition.

The real value is everything you become capable of doing by the time you reach it.

You learn to make decisions faster.

You learn to communicate.

You learn to compromise.

You learn to prioritize.

You learn to recover when something fails.

You learn to trust teammates.

You learn to accept criticism.

You learn to explain your work.

And you learn that a team can create something greater than any individual could create alone.

Those lessons will stay with me long after any particular competition is forgotten.

I want to carry them into college.

I want to compete, but I also want to collaborate.

I want to move quickly, but I also want to know when patience is necessary.

I want to aim for ambitious outcomes, but I also want to respect practical constraints.

I want to win, but I want to remain willing to learn from losing.

Most importantly, I want to keep building.

Because the best part of a competition is not hearing your name announced at the end.

It is looking at something that existed only as an idea a few hours earlier and realizing:

**We made this real.**

That is the finish line I care about most.
