
Education is often described as a pathway to a better career. While I agree with this, I believe education represents something much larger. It gives people the ability to understand the world, question existing assumptions, develop independent judgment, and create opportunities that may not otherwise be available to them. My own interest in higher education comes from this broader understanding. I do not want to pursue a degree simply as a qualification. I want education to transform the way I think, the way I solve problems, and the contribution I can make to society.

My interest in learning has always been connected to curiosity.

When I encounter something unfamiliar, I want to understand it.

When I see a process that appears inefficient, I wonder whether it can be improved.

When I discover a technology, I want to know what happens behind it.

This curiosity has gradually led me toward computer science, artificial intelligence, and engineering.

But the more I learn, the more I understand that education is not simply the accumulation of knowledge.

It is the development of capability.

A student can memorise information without understanding it.

A person can learn programming syntax without becoming a good problem solver.

Someone can earn high marks without learning how to apply knowledge to unfamiliar situations.

True education goes beyond these outcomes.

It teaches a person how to think.

This is one of the main reasons I want to continue my education.

I want to become better at analysing problems, questioning assumptions, evaluating evidence, and creating solutions.

Computer science provides an excellent environment for developing these abilities.

Programming requires logical thinking.

Algorithms require efficiency.

Mathematics requires precision.

Artificial intelligence requires statistical reasoning.

Software engineering requires planning and collaboration.

Each of these areas develops a different form of thinking.

Together, they can create a strong foundation for solving complex problems.

However, education becomes more valuable when knowledge is connected to real-world needs.

A student may learn how a database works in a classroom.

The deeper learning begins when they use that knowledge to build a system that solves an actual problem.

A student may learn machine learning theory.

The deeper understanding develops when they work with imperfect data and discover the limitations of their model.

A student may learn software engineering principles.

The deeper lesson emerges when a project becomes large enough to require maintainability, testing, security, and collaboration.

Practical experience transforms abstract knowledge into understanding.

This is why I want my higher education to include projects and research.

I want opportunities to apply concepts rather than simply study them for examinations.

Education should prepare students for uncertainty.

Textbook problems often provide all the necessary information.

Real-world problems rarely do.

A real problem may be poorly defined.

The data may be incomplete.

The requirements may change.

Different stakeholders may want different outcomes.

The available resources may be limited.

There may be several possible solutions.

There may be no perfect solution.

Students need opportunities to experience this uncertainty before entering professional life.

This is one reason I value project-based learning.

Projects force students to make decisions.

They must determine what information they need.

They must choose an approach.

They must manage time.

They must test assumptions.

They must respond to failure.

They must communicate with others.

These experiences create skills that examinations alone cannot fully develop.

Education also provides access to people.

A university brings together professors, researchers, students, professionals, and communities.

Each interaction can create learning.

A professor can introduce a research problem.

A classmate can provide a different perspective.

A senior student can share practical experience.

A professional can explain industry expectations.

A research group can expose students to advanced methods.

These opportunities are part of the value of higher education.

I want to make use of them actively.

I do not want to treat university as a place I attend only for lectures.

I want to participate in its intellectual community.

I want to ask questions.

I want to join projects.

I want to attend technical discussions.

I want to explore research.

I want to collaborate.

And I want to contribute.

Education also teaches responsibility.

As knowledge increases, so does the ability to influence outcomes.

A programmer can create software used by thousands of people.

A data scientist can build a model that influences decisions.

An AI engineer can develop systems that affect how people receive information.

These responsibilities require ethical thinking.

Technical ability without responsibility can create problems.

Therefore, I want my education to include consideration of the social consequences of technology.

Artificial intelligence makes this particularly important.

AI systems can provide significant benefits.

They can help analyse information, automate repetitive tasks, support research, assist education, and improve services.

But AI can also produce errors.

It can reflect biases in data.

It can create privacy concerns.

It can be misused.

Therefore, engineers must understand both capability and limitation.

I want to develop that balanced understanding.

Education should teach students not to ask only, "Can we build it?"

It should also encourage questions such as:

"Should we build it?"

"Who will benefit?"

"Who could be harmed?"

"What assumptions does the system make?"

"How can its limitations be communicated?"

These questions connect technical knowledge with responsibility.

This broader understanding of education is particularly relevant in India.

India has a large and diverse population with different levels of access to resources.

Education can play a significant role in creating opportunities.

Digital technologies can potentially expand access to information, training, and services.

Online learning platforms can allow students to access material beyond their local institutions.

Digital tools can support people who cannot easily travel for education.

AI-based systems can potentially provide personalised learning assistance.

However, technology does not automatically create equality.

Access to devices, connectivity, language, and digital literacy still matters.

Therefore, educational technology must be designed carefully.

It should address barriers rather than simply assume they do not exist.

This is an area I would like to explore in the future.

I am particularly interested in how AI can support education without replacing the human elements that make learning meaningful.

An intelligent learning system could identify areas where a student is struggling.

It could provide additional practice.

It could explain a concept in different ways.

It could suggest resources.

It could provide feedback.

But a teacher provides something different.

A teacher can understand emotions.

A teacher can recognise when a student lacks confidence.

A teacher can encourage effort.

A teacher can understand context.

Therefore, technology should support teachers and students rather than simply attempt to replace them.

This is the kind of human-centred AI I want to understand.
