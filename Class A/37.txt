
When I first became interested in engineering, I believed that technical knowledge would be the most important skill I needed to develop. I focused on programming languages, algorithms, software tools, and artificial intelligence. Over time, however, my experiences with projects and teamwork taught me an important lesson: technical knowledge becomes significantly more valuable when it can be communicated clearly.

An engineer may develop an excellent solution, but if the problem is not understood correctly, the solution may not address the real need. A researcher may make an important discovery, but if the results cannot be explained, others may not be able to build upon the work. A software developer may create a technically sophisticated system, but if users cannot understand how to use it, the system may fail to create meaningful value.

These experiences have changed my understanding of engineering.

I now believe that communication is not a secondary skill for a technical professional.

It is part of engineering itself.

My understanding of this began with simple academic experiences.

When I first learned programming, I often focused on getting the correct output. If the program worked, I considered the task complete.

As my projects became more complex, I realised that working code was only one part of a successful project.

Someone else might need to understand the code.

A teammate might need to modify it.

A user might need instructions.

A mentor might need to evaluate the design.

A future developer might need to maintain the system.

Suddenly, clarity became important.

Code that works but cannot be understood easily can become difficult to maintain.

A project whose requirements are unclear can lead to wasted effort.

A team that does not communicate regularly can create incompatible components.

These situations taught me that communication affects technical quality.

This became particularly clear when working on group projects.

Different members often have different assumptions about what the final product should accomplish.

One person may interpret a requirement one way.

Another may interpret it differently.

If these differences are not discussed early, the team may spend considerable time building separate solutions that do not fit together.

I learned that a short conversation at the beginning can prevent hours of unnecessary work later.

This changed the way I approach projects.

Before beginning implementation, I want to understand the objective.

What problem are we solving?

Who will use the system?

What features are essential?

What limitations exist?

What does each team member need to complete their work?

These questions may seem simple, but they create a shared understanding.

Communication therefore becomes part of planning.

I have also learned that listening is as important as speaking.

Technical professionals sometimes focus heavily on explaining their own ideas.

However, effective communication begins with understanding the other person.

A user may describe a problem without using technical terminology.

A client may explain a requirement in terms of outcomes rather than implementation.

A teammate may express a concern indirectly.

A student may struggle with a concept without knowing exactly what they do not understand.

The engineer must listen carefully enough to identify the underlying issue.

This is especially important when developing human-centred technology.

A developer cannot design a useful system simply by assuming what users want.

Users must be observed, questioned, and understood.

Their feedback may reveal problems that were invisible during development.

This has influenced my interest in software and artificial intelligence.

Technology should not be developed in isolation from its users.

The people who use a system often provide information that technical testing alone cannot reveal.

An application may be functionally correct but difficult to navigate.

An AI system may provide accurate predictions but present them in a way that users cannot understand.

A platform may contain many features but make the most important task difficult to complete.

These are communication and design problems as much as technical problems.

The system must communicate with the user.

The user must understand what the system is doing.

The system must provide understandable feedback.

This is one reason I am interested in user-centred design.

I want to learn how technical systems can communicate information clearly without overwhelming the user.

This is particularly relevant to artificial intelligence.

AI systems can be complex.

A model may process large quantities of data and produce a prediction, but the user may need to know how much confidence to place in that prediction.

If an AI system provides an answer without communicating its limitations, users may assume it is more reliable than it actually is.

Therefore, AI communication involves more than generating outputs.

It involves communicating uncertainty, limitations, and context.

I believe this will become increasingly important as AI systems are adopted across different industries.

A doctor may need to understand why an AI system flagged a medical image.

A teacher may need to understand how an educational recommendation was generated.

A business professional may need to understand why a system identified a particular trend.

A user should not necessarily need to understand the entire mathematical model, but they should receive enough information to use the system responsibly.

This requires careful communication.

My interest in research has also strengthened my appreciation for communication.

Research does not end when an experiment produces a result.

The result must be documented.

Methods must be explained.

Data must be described.

Limitations must be acknowledged.

Conclusions must be supported.

Other researchers must be able to understand what was done.

A poorly communicated research result is difficult to reproduce or evaluate.

Therefore, scientific writing is an important part of scientific work.

I want to develop this ability during higher education.

I want to learn how to read technical papers critically and eventually write clearly about my own work.

I want to communicate technical ideas without unnecessary complexity.

At the same time, I do not want to oversimplify important information.

The challenge is to make complex ideas understandable without making them inaccurate.

This is a skill I want to practise.

Communication is also important when working with people from different disciplines.

A computer scientist and a healthcare professional may use completely different terminology.

An engineer and an environmental scientist may describe the same problem differently.

A designer may focus on user experience while an engineer focuses on system performance.

A successful interdisciplinary project requires participants to develop a shared language.

The engineer must be able to explain technical constraints.

The domain expert must be able to explain practical requirements.

Both must understand enough of the other's perspective to collaborate effectively.

This is one of the reasons I want to participate in interdisciplinary projects during university.

I want to learn how to communicate with people whose technical backgrounds differ from mine.

This ability will be valuable in my future career.

Modern engineering projects rarely remain within a single technical area.

Artificial intelligence projects may involve data scientists, software engineers, domain experts, designers, product managers, and researchers.

Each person contributes different knowledge.

The project succeeds when those contributions are coordinated.

Communication provides that coordination.

A technically strong engineer who cannot communicate effectively may struggle to contribute fully to a team.

A good communicator without sufficient technical understanding may struggle to make appropriate technical decisions.

The strongest professionals combine both.

I want to develop that combination.

Communication also plays an important role in leadership.

A leader must communicate goals clearly.

Team members need to understand what they are working toward.

Responsibilities need to be explained.

Deadlines need to be discussed.

Problems need to be reported.

Decisions need to be documented.

Without communication, leadership becomes difficult.

This is particularly important during stressful situations.

When deadlines are approaching or a technical problem appears, unclear communication can increase confusion.

A leader needs to remain calm and communicate priorities.

What must be completed first?

Which problems are blocking progress?

Who is responsible for each task?

What can be postponed?

What support is required?

Clear answers to these questions can help a team remain focused.

I want to develop this ability through group projects and future professional experience.

Another lesson I have learned is that communication should be adapted to the audience.

The way I explain a machine learning model to a professor should be different from how I explain it to a school student.

The way I discuss an API with another developer should be different from how I explain its purpose to a non-technical stakeholder.

The information may be the same, but the presentation should change.

This does not mean changing the truth.

It means choosing the right level of detail.

Good communication begins by asking:

Who am I speaking to?

What do they already know?

What do they need to understand?

What decision will they make based on this information?

These questions can make technical communication more effective.

I have experienced the importance of this while helping others learn programming.

A concept that seems obvious to someone with technical experience may be difficult for a beginner.

When I first explained programming concepts, I sometimes used technical terminology without realising that it created confusion.

I gradually learned to use examples instead.

Instead of beginning with a formal definition, I could demonstrate the concept through a familiar situation.

For example, programming loops can be explained through repeated daily actions.

Conditional statements can be connected to ordinary decisions.

Variables can be compared with containers that hold changing values.

These analogies can make abstract concepts easier to approach.

Teaching others therefore improved my own communication.

It also improved my understanding of the subject.

When I had to explain something clearly, I had to understand it clearly first.

This is one of the reasons I value teaching and mentoring.

They create opportunities for mutual learning.

Communication is also connected to empathy.

A person may understand what they want to say but fail to consider how the listener will receive it.

An effective communicator considers the other person's perspective.

This is particularly important in engineering because technical decisions can affect users who have little technical knowledge.

An engineer must think about how a system will be experienced.

Will the instructions be clear?

Will the error messages be understandable?

Will the user know what to do next?

Will the system explain an important decision?

These questions demonstrate that communication is embedded within product design.

A well-designed interface communicates without requiring a separate explanation.

Icons, labels, notifications, colours, layouts, and feedback all communicate information.

Therefore, communication extends beyond spoken and written language.

It is part of the design of technology itself.

This is an area I want to explore further.

AI systems are becoming increasingly conversational.

As these systems become more capable, their communication becomes increasingly important.

An AI assistant should not simply produce an answer.

It should avoid unnecessary complexity.

It should communicate uncertainty when appropriate.

It should avoid creating false confidence.

It should make limitations clear.

These considerations will become important as AI systems are used in education, healthcare, business, and public services.

I want to contribute to the development of such systems.

Developing useful multilingual technology therefore requires both technical knowledge and cultural understanding.

This is another example of why engineering cannot be separated completely from communication.
