
For a long time, I thought mathematics was something that existed mostly inside classrooms.

There were equations to solve, formulas to remember, graphs to draw, and examinations to complete. I understood that mathematics was important, but I did not always understand why.

Then I started studying computer science.

Suddenly, mathematics began appearing everywhere.

It was inside algorithms.

It was behind probability.

It shaped machine learning.

It described relationships between data.

It helped explain optimization.

It provided the language through which computers could model problems that seemed impossible to express otherwise.

I began realizing that the mathematics I had treated as a collection of academic exercises was actually one of the foundations of the technology I was becoming interested in.

That realization changed the way I learned.

Before programming, I often approached mathematics by asking, "What formula should I use?"

After programming, I increasingly began asking, "What is this formula actually describing?"

That difference may seem small, but it transformed mathematics from something I needed to complete into something I wanted to understand.

One of the first areas where I noticed this change was probability.

Probability initially seemed abstract.

Why should I care about the likelihood of an event?

Why should I calculate conditional probability?

Why should uncertainty need mathematics?

Machine learning gave me an answer.

A computer often does not have complete certainty.

An image may resemble several categories.

A language model may assign different probabilities to possible words.

A prediction may be more likely than another without being guaranteed.

Instead of demanding certainty, machine learning often works with probabilities.

This made probability feel much more concrete.

The concepts were no longer isolated formulas.

They were tools for reasoning under uncertainty.

That idea fascinated me.

Human beings constantly make decisions without complete information.

We estimate.

We compare possibilities.

We update our beliefs when new evidence appears.

We rarely know everything.

Yet we still need to act.

Mathematics provides ways to represent that uncertainty.

This connection made me want to understand the subject more deeply.

I began paying attention to the mathematical ideas underneath the technologies I was learning.

Linear algebra was another example.

At first, vectors and matrices seemed like abstract objects.

Then I encountered machine learning.

Data could be represented as vectors.

Images could be represented numerically.

Transformations could be expressed through matrices.

Large datasets could be manipulated using mathematical operations.

The abstract objects suddenly had a purpose.

A matrix was no longer just an arrangement of numbers.

It could represent a transformation.

A vector was not simply a list of values.

It could represent information.

This was one of the moments when I understood that mathematics often becomes more interesting when you know what it is capable of describing.

The same happened with optimization.

When I first encountered the idea of minimizing a function, it seemed like another mathematical exercise.

Machine learning changed the context.

Training a model often involves finding parameters that minimize some measure of error.

The process becomes an optimization problem.

A model begins with one set of parameters.

It produces predictions.

The predictions are compared with expected results.

The error is measured.

The parameters are adjusted.

The process repeats.

Suddenly, an abstract mathematical concept became part of a system that could learn from data.

That connection fascinated me.

It also changed how I approached programming.

I began realizing that code is often the implementation of an idea that exists at a more abstract level.

A programmer writes instructions, but behind those instructions are mathematical structures, logical relationships, and assumptions about the problem.

Understanding the abstraction can make the implementation easier.

This became particularly useful when I encountered algorithms.

Instead of memorizing how an algorithm worked, I became more interested in why it worked.

Why does a particular sorting algorithm require a certain amount of time?

Why does a particular search strategy become inefficient as the dataset grows?

Why does a graph representation make some problems easier to solve?

Why does a particular optimization strategy converge?

These questions made computer science feel less like programming syntax and more like applied mathematics and logic.

That is one of the reasons I became increasingly interested in algorithms.

An algorithm is not simply a sequence of instructions.

It is a structured way of thinking about a problem.

The same problem can often be approached through several algorithms, each with different trade-offs.

One may use more memory but run faster.

Another may use less memory but require more computation.

One may work well for small datasets but become inefficient at scale.

These trade-offs taught me that there is rarely one universally perfect solution.

Good problem solving requires understanding the conditions under which a solution operates.

That lesson extends far beyond mathematics.

It has influenced how I approach software projects.

When I build an application, I increasingly think about how the system will behave as the amount of data grows.

A solution that works for ten users may not work for ten thousand.

A database query that feels instantaneous on a small dataset may become expensive at scale.

An algorithm that is acceptable for a small input may become impractical for a large one.

Mathematical thinking helps reveal these differences before they become problems.

This is why I have come to value fundamentals.

Technology changes quickly.

Frameworks change.

Programming languages evolve.

AI models become more capable.

Development tools improve.

But algorithms, mathematics, and logical reasoning remain relevant.

They provide a foundation beneath the changing tools.

I want to build my education on that foundation.

My interest in mathematics has also changed the way I respond to difficult problems.

Earlier, if I could not immediately solve a problem, I often assumed that I was missing a particular formula or trick.

Now I am more likely to ask whether I understand the structure of the problem.

What information is given?

What is unknown?

What relationships exist between the variables?

What assumptions can I make?

Can the problem be divided into smaller pieces?

Can I represent it differently?

This approach has become useful in programming as well.

A difficult software problem can become easier when represented differently.

A complex dataset can become understandable after visualization.

A large task can become manageable when decomposed.

A confusing system can become clearer when its dependencies are mapped.

Mathematics taught me to look for structure.

Computer science gave me tools for using that structure.

The combination has become one of the strongest influences on the way I think.

My interest in artificial intelligence has made this relationship even stronger.

AI can sometimes appear almost magical from the outside.

A model receives an image and identifies an object.

A language model generates an answer.

A system predicts a pattern.

But underneath those impressive results are mathematical operations.

There are vectors, matrices, probability distributions, optimization procedures, functions, parameters, and transformations.

The more I learn, the less mysterious AI becomes.

It does not become less impressive.

It becomes more impressive.

Understanding even a small part of what happens underneath the surface makes the achievement feel more meaningful.

It also makes me more aware of the limitations.

Mathematical models are representations of reality.

They are not reality itself.

A model simplifies.

It makes assumptions.

It focuses on certain variables and ignores others.

That means a model can be useful while still being incomplete.

This idea has become important to me because AI systems increasingly influence real-world decisions.

If we use a mathematical model to make predictions, we need to understand what the model represents and what it leaves out.

A high-performing model can still fail in situations that differ from the data used to build it.

Understanding the mathematics does not eliminate these risks, but it helps us recognize them.

This is one reason I want deeper academic training.

I do not want to treat AI models as black boxes that I simply call through a library.

I want to understand the principles behind them.

I want to understand why particular algorithms work.

I want to understand how optimization changes model behavior.

I want to understand how probability represents uncertainty.

I want to understand the mathematics that allows complex systems to learn from data.

This curiosity is not limited to AI.

I am also interested in how mathematics describes physical systems.

Physics uses mathematical models to describe motion, energy, waves, forces, and countless other phenomena.

Computer simulations can then use those models to explore situations that are difficult or impossible to reproduce physically.

This creates another connection that fascinates me.

Mathematics can describe an idea.

Computer science can implement that description.

Computing can then allow us to experiment with the model.

The cycle between mathematics, computation, and observation is powerful.

It allows us to investigate problems at scales that would otherwise be inaccessible.

This is one of the reasons I am interested in scientific computing and computational research.

A computer is not simply a machine that performs arithmetic quickly.

It can become a laboratory for ideas.

We can simulate systems.

We can test hypotheses.

We can analyze large datasets.

We can explore possibilities.

We can compare models.

We can identify patterns.

And mathematics provides much of the language that makes this possible.

The more I learn, the more I appreciate the elegance of that relationship.

There is another lesson mathematics has taught me: precision matters.

In everyday conversation, ambiguity is often acceptable.

In mathematics, a small change in notation can change the meaning of an expression.

A wrong assumption can produce a completely different result.

A proof requires every important step to be justified.

Programming has a similar characteristic.

A computer does not interpret what I intended.

It executes what I actually wrote.

This has made me more careful.

I have learned to distinguish between what I think a system does and what I can demonstrate that it does.

That difference is important in engineering.

It is easy to assume that a program is correct because it worked once.

It is harder—and more useful—to test the assumptions systematically.

Mathematics trained me to value proof.

Programming trained me to value testing.

Together, they have taught me to seek evidence.

This mindset is particularly important in research.

Research is not about defending an idea simply because you like it.

It is about determining whether the evidence supports it.

Sometimes the result confirms your hypothesis.

Sometimes it does not.

Both outcomes are valuable.

A negative result can eliminate an incorrect explanation.

A surprising result can reveal an assumption that needs examination.

I want to experience that process more deeply in college.

I want to work on problems where the answer is not already known.

I want to read research papers and understand the mathematical reasoning behind them.

I want to implement algorithms and compare their behavior experimentally.

I want to explore machine learning beyond simply training existing models.

And I want to develop the mathematical maturity necessary to understand research rather than merely reproduce it.

This is one reason I see college as an opportunity to move from application toward understanding.

So far, much of my learning has involved taking existing tools and using them to build projects.

That has been valuable.

It has taught me how to develop applications, experiment with AI, work with data, and solve practical problems.

But I now want to go deeper.

I want to understand what happens beneath the tools.

I want to know why algorithms have the properties they do.

I want to understand how mathematical assumptions influence computational systems.

I want to learn how new algorithms are designed rather than only how existing ones are implemented.

That transition—from user of knowledge to investigator of knowledge—is something I am looking forward to.

I also recognize that mathematics can be difficult.

There have been concepts I did not understand immediately.

There have been problems where I tried several approaches before finding one that worked.

Sometimes I have had to return to fundamentals before I could understand a more advanced topic.

But I no longer see that difficulty as a reason to avoid the subject.

In fact, it is part of what makes mathematics valuable.

It forces me to slow down.

It prevents me from relying entirely on intuition.

It makes me justify my reasoning.

It teaches me to remain with a problem even when the answer is not obvious.

Those habits are valuable in every technical field.

They are valuable in life as well.

One of the most important changes in my thinking has been learning that being stuck is not the same as being incapable.

Sometimes being stuck means that I need a different representation.

Sometimes I need more information.

Sometimes I need to revisit an earlier concept.

Sometimes I need someone else's perspective.

Sometimes I simply need more time.

That realization has made difficult problems less intimidating.

I do not expect myself to immediately understand everything.

I expect myself to investigate.

That is how I want to approach university.

I want to be challenged by mathematics that initially seems beyond my ability.

I want to encounter algorithms that require careful reasoning.

I want to study machine learning at a deeper level.

I want to explore the relationship between mathematical models and real-world systems.

I want to work with students who enjoy asking difficult questions.

And I want to discover areas of mathematics that I have not yet encountered.

My long-term interests lie in artificial intelligence, software engineering, and computational research.

I want to build systems that are useful, but I also want to understand the principles that make them possible.

I want to be capable of moving between theory and implementation.

I want to be able to read a mathematical idea and think about how it could become an algorithm.

I want to be able to examine an algorithm and understand its mathematical structure.

I want to be able to build a system and recognize when its underlying assumptions are too weak.

That combination of theoretical understanding and practical ability is the kind of education I am seeking.

When I look back at the way I once viewed mathematics, I am surprised by how much my perspective has changed.

I used to see equations as problems I had to solve.

Now I often see them as descriptions of relationships.

I used to see formulas as things to remember.

Now I ask what they represent.

I used to see mathematics as a subject separate from programming.

Now I see it as one of the languages through which computing becomes possible.

And I used to think that understanding mathematics meant reaching the correct answer.

Now I think it also means understanding why the answer is correct.

That distinction captures how I want to learn in the future.

I do not want to memorize my way through difficult subjects.

I want to understand them deeply enough that I can use the ideas in unfamiliar situations.

I want to develop the ability to recognize patterns, construct arguments, challenge assumptions, and create new approaches.

Those are skills that will remain valuable regardless of how technology changes.

The technologies of tomorrow may be completely different from the tools I use today.

But mathematics will continue to describe relationships.

Algorithms will continue to require reasoning.

Uncertainty will continue to exist.

Optimization will continue to matter.

And people will continue to need ways to turn complex problems into understandable structures.

That is why I now see mathematics differently.

It is not merely a subject I study.

It is a way of seeing.

It teaches me to look beneath the surface.

It teaches me to search for structure.

It teaches me to question assumptions.

It teaches me that complexity can sometimes be understood by finding the right representation.

And it reminds me that a difficult problem is not necessarily an impossible one.

Sometimes the problem simply needs to be expressed differently.

That lesson has followed me from the classroom into programming, artificial intelligence, research, and everyday problem solving.

I began by trying to solve mathematical problems.

Eventually, I realized that mathematics was teaching me how to solve problems themselves.

That is the lesson I want to carry into college.

Not simply the ability to calculate.

Not simply the ability to code.

But the ability to look at something complicated and ask:

What is really happening here?

What structure am I missing?

And how can I understand it well enough to build something new?
