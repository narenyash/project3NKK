
Some of the most interesting problems are the ones nobody talks about.

I began noticing this while paying attention to ordinary routines around me. A shopkeeper writing an order in a notebook. A student searching through several messaging groups to find an assignment. A small business owner keeping track of payments manually. A customer waiting while someone searches through a stack of papers for information.

None of these situations seemed important enough to become headlines.

Yet they consumed time every day.

That made me curious.

Why do some problems become so familiar that people stop thinking of them as problems?

And could technology help solve them without making their lives more complicated?

This question has influenced the way I approach computer science.

When I first became interested in programming, I was fascinated by what computers could do. I wanted to learn programming languages, build applications, experiment with artificial intelligence, and understand how software worked.

Over time, however, my focus shifted.

I became less interested in building technology simply because it was possible and more interested in identifying situations where technology could genuinely help.

That change began with observation.

I started paying attention to how people around me actually used technology.

I noticed that access to technology does not necessarily mean effective use of technology.

A person may own a smartphone but still manage their business through handwritten records.

A student may have access to online learning but still struggle to organize information.

A small organization may have internet access but rely on disconnected spreadsheets because professional software is too expensive or complicated.

These situations made me realize that the digital divide is not only about whether someone owns a device.

It can also be about whether technology fits the person's circumstances.

This became particularly important to me as I started developing software.

As a developer, it is easy to think about what a system can do.

Users think about something else.

They think about whether it saves time.

Whether it is understandable.

Whether it works on their device.

Whether they can afford it.

Whether they can trust it.

Whether they need to learn something new just to use it.

These questions changed the way I evaluated my own projects.

Earlier, I considered a project successful when the code worked.

Later, I began asking whether the person using it would actually benefit.

That difference seems small.

It is not.

A technically impressive application can still be useless if it solves a problem nobody has.

A simple application can be extremely valuable if it removes a frustrating task that people perform every day.

This realization made me interested in small businesses.

Large companies often have access to specialized software, technical teams, consultants, and infrastructure.

Small businesses may not.

Yet small businesses handle complicated processes every day.

They manage customers, inventory, payments, orders, employees, suppliers, and records.

Many of these tasks involve repetitive work that software could simplify.

I became interested in building technology for these environments because the impact is easier to understand.

If an application saves someone several hours each week, the benefit is tangible.

If it reduces an error in an order, the benefit is tangible.

If it allows a business owner to find information in seconds instead of searching through notebooks, the benefit is tangible.

This perspective influenced one of my full-stack projects.

Rather than beginning with a technology stack, I began by thinking about the workflow.

What information enters the system?

Who needs it?

Where is it stored?

Who changes it?

What happens when something goes wrong?

What should the user see?

These questions led naturally to decisions about the frontend, backend, database, authentication, and APIs.

The technical architecture became a consequence of understanding the problem.

That was an important shift in my development as a programmer.

I learned that good engineering begins before the first line of code.

It begins with observation.

It begins with asking questions.

It begins with understanding constraints.

This lesson also influenced my interest in artificial intelligence.

AI is often presented as something capable of solving extremely complex problems.

But I became more interested in its quieter applications.

Could AI help a small business organize information?

Could it help a student understand difficult material?

Could it help someone interact with software without learning complicated interfaces?

Could it make information more accessible to someone who is not comfortable with technology?

These questions interested me because they connected advanced technology with ordinary situations.

I began to see AI not merely as a field of research but as an interface between people and complexity.

Modern software systems can be incredibly powerful, but that power often comes with complexity.

Users may need to navigate menus, understand settings, fill out forms, remember passwords, and learn unfamiliar workflows.

An intelligent system could potentially simplify some of these interactions.

Instead of forcing users to understand how a system is organized, perhaps the system could understand what the user is trying to accomplish.

That possibility fascinates me.

But it also raises an important question:

How much should technology adapt to people, and how much should people adapt to technology?

I believe good technology should do more of the adapting.

A system should not assume that every user is technically experienced.

It should not punish people for having limited digital knowledge.

It should not make simple tasks complicated merely because its internal architecture is complicated.

This is why accessibility has become an important part of how I think about software.

Accessibility is sometimes treated as a specialized feature.

I see it more broadly.

A system is more accessible when it communicates clearly.

It is more accessible when it works on ordinary devices.

It is more accessible when users do not need technical knowledge to understand it.

It is more accessible when errors are explained rather than simply displayed.

It is more accessible when the system considers different ways people interact with technology.

These principles benefit everyone.

A confusing application is not only difficult for a person with a disability.

It is difficult for anyone who is using it for the first time.

A slow website is not only inconvenient for someone with limited connectivity.

It is inconvenient for everyone.

Designing for constraints often creates better technology for all users.

This idea has also influenced my interest in India.

India presents an unusual technological environment.

It contains highly advanced technology alongside very traditional systems.

People can use digital payments while still keeping handwritten records.

A small business can communicate with customers through messaging applications while managing inventory manually.

Students can access sophisticated online resources while learning through very different educational environments.

These combinations create interesting engineering problems.

Technology cannot simply be copied from one environment and assumed to work everywhere.

It has to fit the people who use it.

That means understanding local habits, languages, connectivity, affordability, infrastructure, and trust.

I find these constraints exciting rather than limiting.

Constraints force creativity.

If a system cannot assume fast internet, it must be designed differently.

If users cannot afford expensive hardware, the software must work on devices they already have.

If users are uncomfortable with complex interfaces, the interaction must become simpler.

If people speak different languages, communication must become more flexible.

These are not merely technical challenges.

They are design challenges.

They are human challenges.

And solving them requires both engineering and empathy.

This is one reason I want to continue studying computer science.

I want stronger technical foundations so that I can build systems capable of operating under real-world constraints.

I want to understand distributed systems so that applications can remain reliable as they grow.

I want to study databases so that information can be stored and retrieved efficiently.

I want to study artificial intelligence so that systems can become more adaptive.

I want to study human-computer interaction so that those systems remain understandable.

And I want to learn from disciplines outside computer science because real-world problems rarely fit neatly inside one academic department.

My interest in technology has therefore become broader over time.

I still enjoy writing code.

I still enjoy the satisfaction of seeing an application work.

I still become absorbed in difficult technical problems.

But now I also enjoy asking whether the problem itself is worth solving.

That question has become one of my favorite parts of engineering.

There are thousands of things we could build.

We cannot build all of them.

So we have to decide which ones matter.

This requires judgment.

It requires listening.

It requires understanding people who may not have the same technical background as the developer.

It requires accepting that a developer's idea of an ideal product may not match the user's needs.

I have experienced this in my own projects.

Sometimes I have spent considerable time building a feature that seemed useful to me, only to realize that users cared more about something much simpler.

Those experiences taught me to pay attention.

Instead of asking people whether they like an idea, I try to understand what they actually do.

There is a difference.

Someone may say that an application is useful because the concept sounds good.

But their behavior may reveal that they rarely use it.

Understanding that difference is essential.

It is also one of the reasons I enjoy building prototypes.

A prototype turns an assumption into something that can be tested.

Before building, I can imagine that a feature will work.

After building, I can observe what actually happens.

Reality becomes feedback.

This process reminds me of scientific experimentation.

You start with a hypothesis.

You test it.

You examine the result.

Then you modify your understanding.

That cycle has become central to the way I learn.

It has also made me more comfortable with changing my mind.

I once believed that a good engineer should have strong opinions about how a system should be designed.

Now I think a good engineer should have strong principles but flexible solutions.

The principle might be that software should be reliable.

The implementation can change.

The principle might be that a system should be accessible.

The design can evolve.

The principle might be that technology should solve a real problem.

The specific technology can change completely.

This flexibility will be important in my future career.

The technologies I am learning today will not remain unchanged.

New programming frameworks will emerge.

AI models will become more capable.

New interfaces will appear.

Entire categories of software may disappear.

But the underlying need to understand problems will remain.

That is the skill I want to develop most deeply.

I want to become someone who can enter an unfamiliar environment, observe how people work, identify inefficiencies, understand the constraints, and determine whether technology can help.

I want to be able to move from a conversation to a problem statement, from a problem statement to a prototype, and from a prototype to a reliable system.

That process requires more than programming.

It requires communication.

It requires research.

It requires design.

It requires persistence.

It requires the willingness to listen when someone says, "This doesn't actually help me."

That sentence can be more valuable than praise.

It tells you where your assumptions were wrong.

It gives you a chance to improve.

This mindset has also influenced the kind of impact I hope to have.

I do not necessarily want to build the largest application.

I want to build something useful.

The scale can come later.

A system that helps one person may not seem revolutionary.

But if the same idea can eventually help thousands or millions of people, the initial simplicity becomes a strength rather than a weakness.

Many important technologies began by solving a specific problem.

The challenge is recognizing which small problems contain larger possibilities.

That is what I want to learn to do.

My long-term interests lie in artificial intelligence, software engineering, and technology for practical applications.

I am particularly interested in building systems that make complex processes easier for ordinary users.

I want to explore intelligent interfaces, automation, and AI-powered tools that can adapt to different users and environments.

But I also want to remain grounded in the problems themselves.

I do not want to start with the question, "Where can I use AI?"

I want to start with:

"What is difficult here?"

Then:

"Why is it difficult?"

Then:

"Who experiences the problem?"

And only after that:

"Could technology make it better?"

That sequence has become my philosophy of engineering.

Technology should be a means, not the starting point.

The starting point should be people.

The problems we learn to ignore are often the ones that deserve another look.

A repetitive task.

A confusing process.

A wasted hour.

An inaccessible service.

A system that works for experts but excludes beginners.

These problems may seem ordinary.

But ordinary problems affect millions of people precisely because they occur every day.

I want to learn how to notice them.

I want to learn how to investigate them.

And eventually, I want to learn how to build solutions that make them less difficult.

That is what computer science means to me now.

It is not simply the study of computers.

It is a way of thinking about problems.

It teaches me to break complexity into smaller pieces, identify patterns, design systems, test assumptions, and improve solutions.

But the most important lesson is one I learned outside the code:

Technology has value only when it improves something that matters.

That is the kind of engineer I want to become.

Someone who does not only ask, "Can we build it?"

Someone who also asks, "Will it help?"

And, most importantly:

"Who will it help?"
