
My interest in technology did not begin with a particular programming language or a specific career goal. It began with a simple habit: noticing problems and wondering whether they could be solved differently.

Sometimes the problems were small. Information was difficult to organise. A repetitive task consumed unnecessary time. A digital system was confusing to use. A process required several steps when fewer might have been possible. None of these problems seemed extraordinary individually, but they gradually changed the way I looked at the world around me.

I began to realise that engineering is not primarily about creating technology.

It is about understanding problems well enough to create something useful.

This distinction has become one of the most important principles in my academic journey.

When I first became interested in programming, I was fascinated by the ability to make a computer perform an action through instructions. Writing a small program and seeing it work created a sense of accomplishment. I began exploring different programming concepts and experimenting with simple applications.

At that stage, I was primarily focused on what I could build.

Over time, my attention shifted toward why I should build it.

A technically impressive application is not necessarily useful.

A sophisticated algorithm may solve a problem that nobody actually has.

A system with dozens of features may be less effective than a simple system that performs one important task well.

This realisation changed how I approach projects.

Instead of starting with a technology, I increasingly try to start with a problem.

Who experiences it?

Why does it exist?

How is it currently being handled?

What makes the existing process difficult?

What would improvement look like?

Only after understanding these questions should technology become part of the discussion.

This way of thinking has made software development more meaningful to me.

For example, when designing an application, it is tempting to immediately think about screens, buttons, databases, and programming frameworks.

But these technical components are only tools.

The real question is what the user needs.

If the user wants to complete a task quickly, a complicated interface may be a poor solution even if it is technically sophisticated.

If the user has limited connectivity, an application that requires constant internet access may not be appropriate.

If the user is unfamiliar with technology, complicated terminology may create unnecessary barriers.

These considerations demonstrate why understanding the problem must come before implementation.

This principle has also influenced my interest in artificial intelligence.

AI is an exceptionally powerful technology, but its existence does not automatically justify its use.

It is possible to add AI to a system simply because AI is currently popular.

However, a responsible engineer should ask whether AI actually improves the outcome.

If a simple rule can solve the problem reliably, a complex model may be unnecessary.

If the available data is poor, machine learning may produce unreliable results.

If a decision requires human judgment, full automation may be inappropriate.

Therefore, choosing not to use AI can sometimes be a better engineering decision than using it.

This perspective has made me more interested in understanding the fundamentals behind technology.

I want to know not only how to implement an algorithm, but also when that algorithm is appropriate.

I want to know not only how to train a model, but also whether the data is sufficient.

I want to know not only how to deploy software, but also whether users can realistically use it.

Engineering decisions should be based on requirements rather than excitement alone.

This approach is especially important in India.

The country contains enormous diversity in geography, language, infrastructure, income, education, and access to technology.

A solution designed for a major metropolitan environment may not work equally well in a smaller town or rural setting.

A platform requiring high-speed connectivity may be inconvenient for users with limited internet access.

An application available only in one language may exclude large groups of people.

A system requiring expensive hardware may be inaccessible to the communities that need it most.

Therefore, solving problems in India often requires thinking about constraints from the beginning.

This is one of the aspects of engineering that interests me most.

How can we create solutions that are not only technically possible but also practical?

A practical solution must consider cost.

It must consider infrastructure.

It must consider maintenance.

It must consider user behaviour.

It must consider scalability.

And it must consider accessibility.

These factors may not appear in a programming textbook, but they determine whether technology succeeds in the real world.

Education is one area where I believe this approach can have significant value.

Students have different learning styles, educational backgrounds, and levels of access to resources.

Technology can provide additional support through digital learning platforms, adaptive systems, intelligent tutoring tools, and personalised educational content.

But the objective should not simply be to make education more technological.

The objective should be to make learning more effective and accessible.

If an AI system gives students immediate answers without encouraging them to understand the reasoning, it may reduce learning.

A better system might provide hints, explanations, examples, and feedback.

The technology should support the learning process rather than replace it.

This is an example of how starting with the problem changes the design.

The problem is not that students lack access to answers.

The problem may be that they lack understanding, guidance, practice, or feedback.

Once the problem is defined correctly, the appropriate technological solution becomes clearer.

Agriculture provides another example.

Farmers face challenges involving weather, water, soil, crop health, market conditions, and resource availability.

Technology can potentially help through sensors, satellite imagery, weather information, machine learning, and digital platforms.

However, an agricultural application should not be designed simply because these technologies are available.

The system must answer a practical question.

What decision will the farmer make using this information?

How frequently is the information needed?

Can the farmer access the system?

Is the cost reasonable?

Can the information be trusted?

Will the system work under local conditions?

These questions determine whether the technology is useful.

The same principle applies to healthcare.

Artificial intelligence can assist with medical imaging, data management, monitoring, and decision support.

But healthcare decisions can have serious consequences.

Therefore, accuracy alone is not enough.

A system must be evaluated carefully.

Its limitations must be understood.

Medical professionals must be involved.

Patient information must be protected.

The technology should support clinical expertise rather than create unjustified confidence in automated predictions.

This illustrates a broader principle I want to follow throughout my career.

The importance of a technology depends on the importance of the problem it solves and the quality of the solution.

Technology should be a means rather than the final objective.

Environmental challenges provide another example.

India faces issues involving air pollution, water management, waste, biodiversity, and climate-related risks.

Software and AI can help analyse environmental data, monitor changes, optimise resources, and support decision-making.

However, environmental problems are not simply data problems.

They involve behaviour, infrastructure, economics, policy, and communities.

Technology can contribute to these systems, but it cannot replace them.

Understanding this prevents unrealistic expectations.

I want to become an engineer who understands both the capabilities and limitations of technology.

This is why I am increasingly interested in systems thinking.

A system is rarely isolated.

Changing one component can influence another.

A new application may change user behaviour.

A new automated process may affect employment.

A new data-collection system may create privacy concerns.

A new energy technology may affect resource consumption.

Therefore, engineers need to think beyond the immediate function of a product.

They need to consider the larger environment in which it operates.

These experiences can teach me how to translate real-world needs into technical requirements.

I believe that ability will be one of the most important skills in my future career.

A person may be an excellent programmer but still struggle to identify the right problem.

Another person may understand a social problem deeply but lack the technical knowledge required to develop a solution.

The most effective teams combine both perspectives.

I want to become someone who can contribute to that connection.

This is also why communication matters.

Before building a solution, an engineer must communicate with users and stakeholders.

They must ask questions.

They must listen.

They must clarify expectations.

They must identify constraints.

They must understand what success means to the people who will use the system.

Only then can development begin with confidence.

I want to develop this ability through practical projects and research.

I also want to learn from failure.

Sometimes a project may appear useful during development but fail when tested by actual users.

That failure can be valuable.

It may reveal that the original problem was misunderstood.

It may show that a feature is unnecessary.

It may reveal an unexpected user behaviour.

It may demonstrate that the proposed solution is too expensive or complicated.

Rather than treating such results as wasted effort, I want to treat them as information.

The purpose of engineering is not to defend an idea.

It is to improve the solution.

This mindset encourages iteration.

Identify the problem.

Design a possible solution.

Build a prototype.

Test it.

Collect feedback.

Analyse the results.

Improve the design.

Repeat.

This cycle is at the heart of many successful technological products.

I want to develop comfort with this process.

My interest in artificial intelligence has made iteration particularly important.

Machine learning models often require experimentation.

A first model may not perform well.

Changing the dataset may improve results.

Changing the features may produce a different outcome.

Another architecture may perform better.

A different evaluation method may reveal weaknesses.

This process requires patience.

It also requires intellectual honesty.

If an approach does not work, I should not try to hide the result.

I should understand why.

Research progresses when unsuccessful approaches are analysed rather than ignored.

I want to work on questions where the answer is uncertain.

I want to investigate possible solutions.

Users can then provide feedback that creates new research questions.

This cycle can produce meaningful innovation.

My long-term goal is to work at this intersection.

I want to develop intelligent software systems that address real problems.

I am interested in applications of AI, but I want the application to determine the technology rather than the reverse.

If AI provides a genuine advantage, I want to use it.

If a simpler approach is better, I want to choose simplicity.

If human judgment is necessary, I want to preserve it.

This philosophy can help prevent unnecessary complexity.

Simplicity is often underestimated in engineering.

A system does not become better simply because it contains more features.

Every additional component can introduce complexity.

Complexity can create maintenance problems.

It can increase costs.

It can create new failure points.

It can make systems harder to understand.

Therefore, the best solution may sometimes be the simplest one that reliably addresses the problem.

I want to develop this judgment.

I also want to consider scalability.

A solution may work perfectly for ten users and fail for ten million.

As systems grow, databases, infrastructure, security, performance, and cost become important.

This is another reason I want to study software architecture and system design.

I want to understand how useful ideas can become reliable systems at larger scales.

India's large population creates particularly interesting opportunities for this kind of engineering.

A successful digital solution can potentially reach millions of users.

This creates enormous potential for impact.

But it also increases responsibility.

A small error can affect many people.

A poorly designed interface can exclude large groups.

A security vulnerability can have serious consequences.

Therefore, scale requires careful engineering.

I want to learn how to build systems that are reliable not only in demonstrations but also under real-world conditions.

Accessibility is another part of this responsibility.

Technology should not assume that every user has the same abilities or resources.

Some users may have disabilities.

Some may use older devices.

Some may have limited digital literacy.

Some may prefer regional languages.

Some may have intermittent connectivity.

These factors should influence design.

I want to develop technology that considers such differences rather than treating them as unusual exceptions.

This is one reason human-centred design interests me.

A good system should adapt to people rather than forcing everyone to adapt to the system.

Artificial intelligence may eventually provide more personalised interfaces and services.

But personalisation should be designed responsibly.

The system needs appropriate data.

Users should understand important aspects of how their information is used.

Privacy must be considered.

Bias must be evaluated.

The benefits should justify the complexity.

Again, the problem must guide the solution.

This principle also applies to sustainability.

Technology itself consumes resources.

Data centres require energy.

Hardware requires materials.

Electronic devices eventually become waste.

Therefore, engineers should consider environmental consequences.

A computationally expensive solution may not always be preferable to a smaller and more efficient one.

An AI system should be evaluated not only for accuracy but, where relevant, for computational cost and energy use.

This is another example of why engineering requires trade-offs.

A solution must balance performance, cost, efficiency, reliability, and user needs.

There is rarely one perfect answer.

The engineer's role is to understand the requirements and make informed decisions.

I want to develop that ability.
