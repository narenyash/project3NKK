
Leadership is often associated with authority, decision-making, and the ability to direct others. My understanding of leadership has developed differently. Through academic projects, technical competitions, teamwork, and collaborative learning, I have come to believe that leadership is less about being the person with all the answers and more about helping a group move toward a common objective.

This understanding has influenced the kind of student, engineer, and professional I want to become.

My early understanding of teamwork was relatively simple. If several people were assigned a project, each person could complete a particular part and the work could then be combined. This approach appeared efficient, but practical experience showed me that successful teamwork requires much more than dividing tasks.

People approach problems differently.

They communicate differently.

They work at different speeds.

They may have different levels of technical knowledge.

They may disagree about priorities.

A project succeeds when these differences are managed constructively.

This is where I began to understand leadership.

A leader does not necessarily need to be the most technically advanced person in the group.

A leader needs to understand the strengths of different people and create conditions in which those strengths can contribute to the common goal.

This lesson became particularly relevant during technical projects.

In software development, a project may involve frontend development, backend systems, database design, artificial intelligence, testing, documentation, and presentation. One person may not be equally strong in all these areas.

Instead of expecting everyone to perform the same tasks, a team can use its diversity as an advantage.

One member may be highly skilled in programming.

Another may have stronger design instincts.

Another may communicate well with users.

Another may be good at research.

Another may identify problems during testing.

Effective leadership recognises these differences rather than trying to eliminate them.

I have learned that assigning responsibilities according to strengths can improve both efficiency and confidence.

A person who is trusted with an area they understand well is more likely to contribute confidently.

At the same time, team members should have opportunities to learn outside their existing strengths.

Leadership therefore involves both delegation and development.

A leader should distribute responsibilities, but should also encourage people to grow.

This is particularly important for students.

A project should not simply produce a final submission.

It should produce learning.

If one person performs all the important tasks while everyone else watches, the team may complete the project but fail to create a meaningful learning experience.

I therefore believe that good teamwork should allow every member to contribute.

This does not mean that every person must perform identical tasks.

It means that every person should understand how their work contributes to the larger objective.

Communication is essential for this.

A team can have talented individuals and still fail if they do not communicate effectively.

One person may change a component without informing another.

A requirement may be misunderstood.

A deadline may be assumed rather than confirmed.

A technical limitation may remain undisclosed.

These problems can create unnecessary delays.

I have learned that regular communication can prevent many of them.

Simple practices such as discussing progress, identifying blockers, documenting decisions, and clarifying responsibilities can make a significant difference.

This may seem less exciting than writing code or designing a system, but it is essential to successful engineering.

Leadership therefore begins with communication.

It also requires listening.

A leader who speaks constantly but does not listen can miss important information.

A teammate may identify a technical problem.

Another may notice that the planned approach is unrealistic.

A quieter member may have an idea that has not yet been considered.

If the leader dominates every discussion, these contributions may never emerge.

I want to develop the habit of listening carefully before making decisions.

This does not mean accepting every suggestion.

It means giving ideas enough attention to evaluate them fairly.

A strong decision should be based on reasoning and evidence rather than simply on who proposed the idea.

This principle becomes particularly important when disagreements occur.

Disagreement is natural in a team.

Two people may have different technical approaches.

One person may prioritise speed.

Another may prioritise reliability.

One may prefer simplicity.

Another may prefer a more flexible architecture.

These disagreements do not necessarily indicate that the team is failing.

They can indicate that important trade-offs are being considered.

The goal should be to move from personal preferences toward objective criteria.

What does the project require?

Which approach is more reliable?

Which is easier to maintain?

What does the user need?

What evidence supports the decision?

This approach can turn disagreement into productive discussion.

I want to develop this style of leadership.

I do not want to win arguments.

I want the team to make better decisions.

This distinction is important.

Leadership is not about proving that one person is correct.

It is about helping the group reach the best possible outcome.

Another important part of leadership is responsibility.

When a project faces difficulties, it can be tempting to identify who is responsible for the problem.

However, blame rarely solves the immediate issue.

A better approach is to determine what happened, identify what needs to be corrected, and decide how the team can prevent a similar problem in the future.

This does not mean ignoring accountability.

People should take responsibility for their work.

But accountability should lead to improvement rather than fear.

I want to contribute to team environments where people can communicate problems early.

If someone discovers a mistake, they should feel able to report it.

If someone needs help, they should be able to ask.

If a deadline is at risk, the team should know early enough to respond.

This creates trust.

Trust is one of the foundations of effective teamwork.

I have also learned that leadership becomes especially important under pressure.

During competitions or deadlines, teams have limited time.

Stress can make communication difficult.

People may become impatient.

Decisions may need to be made quickly.

A leader must help the team remain focused.

This does not mean ignoring stress.

It means preventing stress from destroying collaboration.

A calm approach can make a significant difference.

Identify the immediate problem.

Determine what can be completed.

Prioritise essential tasks.

Communicate clearly.

Continue.

This process can keep a team moving even when the original plan has changed.

I want to develop this ability because engineering careers often involve deadlines and uncertainty.

Projects may not always proceed according to plan.

Requirements can change.

Technical issues can appear unexpectedly.

Clients or users may provide new information.

Teams need to adapt.

Leadership helps coordinate that adaptation.

I also believe leadership should involve learning from others.

A person does not become a leader simply because they have been given a leadership title.

Leadership is earned through reliability.

If teammates know that a person listens, communicates clearly, accepts responsibility, and supports others, they are more likely to trust that person.

Trust cannot be demanded.

It has to be built through consistent behaviour.

This is why I do not think leadership begins when someone receives a formal position.

It begins with everyday actions.

Helping a teammate understand a difficult concept.

Taking responsibility for an unfinished task.

Communicating a problem early.

Giving credit to someone who contributed.

Listening when another person has a concern.

Offering support when a teammate is struggling.

These actions create the foundation for leadership.

My experience with technical learning has also shown me that leadership can involve teaching.

When I help someone understand programming, I am not simply transferring information.

I am helping them become more independent.

This has changed how I think about leadership.

A leader should not try to make everyone dependent on them.

The goal should be to make the team stronger.

If a leader is the only person who understands an important part of the project, the team becomes vulnerable.

If knowledge is shared, the team becomes more resilient.

This principle is especially important in software engineering.

Documentation, code reviews, knowledge sharing, and mentoring can reduce dependence on individual team members.

I want to encourage this approach in my future teams.

I also want to develop leadership through service.

Helping others does not always involve formal authority.

A student can organise a study group.

A developer can help a teammate debug a problem.

A senior engineer can mentor a junior colleague.

A researcher can guide a new student through an unfamiliar method.

These actions improve the community around them.

I want leadership to be connected to contribution rather than status.

This is particularly meaningful to me because I believe education should create opportunities for shared growth.

A university is not simply a place where individuals compete for grades.

It can also be a community in which students learn from one another.

I want to participate actively in that community.

I hope to join technical organisations, research groups, innovation initiatives, and student projects.

I want to contribute ideas.

I also want to help others develop theirs.

I believe this will strengthen my own understanding.

Working with people from different backgrounds will also challenge my assumptions.

A team member may approach a problem differently.

Instead of immediately judging the approach as inefficient, I want to understand why they chose it.

Perhaps they have identified a requirement I overlooked.

Perhaps they have experience with a different technology.

Perhaps their approach works better for a particular user.

Different perspectives can improve decisions.

This connects leadership with openness.

A leader should not become so attached to their own ideas that they cannot recognise better alternatives.

I want to remain willing to change my mind when evidence supports a different approach.

This is particularly important in technical fields.

Technology changes.

Best practices evolve.

Research produces new knowledge.

The approach that was correct yesterday may not be appropriate tomorrow.

A leader must therefore remain a learner.

I want to develop this quality throughout my career.

My interest in artificial intelligence has reinforced the importance of collaborative leadership.

AI projects often involve multiple areas of expertise.

Machine learning researchers may focus on models.

Data engineers may focus on datasets and pipelines.

Software engineers may focus on deployment.

Domain experts may define the real-world problem.

Designers may focus on user interaction.

Ethics and policy specialists may consider broader consequences.

No single person can provide all of these perspectives.

Effective leadership must bring them together.

I want to become capable of working across these boundaries.

I want to understand enough about different roles to communicate effectively with each of them.

At the same time, I want to respect expertise.

Leadership does not mean pretending to know everything.

A strong leader should be able to say, "You understand this area better than I do. What do you recommend?"

That question can produce better decisions than pretending to have an answer.

I believe this humility is essential.

It is particularly important for young professionals because technical knowledge is always incomplete.

There will always be someone with more experience.

There will always be another field to learn.

There will always be problems outside one's expertise.

Recognising this creates opportunities for collaboration.

Leadership also requires ethical responsibility.

A leader's decisions can affect an entire team.

If a leader encourages cutting corners, others may follow.

If a leader ignores quality issues, the final product may suffer.

If a leader takes credit for another person's contribution, trust can disappear.

Therefore, leadership must be grounded in integrity.

I want to lead by example.

If quality matters, I should demonstrate attention to quality.

If honesty matters, I should communicate honestly.

If teamwork matters, I should participate in the work.

If learning matters, I should continue learning myself.

People are more likely to trust behaviours they can observe.

This is another reason I believe leadership is a responsibility rather than a privilege.

The person leading a team has an obligation to protect the team's objectives and support its members.

My long-term goal is to work in artificial intelligence and software engineering.

Eventually, I would like to lead technical projects where I can combine engineering knowledge with teamwork and problem solving.

However, I do not expect to become an effective leader immediately.

Leadership is a skill that develops through experience.

I want to begin by becoming a reliable team member.

I want to learn how projects operate.

I want to understand different roles.

I want to experience both successful and unsuccessful collaborations.

I want to learn how teams handle disagreement.

I want to learn how decisions are made under pressure.

Over time, these experiences can prepare me for greater responsibility.

I believe this gradual development is important.

A leadership position should not be pursued simply for its title.

It should be accepted when a person is prepared to take responsibility for the outcome.

I want to earn that responsibility through competence and trust.

My academic goals will give me many opportunities to develop these qualities.

Group assignments can teach coordination.

Research projects can teach intellectual collaboration.

Technical competitions can teach decision-making under time constraints.

Student organisations can teach planning and communication.

Mentoring can teach patience.

Each experience can contribute to leadership development.

I want to approach these opportunities intentionally.

I also want to learn from leaders around me.

Professors, mentors, senior students, and professionals can demonstrate different leadership styles.

Some may lead through technical expertise.

Some may lead through communication.

Some may lead through empathy.

Some may lead through vision.

Observing these differences can help me understand which qualities are effective in different situations.

I do not want to copy one leadership style completely.

I want to develop a style that is appropriate to my own personality and values.

For me, that style would be based on listening, responsibility, clarity, and collaboration.

I want people working with me to feel that their ideas matter.

I want them to know what the team is trying to achieve.

I want responsibilities to be clear.

I want problems to be communicated honestly.

And I want success to be shared.

One of the most important lessons I have learned is that a team should celebrate collective achievement rather than individual visibility.

A project may have one person presenting the final result, but many people may have contributed behind the scenes.

The person who designed the database.

The person who tested the application.

The person who researched the problem.

The person who prepared the documentation.

The person who identified a critical bug.

All of these contributions matter.

A leader should recognise them.

Recognition builds motivation and trust.

I want to make this part of my future professional behaviour.

Leadership also involves helping people through difficulties.

A teammate who is struggling may not need criticism.

They may need clarification, additional time, or another approach.

A leader should distinguish between lack of effort and genuine difficulty.

Sometimes people perform below their ability because they do not understand what is expected.

Clear communication can solve the problem.

Sometimes they may need guidance.

Sometimes responsibilities need to be redistributed.

The goal should be to help the team succeed while maintaining accountability.

This balance requires judgment.

I want to develop that judgment through experience.

Engineering is rarely an individual activity.

Complex systems require collaboration.

Large problems require different perspectives.

Therefore, technical competence and leadership cannot be completely separated.

A strong engineer should know how to contribute to a team.

A strong technical leader should understand the work deeply enough to make informed decisions.
