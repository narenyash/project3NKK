
The first instinct when a program fails is to look for the bug.

I open the code.

I check the error message.

I trace the execution.

I change something.

I run it again.

Sometimes the problem disappears.

Sometimes it becomes worse.

And sometimes I discover that the line I thought was responsible had nothing to do with the actual problem.

Over time, I realized that debugging software taught me something about myself.

I often make the same mistake when dealing with personal challenges.

I look for the most obvious explanation.

I assume I know what went wrong.

I try to fix the visible problem.

Then I discover that the real cause is somewhere deeper.

That realization has changed the way I think about failure.

I used to see failure as evidence that something had gone wrong.

Now I increasingly see it as information.

A failed program tells me something about my assumptions.

A failed experiment tells me something about my hypothesis.

A difficult academic subject tells me something about what I have not yet understood.

A project that does not work as expected tells me that my original model of the problem was incomplete.

Failure does not automatically tell me the solution.

But it gives me evidence.

And evidence is useful.

My relationship with failure changed gradually through programming.

When I first started coding, errors were frustrating.

I would see a long message on the screen and immediately feel that I had done something fundamentally wrong.

Sometimes I would search for the exact error online and copy a solution without fully understanding it.

The immediate problem would disappear.

But the next time I encountered something similar, I would be stuck again.

Eventually, I realized that fixing an error and understanding an error were different things.

The first solves the immediate problem.

The second improves me.

That distinction became important.

I started reading error messages more carefully.

Instead of asking only, "How do I remove this error?" I began asking:

"What is the program actually telling me?"

"What assumption did I make?"

"At what point did the behavior change?"

"What information can I gather before changing anything?"

This made debugging slower at first.

It also made me better.

I began learning that the first visible symptom is not necessarily the cause.

A screen may fail to load because of a backend problem.

The backend problem may come from a database query.

The query may fail because the input is malformed.

The input may be malformed because the frontend and backend disagree about the data format.

The problem is not where the user sees it.

It is somewhere earlier in the chain.

This idea became a useful metaphor for life.

Sometimes the visible problem is not the real problem.

If I am struggling with a subject, the issue may not be that the subject is too difficult.

I may be missing a prerequisite.

If I am struggling to complete a project, the issue may not be a lack of motivation.

The scope may be unrealistic.

If I am frustrated with a result, the issue may not be the result itself.

My expectations may have been wrong.

Learning to search for root causes has made me more honest with myself.

It is easy to blame circumstances.

Sometimes circumstances really are responsible.

But sometimes the more uncomfortable explanation is that I approached the problem incorrectly.

Accepting that possibility is useful because it gives me something I can change.

This became especially clear when I started working on more ambitious technical projects.

An idea can look simple when described in one sentence.

"Build an AI system."

"Create a mobile application."

"Automate this process."

"Develop a recommendation system."

The sentence hides the complexity.

Once development begins, dozens of smaller problems appear.

Data must be collected.

Requirements must be defined.

Interfaces must be designed.

APIs must communicate.

Errors must be handled.

Models must be evaluated.

Security must be considered.

The system must be tested.

The first version almost never works exactly as imagined.

Earlier, I interpreted that as failure.

Now I see it as the normal beginning of engineering.

The first version is a hypothesis.

The second version is a response to evidence.

The third version reflects what I learned from the second.

The process continues.

This has made me less emotionally attached to my first ideas.

That is an important change.

An idea can feel personal when you have spent time developing it.

Criticism can therefore feel like criticism of you.

But a project is not your identity.

An idea is not your worth.

A design can be changed without invalidating the effort that created it.

I have learned to separate myself from my work enough to improve it honestly.

This is particularly important when working with other people.

A teammate may point out a problem in something I built.

My first reaction may be defensive.

Why did they not understand what I intended?

Then I remind myself that the user will not see my intentions either.

If the design is confusing, explaining what I meant does not solve the problem.

The design needs to change.

That realization has helped me become more receptive to feedback.

Feedback is not always comfortable.

But discomfort can be useful.

It often points toward something I need to examine.

I have also learned that not every failure should be fixed in the same way.

Sometimes the problem is a small bug.

Fix the bug.

Sometimes the architecture is wrong.

Redesign the system.

Sometimes the requirements are wrong.

Return to the problem.

Sometimes the entire idea is not useful.

Stop.

That last possibility is difficult.

People naturally want their work to have been worthwhile.

But continuing a bad idea simply because time has already been invested creates an even larger cost.

I have learned that stopping can be a form of progress.

If an experiment demonstrates that an approach does not work, the result is still useful.

It narrows the search.

It prevents repeating the same mistake.

It gives information for the next attempt.

This is one of the reasons I enjoy scientific and technical experimentation.

Failure can be structured.

You can change one variable.

Observe the result.

Compare outcomes.

Build a better hypothesis.

The process transforms uncertainty into learning.

That approach has influenced my interest in machine learning.

Training a model is not simply a matter of selecting an architecture and pressing a button.

A model can fail for many reasons.

The dataset may be too small.

The labels may be inconsistent.

The features may be inadequate.

The model may overfit.

The evaluation may be misleading.

The problem itself may be poorly defined.

When a model performs badly, the solution is not always "use a bigger model."

Sometimes the correct response is to examine the data.

Sometimes the evaluation metric is wrong.

Sometimes the task needs to be reformulated.

This reinforced a principle I now try to remember:

Do not make the solution more complicated before understanding the problem.

A larger model is not automatically a better solution.

More features are not automatically better.

More code is not automatically better.

More technology is not automatically better.

Sometimes improvement comes from removing something.

Simplifying a workflow.

Cleaning a dataset.

Changing a requirement.

Improving an explanation.

Removing an unnecessary dependency.

This lesson extends beyond engineering.

When something in my life is not working, I try to ask whether I am adding complexity instead of addressing the actual issue.

Sometimes the answer is yes.

This way of thinking has made me more reflective.

I have started treating mistakes as opportunities to inspect my assumptions.

Why did I expect this result?

What information did I ignore?

What did I misunderstand?

What would I do differently if I started again?

The last question is particularly valuable.

It turns regret into planning.

I cannot change what happened.

I can change what I learn from it.

That perspective has helped me recover from disappointments.

There have been projects that did not work.

There have been ideas that looked better on paper than they did in reality.

There have been times when I underestimated the difficulty of something.

There have been moments when I wished I had prepared more.

It would be easy to remember these experiences only as failures.

Instead, I try to remember the lesson each one produced.

A project taught me to define scope.

A technical problem taught me to read documentation carefully.

A team challenge taught me to communicate earlier.

A failed experiment taught me to question my assumptions.

A difficult subject taught me to return to fundamentals.

The pattern is clear.

The mistake is temporary.

The lesson can remain.

This has changed the way I think about confidence.

I used to believe confidence meant feeling certain.

Now I think real confidence can mean knowing that you can recover when you are uncertain.

If I encounter a technology I do not understand, I do not need to pretend that I know it.

I can learn.

If a project fails, I do not need to conclude that I cannot build it.

I can investigate why.

If someone gives me criticism, I do not need to defend every decision.

I can determine whether they are right.

This kind of confidence is quieter.

It is not based on always being correct.

It is based on being willing to learn.

That is the kind of confidence I want to bring into college.

University will expose me to problems that are harder than the ones I have encountered so far.

I will probably fail at things.

I will encounter mathematical concepts that take time to understand.

I will build systems that do not work.

I will make incorrect assumptions.

I may conduct experiments that produce disappointing results.

I may pursue ideas that turn out not to be useful.

I do not want to interpret these experiences as evidence that I do not belong.

I want to interpret them as part of becoming better.

The most important skill I can develop is not avoiding every mistake.

It is becoming better at responding to mistakes.

That means learning how to diagnose problems.

It means asking for help when necessary.

It means accepting criticism.

It means changing direction when evidence demands it.

It means trying again without repeating the same mistake.

These are engineering skills.

They are also life skills.

My interest in artificial intelligence has made this even more relevant.

AI systems can fail in ways that are difficult to predict.

A model can generate an incorrect answer.

A vision system can misclassify an image.

An automated agent can take an unintended action.

A recommendation system can learn an undesirable pattern.

The response should not simply be to hope that the system never fails.

We need systems designed to handle failure.

They need monitoring.

They need validation.

They need constraints.

They need recovery mechanisms.

They need human oversight when appropriate.

This has influenced the kind of systems I want to build.

I am interested not only in what a system can do when everything goes right.

I am interested in what happens when something goes wrong.

That question often reveals more about the quality of an architecture than a successful demonstration does.

A robust system is not one that never encounters errors.

It is one that can detect, contain, and recover from them.

I want to develop that mindset as an engineer.

I also want to apply it to research.

A research project should not be designed only to produce a desired result.

It should be designed so that unexpected results can teach us something.

That requires careful experimental design.

It requires documenting assumptions.

It requires reproducibility.

It requires honesty about limitations.

Most importantly, it requires resisting the temptation to hide inconvenient results.

If the evidence contradicts my hypothesis, I want to know.

The goal is not to prove myself right.

The goal is to understand what is true.

That principle has become increasingly important to me.

I want my work to be guided by evidence rather than ego.

I know this is easier to say than to practice.

People naturally become attached to their ideas.

I do too.

But recognizing that tendency makes it easier to question it.

When I feel strongly about a solution, I can deliberately ask:

"What evidence would convince me that I am wrong?"

That question is uncomfortable.

It is also powerful.

It creates room for learning.

This is the attitude I want to carry into college classrooms, laboratories, and research groups.

I want to be willing to ask questions that reveal what I do not understand.

I want to be willing to submit ideas to criticism.

I want to be willing to rebuild something from the beginning if the evidence shows that the original approach was wrong.

And I want to become comfortable with the fact that progress can look messy.

The finished application hides the bugs that came before it.

The successful research paper hides the experiments that failed.

The polished presentation hides the prototypes that were discarded.

The final result is clean.

The process is not.

I have learned to value the process.

That is perhaps what debugging has taught me most.

The goal is not to write code that never needs debugging.

The goal is to become good at debugging.

The same principle applies to learning.

The goal is not to become someone who never makes mistakes.

The goal is to become someone who can examine mistakes honestly and improve.

I still get frustrated when something fails.

I still wish some problems would solve themselves.

I still sometimes make the same mistake twice.

But I recover faster.

I ask better questions.

I look for causes instead of symptoms.

And I try to remember that failure is information.

A bug tells me something.

A wrong answer tells me something.

A failed experiment tells me something.

A difficult conversation tells me something.

A rejected idea tells me something.

The important question is whether I am willing to listen.

My future in computer science will involve increasingly complex systems.

I want to work in artificial intelligence and software engineering.

I want to build systems that are useful, reliable, and responsible.

To do that, I will need technical knowledge.

But I will also need resilience.

I will need patience.

I will need humility.

And I will need the willingness to admit when something is not working.

That is why I no longer think of debugging as simply a programming task.

It is a way of thinking.

Observe the behavior.

Identify the symptom.

Question the assumptions.

Trace the cause.

Test a hypothesis.

Change one thing.

Run the experiment again.

Learn from the result.

Repeat.

That process has helped me become a better programmer.

More importantly, it is helping me become a better learner.

I am still debugging myself.

There are assumptions I have not discovered yet.

There are habits I need to improve.

There are weaknesses I have not fully understood.

There are problems I will only recognize after encountering them.

I do not expect that process to end.

And perhaps I should not want it to.

Because every time I discover something that needs improvement, I also discover an opportunity to become better.

The best engineers are not people whose systems never fail.

They are people who know how to find the failure, understand its cause, and build something stronger afterward.

I want to become that kind of engineer.

And, beyond engineering, that is the kind of person I want to become.
