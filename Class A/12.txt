
I have never been particularly good at ignoring things that seem slightly out of place.

A process that takes longer than it should.

A button that is difficult to find.

A queue that moves slowly even though only a few people are waiting.

A repeated task that everyone performs manually.

A pattern that appears again and again without anyone asking why.

Most people learn to accept these things as part of everyday life.

I tend to question them.

Why does it work this way?

Could it be simpler?

Is there a reason it has always been done like this?

And, sometimes, could I build something to improve it?

This habit of noticing has become one of the most important parts of who I am as a student.

It did not begin as a deliberate skill.

I simply became curious about how things worked.

When I was younger, I would take apart simple ideas mentally even when I had no idea how to explain what I was doing. I wanted to know why a machine responded to one action but not another. I wanted to understand why some systems felt easy to use while others felt unnecessarily complicated.

Eventually, computers gave me a way to investigate those questions.

Programming turned observation into experimentation.

If I noticed a problem, I could try to reproduce it.

If I had an idea for a solution, I could build a small prototype.

If the prototype failed, I could change it.

The process gave my curiosity a practical direction.

One of the first things I learned was that not every problem needs a complicated solution.

This sounds obvious, but it is surprisingly difficult to remember when you are interested in technology.

When you know about machine learning, databases, APIs, cloud platforms, and modern frameworks, it is tempting to use them everywhere.

A simple problem can suddenly become an opportunity to build an enormous system.

But complexity is not automatically sophistication.

Sometimes the best solution is a small change to an existing process.

Sometimes a clear interface is more valuable than an intelligent one.

Sometimes automation is unnecessary.

The important question is not what technology can be used.

It is what technology is actually needed.

That lesson has influenced the way I approach projects.

Before building, I try to understand the process.

Who performs the task?

How often?

What information do they need?

Where does the process become slow?

What mistakes occur?

What happens when something goes wrong?

These questions often reveal that the obvious problem is not the real problem.

A user might say that they need a faster application.

After observing them, I might discover that the real issue is that they have to enter the same information repeatedly.

Someone might ask for a new feature when what they actually need is a simpler workflow.

A business might want a sophisticated dashboard when what it really needs is reliable access to three pieces of information.

Observation changes the problem.

This became particularly important when I started building software for practical use.

As a developer, I naturally think in terms of systems.

I see screens, components, APIs, databases, authentication, and logic.

Users do not.

They see tasks.

They see information.

They see whether something is easy or difficult.

This difference between the developer's perspective and the user's perspective has taught me to pay closer attention.

I began watching how people actually interact with technology rather than assuming they would use it the way I intended.

Sometimes the most valuable feedback is not something a person says.

It is what they do.

If someone repeatedly clicks the wrong button, the interface may be unclear.

If they stop halfway through a process, the task may be too complicated.

If they ask the same question repeatedly, the information may not be presented clearly.

These observations taught me that design is a form of listening.

You do not always need someone to tell you what is wrong.

Sometimes their behavior tells you.

This way of thinking has also influenced my interest in artificial intelligence.

AI systems are often evaluated using numbers.

Accuracy.

Precision.

Recall.

Latency.

Benchmark scores.

These measurements are important.

But I have learned that numbers do not automatically explain usefulness.

A model can perform well on a benchmark and still fail in the environment where it is supposed to operate.

A chatbot can produce fluent responses and still misunderstand a user's real intention.

A recommendation system can make accurate predictions but recommend things the user does not actually want.

The gap between performance and usefulness interests me.

It reminds me of the difference between a system that technically works and one that actually helps.

This is why I have become interested in evaluation.

How do we know that an AI system is doing what we think it is doing?

What should we measure?

What kinds of errors matter most?

How should performance change across different groups of users?

What happens when the system encounters something outside its training distribution?

These questions are more interesting to me than simply asking whether a model is powerful.

Power without understanding can create problems.

Evaluation gives us a way to understand what the system is actually doing.

My habit of noticing patterns has also influenced the way I study.

I pay attention to the moments when I become confused.

If I repeatedly make the same mistake, I ask why.

Maybe I have misunderstood a foundational concept.

Maybe I am applying a rule without understanding its conditions.

Maybe I am solving the problem in a more complicated way than necessary.

This approach has helped me identify weaknesses in my own learning.

Instead of treating mistakes as isolated incidents, I look for patterns.

One mistake may be random.

Ten similar mistakes probably mean something.

This is another reason I enjoy computer science.

Computers are built around patterns.

Algorithms search for structure.

Machine learning models identify patterns in data.

Databases organize relationships.

Networks reveal connections.

Even debugging often involves finding a pattern in failures.

My natural curiosity therefore fits naturally with the subject.

I like asking what repeats.

I like asking what changes.

I like asking what causes the difference.

And I like asking whether a pattern can be used to make a prediction or improve a system.

This way of thinking extends beyond technology.

I have become more attentive to patterns in the world around me.

A routine that always creates unnecessary work.

A problem that affects many people but receives little attention.

A behavior that changes when circumstances change.

A small environmental difference that produces a larger result.

Observation creates questions.

Questions create investigation.

Investigation creates understanding.

That cycle has become central to how I learn.

It has also taught me that curiosity requires humility.

Noticing a pattern does not mean understanding its cause.

Two events can happen together without one causing the other.

A small sample can create a misleading impression.

An assumption can make a pattern look obvious when it is not.

This is where scientific thinking becomes important.

Observation should lead to hypotheses, not immediate conclusions.

A good question is often more valuable than a confident answer.

This lesson has become especially important in AI and data science.

Data can reveal patterns, but patterns need interpretation.

A model can identify correlation without establishing causation.

A dataset can represent only a small portion of reality.

A prediction can be useful without being universally correct.

Understanding these limitations makes me more careful when working with data.

I want to become the kind of engineer who does not simply ask whether a system produces a result, but whether the result should be trusted.

That distinction will matter increasingly as AI systems become more capable.

Technology is moving into areas where mistakes can have meaningful consequences.

AI may help people make decisions about education, finance, employment, healthcare, and other important aspects of life.

The systems therefore need to be evaluated not only for technical performance but for how they behave in the real world.

This is one reason I am interested in responsible AI.

I want to understand how systems can be made more transparent, reliable, and adaptable.

I want to study how models fail.

I want to investigate how data influences behavior.

And I want to explore ways of designing systems that can communicate uncertainty rather than pretending to know everything.

This interest connects with my broader approach to engineering.

I am not satisfied when something simply produces an answer.

I want to understand why.

If a program succeeds, I want to know which assumptions made it succeed.

If a model performs well, I want to know whether the result generalizes.

If an interface feels intuitive, I want to understand what makes it intuitive.

If a process becomes faster, I want to know whether the improvement actually matters to the people using it.

This curiosity sometimes makes me slower.

I can spend a long time investigating something that another person might simply accept.

But I have learned that this is not necessarily wasted time.

Understanding the system can reveal opportunities for improvement.

It can also prevent mistakes.

Some of the most important discoveries in engineering begin with someone asking a question that seems unnecessary.

Why is this done this way?

What happens if we change this assumption?

Why does this work under one condition but fail under another?

Can we remove this step?

Can we measure this differently?

These questions challenge the status quo.

They are also the beginning of innovation.

Innovation is often described as inventing something completely new.

Sometimes it is.

But often, innovation begins by looking at something ordinary and seeing it differently.

A familiar process can be redesigned.

An old assumption can be questioned.

Two existing ideas can be connected in a new way.

A problem everyone accepts can finally be treated as solvable.

That is the kind of innovation that interests me.

I do not need every project to be revolutionary.

I want my work to be thoughtful.

I want to identify problems that matter.

I want to understand them deeply.

I want to test ideas rather than assume they will work.

And I want to build solutions that are appropriate to the situation.

This is also why I enjoy working on multidisciplinary problems.

When two fields meet, there are often patterns that neither field notices alone.

Computer science can provide tools for analyzing data.

Biology can provide examples of complex systems.

Environmental science can provide real-world problems.

Education can reveal questions about human learning.

Design can reveal how people interact with technology.

The intersection is often more interesting than any one field by itself.

I want college to expose me to those intersections.

I want to study computer science deeply while remaining curious about other disciplines.

I want to work with students who see problems differently.

I want professors to challenge my assumptions.

I want research opportunities that require observation before implementation.

And I want to develop the ability to turn a vague observation into a precise research question.

That ability may be one of the most valuable skills I can develop.

There is a major difference between saying:

"Something seems inefficient."

and asking:

"Which part of the process creates the greatest delay, under what conditions, and why?"

The second question can be investigated.

It can be measured.

It can be tested.

It can lead to a solution.

That transformation—from vague curiosity to precise inquiry—is something I want to become better at.

My long-term goal is to work in artificial intelligence and software engineering, developing systems that solve meaningful real-world problems.

But I want to approach those problems with observation before implementation.

I do not want to start by choosing a technology.

I want to start by understanding the situation.

Then I can decide whether AI is appropriate.

Maybe the solution will be machine learning.

Maybe it will be a database.

Maybe it will be automation.

Maybe it will be a redesigned interface.

Maybe it will require no software at all.

The important thing is choosing based on the problem rather than the popularity of the technology.

This principle has become one of the strongest parts of my engineering identity.

I like technology.

I enjoy learning new tools.

I am fascinated by artificial intelligence.

But I am more interested in problems than in tools.

Tools change.

Problems remain.

People continue to struggle with inefficient processes.

Organizations continue to deal with complexity.

Students continue to encounter barriers to learning.

Communities continue to face environmental and social challenges.

Technology can help, but only when we understand what kind of help is actually needed.

That understanding begins with paying attention.

Perhaps that is why I notice things.

I notice the extra step.

The repeated task.

The confusing instruction.

The unexpected result.

The pattern that does not quite make sense.

The question that nobody seems to be asking.

I do not always know what these observations mean.

But I have learned not to ignore them.

Instead, I write them down.

I investigate.

I test.

Sometimes I discover that there is a simple explanation.

Sometimes I discover that my original assumption was wrong.

And sometimes I discover a problem worth solving.

That process has become one of the most rewarding parts of learning for me.

It has taught me that curiosity is not simply wondering about things.

Curiosity becomes powerful when it leads to investigation.

Observation gives you something to notice.

Questions give you something to investigate.

Evidence gives you something to learn from.

And building gives you a way to turn that learning into action.

That is the cycle I want to continue in college.

I want to notice more.

Question more carefully.

Investigate more rigorously.

Build more thoughtfully.

And learn from what happens.

There will always be things that seem ordinary.

A slow process.

A confusing interface.

A repeated task.

A strange pattern.

A problem that everyone has accepted as normal.

I do not know which of these observations will eventually lead to my most important work.

But I know that I want to keep noticing them.

Because sometimes innovation does not begin with a spectacular idea.

Sometimes it begins with a very ordinary question:

"Why does it have to be this way?"
