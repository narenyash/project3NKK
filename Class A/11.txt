
Some of the most important things I have learned about communication came from moments when communication failed.

A sentence can be grammatically correct and still be misunderstood.

An explanation can contain all the necessary information and still leave someone confused.

A message can be translated accurately and still lose its meaning.

These experiences made me realize that communication is not simply the transfer of words from one person to another.

It is the transfer of meaning.

That distinction became particularly interesting to me as I began studying computer science and artificial intelligence.

Computers process languages differently from humans.

For a person, a sentence can contain context, emotion, cultural references, implied meanings, and assumptions that are never explicitly stated.

For a computer, language must eventually be represented in a form that can be processed computationally.

The gap between those two perspectives fascinated me.

I began wondering how machines could work with something as flexible and ambiguous as human language.

This question eventually led me toward natural language processing and modern language models.

But my interest in language began long before I knew the term "natural language processing."

I grew up seeing people communicate in different ways depending on who they were speaking with.

At home, conversations could shift naturally between languages.

In school, technical concepts were often explained differently from how they were discussed casually.

Online, communication became even more flexible.

People shortened words, mixed languages, used emojis, relied on images, and developed expressions that made sense only within a particular community.

The more I paid attention, the more I realized that language is not a fixed system.

It changes according to context.

That makes it incredibly difficult for computers.

A computer may recognize the words in a sentence without understanding what the speaker actually intends.

Consider a simple phrase such as "That was brilliant."

Depending on the context, it could be genuine praise or sarcasm.

The words are identical.

The meaning is not.

Humans usually infer the difference from tone, situation, previous conversation, and shared knowledge.

Teaching a computer to do the same requires much more than vocabulary.

It requires context.

This problem fascinated me because it connected several areas I enjoy: programming, linguistics, mathematics, artificial intelligence, and human behavior.

I began experimenting with language-based AI systems.

At first, I was impressed by how naturally modern models could generate text.

They could summarize information, answer questions, rewrite paragraphs, translate between languages, and maintain conversations.

But the more I used them, the more questions I had.

How does a model learn language?

Does it understand meaning or patterns?

Why can it produce a convincing answer while sometimes making a factual mistake?

How does context affect its predictions?

Why does changing one sentence sometimes change the entire response?

These questions made me want to understand the technology beneath the interface.

I began learning about how language can be represented computationally.

Words can be converted into numerical representations.

Sequences can be processed through neural networks.

Models can learn relationships between tokens.

Attention mechanisms can allow systems to consider relationships between different parts of a sequence.

These concepts were technically fascinating.

But I was even more interested in what they implied.

Human language contains enormous amounts of information about how people think.

The way someone describes a problem can reveal what they understand.

The words they choose can reveal their priorities.

The questions they ask can reveal what they do not know.

Language is therefore not simply a communication system.

It is also a window into human reasoning.

That made natural language processing feel much more significant than simply teaching computers to generate sentences.

It became a question about how machines interact with human thought.

This perspective became particularly important when I thought about education.

A student does not always know how to express what they do not understand.

They may ask a vague question.

They may describe a problem incorrectly.

They may repeat an explanation without actually understanding it.

A human teacher can often recognize this.

An intelligent educational system would need to do something similar.

It would need to understand not only the student's words but the underlying difficulty.

For example, a student might ask for the answer to a mathematics problem when what they actually need is help understanding one step.

Giving the final answer would solve the immediate question but not the underlying problem.

A useful AI tutor should ideally recognize that distinction.

It should ask questions.

It should provide hints.

It should adapt its explanation.

It should determine whether the student needs another example or a simpler concept.

This is where my interest in language intersects with my interest in education.

Language is the interface through which learning often happens.

If technology can understand language more effectively, it may be able to make educational systems more adaptive.

But there is another challenge.

Language is deeply connected to culture.

A phrase that is perfectly natural in one community may sound strange in another.

An idiom may not have a direct equivalent in another language.

A translation may preserve the literal meaning while losing the cultural meaning.

This is especially relevant in a multilingual country like India.

People often move naturally between languages.

A conversation may contain multiple languages within a single sentence.

This kind of communication can be difficult for systems trained primarily on standardized language.

That made me interested in multilingual AI.

How can models serve people who do not communicate primarily in English?

How can systems handle code-switching?

How can technology preserve cultural context rather than simply translating words?

How can AI become useful to people who interact with technology in the languages they actually use every day?

These questions seem increasingly important as AI becomes part of everyday life.

If intelligent systems are designed primarily around one language or one cultural context, their usefulness will be unevenly distributed.

The technology may be powerful, but access to its benefits will remain limited.

This is one reason I am interested in language technology as a form of accessibility.

Accessibility is often discussed in terms of physical interfaces.

Language creates another kind of barrier.

A person may have access to a powerful computer but still struggle to use a service because the information is available only in a language they are not comfortable with.

AI could potentially reduce that barrier.

A user could interact with a system in their preferred language.

Information could be translated.

Complex instructions could be simplified.

Technical concepts could be explained differently for different audiences.

But this also creates responsibility.

Translation systems can make mistakes.

AI-generated explanations can contain inaccuracies.

A fluent response can sound trustworthy even when it is wrong.

This is one of the reasons I have become cautious about judging AI systems based only on how natural their language sounds.

Fluency is not the same as truth.

A system can produce a beautifully written answer that contains a serious error.

This distinction is important because language has persuasive power.

People tend to trust information that is presented confidently and clearly.

AI systems therefore need to be evaluated not only for linguistic quality but also for factual reliability, uncertainty, and appropriate use.

These questions have made me more interested in responsible AI.

I want to understand how systems can communicate their limitations.

I want to explore ways of evaluating language models beyond simple benchmarks.

I want to understand how cultural and linguistic differences affect model performance.

And I want to investigate how intelligent systems can support human communication without replacing human judgment.

My interest in language has also changed the way I communicate with people.

I have become more conscious of the difference between being technically correct and being understandable.

When explaining a technical idea, I try to avoid unnecessary terminology.

When working on a team, I try to clarify assumptions rather than assuming everyone interprets a statement the same way.

When writing documentation, I think about the person reading it for the first time.

These habits may seem simple, but they have made me a better collaborator.

Technology is ultimately created by people and used by people.

If engineers cannot communicate with one another, systems become harder to build.

If developers cannot communicate with users, they may solve the wrong problem.

If researchers cannot explain their results clearly, important discoveries may not reach the people who could benefit from them.

Communication is therefore not separate from engineering.

It is part of engineering.

This became particularly obvious during team projects.

A technical problem can sometimes be solved quickly once everyone agrees on what the problem actually is.

But reaching that agreement can be difficult.

Different people may use the same word to mean different things.

Someone may assume a feature is required while another person considers it optional.

A developer may think a task is complete because the code works, while a user may consider it incomplete because the interface is confusing.

Clear communication prevents these misunderstandings.

It also creates trust.

When people know what a system can and cannot do, they can use it more effectively.

This is especially important for AI.

Users need to understand when they are interacting with an automated system.

They need to know when its output should be verified.

They need to understand what information the system uses.

They need reasonable expectations about its capabilities.

Good AI communication is therefore not simply about making systems sound human.

It is about helping humans understand the system.

That distinction matters to me.

I do not want to build technology that imitates humans only for the sake of appearing intelligent.

I want to build technology that communicates clearly enough to be useful.

Sometimes the best interface may not be a conversation at all.

Sometimes a visual explanation may be better.

Sometimes a short answer is better than a detailed one.

Sometimes a system should ask for clarification instead of guessing.

Understanding when to communicate—and how—is as important as generating language itself.

This perspective has made me interested in human-computer interaction.

For decades, computers required users to adapt to machines.

Users had to learn commands, interfaces, menus, and technical concepts.

Modern AI is beginning to reverse some of that relationship.

People can describe what they want using ordinary language.

The computer can interpret the request and determine how to respond.

This has enormous potential.

It can make technology easier to access.

But it also introduces new problems.

Human language is ambiguous.

Users do not always know exactly what they want.

They may change their minds.

They may give incomplete instructions.

They may expect the system to understand context that has never been explicitly provided.

Building systems that handle these situations reliably is a fascinating engineering challenge.

It requires language understanding, reasoning, memory, interface design, and careful handling of uncertainty.

These are areas I want to explore further.

My long-term interest is therefore not simply in language models.

It is in the broader relationship between humans and intelligent systems.

How should people communicate with AI?

How should AI communicate uncertainty?

How can intelligent systems adapt to different users?

How can they work across languages and cultures?

How can they help people without making them dependent?

These questions are likely to become increasingly important as AI becomes part of everyday life.

I want to be prepared to contribute to that future.

College will give me the opportunity to develop the knowledge necessary to do so.

I want to study computer science deeply, particularly algorithms, artificial intelligence, machine learning, and systems.

I also want to explore linguistics and human-computer interaction where possible.

I want to participate in research involving natural language processing.

I want to experiment with multilingual systems.

I want to understand how language models are evaluated.

And I want to work with people who approach communication from perspectives different from my own.

My goal is not simply to build a system that can speak many languages.

It is to understand what meaningful communication requires.

That is a much harder problem.

Translation is not only replacing words.

Communication is not only producing sentences.

Understanding is not only recognizing vocabulary.

Meaning depends on context.

It depends on experience.

It depends on culture.

It depends on what has already been said.

And sometimes it depends on what has not been said.

That complexity is exactly what makes the problem interesting.

I am drawn to problems that cannot be solved by one simple rule.

Language is one of them.

It combines structure and ambiguity.

It has rules but constantly changes.

It can be precise or poetic.

It can communicate information or emotion.

It can be used to teach, persuade, question, comfort, argue, or create.

Building machines that interact with such a system requires us to understand something about ourselves as well.

That is what I find most fascinating.

When we teach a computer to process language, we are forced to ask what language actually is.

When we ask a machine to understand context, we are forced to define what context means.

When we ask an AI system to explain something, we are forced to think about what makes an explanation useful.

Technology becomes a mirror.

It reveals the assumptions we normally make without noticing.

That is why language has become more than an academic interest for me.

It has become a way of thinking about technology and people.

I began with a simple observation: sometimes people misunderstand each other even when they use the same words.

That observation led me to programming.

Programming taught me that computers require precise instructions.

AI taught me that language cannot always be made precise without losing something important.

Teaching taught me that understanding depends on the person receiving the explanation.

And communication taught me that the most technically correct message is not always the most useful one.

These lessons have shaped the kind of engineer I want to become.

I want to build systems that are technically sophisticated but understandable.

I want to work on AI that can communicate across languages and contexts.

I want to explore technologies that make information more accessible.

And I want to remember that behind every input is a person trying to accomplish something.

A sentence is not merely a sequence of words.

A question is not merely a string of tokens.

A conversation is not merely data moving between two systems.

There is intention behind it.

There is context.

There is a human being trying to be understood.

The technology I hope to build should never lose sight of that.

Perhaps the most important thing language has taught me is that communication begins not with speaking, but with understanding.

That is the principle I want to carry into computer science.

Before building a system that responds, I want to understand what people are trying to say.

Before designing an interface, I want to understand what people are trying to do.

Before creating an AI model, I want to understand the problem it is supposed to solve.

And before assuming that technology has succeeded, I want to ask whether the person on the other side actually feels understood.

That is the kind of technology I hope to help create.

Technology that does not merely process our words.

Technology that helps people understand one another better.
