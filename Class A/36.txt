
Growing up and becoming independent is not a single event. It is a gradual process in which a person learns to make decisions, accept responsibility, understand consequences, and develop confidence in their own abilities. My academic journey has increasingly taught me that education is not only about acquiring knowledge. It is also about becoming capable of thinking and acting independently.

This understanding has influenced the way I approach my studies, technical interests, projects, and future goals.

When I was younger, learning often meant following instructions. Teachers explained concepts, textbooks provided information, and examinations tested whether I had understood the material. This structure was important because it provided a foundation.

As I became more interested in technology, however, I encountered situations where there were no clear instructions.

If I wanted to build a project, I had to decide what to build.

If something failed, I had to determine why.

If I encountered a new technology, I had to decide how to learn it.

If several possible solutions existed, I had to evaluate them.

These experiences gradually taught me to take greater responsibility for my own learning.

Programming was particularly important in this development.

A programming problem may provide an objective, but it rarely provides every step required to reach the solution.

The programmer must determine how to approach the problem.

They must choose an algorithm.

They must decide how to structure the code.

They must test the result.

They must identify errors.

They must revise the solution.

This process encouraged me to become more independent.

Instead of waiting for someone to tell me exactly what to do, I began learning how to investigate problems myself.

This did not mean refusing help.

In fact, I learned that independent learning and asking for help are not opposites.

An independent learner recognises when they have reached the limit of their current understanding and knows how to seek the information necessary to move forward.

The difference is that the learner takes responsibility for finding that information.

This distinction has become important to me.

When I encounter an unfamiliar technical concept, I do not expect someone else to teach it to me step by step.

I begin by researching.

I look for explanations.

I compare different sources.

I experiment with small examples.

I identify what I do not understand.

Then, if necessary, I ask someone more experienced.

This process has made me more confident in unfamiliar situations.

I have learned that not knowing something is not a permanent limitation.

It is simply the starting point of learning.

This mindset will be important in higher education.

University provides greater freedom than school.

Students have more control over how they spend their time, which opportunities they pursue, and how deeply they explore a subject.

That freedom is valuable, but it also creates responsibility.

No one can monitor every hour of a student's day.

A student must decide whether to study regularly or postpone work.

They must decide whether to explore a subject beyond the syllabus.

They must decide how seriously to participate in projects.

They must decide which opportunities are worth pursuing.

This independence is one of the aspects of university life that I look forward to.

I want to become more responsible for my own development.

I understand that freedom without discipline can become unproductive.

If I have complete control over my time but do not manage it carefully, opportunities can easily be wasted.

Therefore, independence requires discipline.

A student who wants to pursue academics, projects, research, and extracurricular activities must learn to organise priorities.

This is something I have gradually learned through my own experiences.

When I first became interested in multiple areas of technology, I wanted to explore everything simultaneously.

I would begin learning one tool and then become interested in another.

I would think about several project ideas at the same time.

Although this enthusiasm was valuable, it sometimes made it difficult to complete what I had started.

I learned that independence does not mean following every interest immediately.

It means deciding which interests deserve attention now and which can be explored later.

Prioritisation is therefore part of independence.

This has changed how I set goals.

Instead of simply saying that I want to learn artificial intelligence, I can divide the objective into smaller stages.

First, I can strengthen the necessary mathematics.

Then I can understand basic machine learning concepts.

Then I can implement simple models.

Then I can study more advanced methods.

Then I can apply the knowledge to projects.

This creates a path from intention to action.

It also allows me to measure progress.

Independence has similarly influenced how I approach projects.

When building software, I cannot always rely on someone to identify every problem for me.

I need to test the system.

I need to read documentation.

I need to interpret error messages.

I need to investigate possible causes.

I need to make decisions.

This process has strengthened my problem-solving ability.

It has also taught me patience.

Sometimes the answer to a technical problem is not immediately visible.

I may need to try several approaches.

I may need to read multiple explanations.

I may need to simplify the problem.

I may need to rebuild part of the system.

Learning to continue through these difficulties has increased my confidence.

I have realised that independence is not the ability to solve everything alone.

It is the ability to remain responsible for finding a path forward.

This distinction is important.

There will always be problems that require collaboration.

No engineer can know everything.

No student can master every field independently.

No researcher can answer every question alone.

Therefore, independence must coexist with collaboration.

A mature person should know when to work independently and when to seek assistance.

I want to develop this judgment.

Working with others has also taught me that independence does not mean ignoring other people's ideas.

A person can take responsibility for a decision while still listening carefully to others.

In fact, independent thinking requires the ability to evaluate different perspectives.

If I accept an idea simply because someone else suggested it, I am not thinking independently.

If I reject every idea simply because it is not mine, I am also not thinking independently.

Independent thinking means examining the evidence and reasoning behind an idea.

Then making a decision based on that understanding.

This is an important quality for an engineer.

Technical decisions often involve trade-offs.

There may be several valid approaches.

The engineer must consider performance, cost, reliability, scalability, maintainability, security, and user requirements.

No one can provide a universal answer.

The engineer must evaluate the situation and make a reasoned decision.

I want to develop this ability through higher education.

My interest in artificial intelligence has made independent thinking even more important.

Modern AI tools make it possible to obtain answers quickly.

However, obtaining an answer is not the same as understanding it.

An AI system can generate code.

It can explain a concept.

It can suggest a solution.

But the responsibility for verifying the result remains with the person using it.

If I accept every generated answer without evaluation, I am not developing my own capabilities.

I want technology to support my learning rather than replace it.

When using intelligent tools, I want to ask whether the result is correct.

Why does the solution work?

Are there limitations?

Could there be another approach?

Does the explanation make sense?

This habit of verification is another form of independent thinking.

I believe it will become increasingly important as AI becomes more integrated into education and professional work.

Students will have access to increasingly powerful tools.

The challenge will be maintaining intellectual independence while using them.

The goal should not be to avoid technology.

The goal should be to use technology intelligently.

This is a principle I want to follow.

Independence also means accepting responsibility for mistakes.

When someone else makes every decision, it is easy to blame them when something goes wrong.

When I make a decision myself, I must also accept its consequences.

This can be uncomfortable.

But it is necessary for growth.

If I choose an approach that does not work, I should examine the decision rather than blame circumstances immediately.

What information did I have?

What assumption did I make?

What did I overlook?

What could I have done differently?

This process develops judgment.

It also develops maturity.

I believe higher education will provide many opportunities to develop this quality.

Projects will require independent decisions.

Research will involve uncertainty.

Group work will require responsibility.

Internships may involve professional expectations.

These experiences will gradually prepare me for professional life.

Another important aspect of independence is financial and practical awareness.

As a student progresses toward adulthood, education increasingly becomes connected to real-world responsibilities.

Career decisions, professional opportunities, further education, and personal goals require careful consideration.

I want to develop the ability to evaluate opportunities based on long-term value rather than immediate appeal.

For example, a job opportunity may offer a good salary but limited learning.

Another may provide stronger technical exposure and opportunities for growth.

A project may appear impressive but provide little meaningful experience.

A course may be popular but not necessarily appropriate for my goals.

Independence means learning how to evaluate these choices.

I do not expect every decision to be perfect.

But I want to make decisions thoughtfully.

This is another reason I value education.

Education improves not only professional skills but also judgment.

It teaches students how to analyse information, compare alternatives, and recognise consequences.

These abilities are useful throughout life.

My academic interests have also taught me that independence requires curiosity.

If I am curious, I am more likely to investigate a problem rather than wait for an explanation.

Curiosity leads to questions.

Questions lead to research.

Research leads to understanding.

Understanding leads to better decisions.

This cycle has influenced my learning style.

I prefer to understand concepts deeply rather than memorise them temporarily.

When I study an algorithm, I want to know not only how it works but why it is useful.

When I study a machine learning model, I want to understand its assumptions.

When I study software architecture, I want to understand why one design may be more appropriate than another.

This approach requires effort, but it creates knowledge that is easier to apply independently.

Independence is also connected to confidence.

There is a difference between confidence based on praise and confidence based on experience.

Praise can provide temporary motivation.

Experience provides evidence.

When I successfully solve a difficult problem after several unsuccessful attempts, I gain confidence that I can handle future difficulties.

When I complete a project that initially seemed beyond my ability, I learn that unfamiliar challenges can become manageable through effort.

These experiences gradually build self-confidence.

I want to continue creating such experiences for myself.

I do not want confidence to come from believing that I am naturally capable of everything.

I want it to come from knowing that I can learn.

This is a more sustainable form of confidence.

It allows me to approach unfamiliar problems without expecting immediate success.

I believe this quality will be essential in research and engineering.

Both fields involve uncertainty.

There will be problems I have never encountered before.

There will be technologies I do not understand.

There will be situations in which no one can provide a simple answer.

The ability to remain calm and investigate will be valuable.

Independence also involves knowing when to change direction.

Taking responsibility for a decision does not mean becoming emotionally attached to it.

If evidence shows that an approach is not working, I should be willing to reconsider it.

This is especially important in technical projects.

A developer may spend significant time implementing a particular feature.

If testing shows that the feature is unnecessary or ineffective, continuing simply because effort has already been invested is not rational.

An independent thinker should be able to evaluate the evidence and change direction.

This is not weakness.

It is judgment.

I want to develop that judgment throughout my education.

My interest in research makes this especially relevant.

Research requires researchers to make decisions with incomplete information.

They must decide which questions are worth investigating.

They must choose methodologies.

They must evaluate results.

They must decide whether evidence supports a conclusion.

These decisions require independence.

At the same time, research is collaborative.

Researchers read existing literature.

They discuss ideas with colleagues.

They receive feedback.

They work with mentors.

Therefore, research demonstrates the balance I want to develop: independent thinking combined with openness to collaboration.

Being able to explain my own ideas clearly is an important part of professional maturity.

If I cannot communicate what I am thinking, other people may not be able to evaluate or contribute to the idea.

I want to develop the ability to present technical concepts clearly.

I want to explain complex ideas without unnecessary jargon.

I want to listen carefully to questions.

I want to respond thoughtfully.

These skills will help me participate more effectively in academic and professional environments.
