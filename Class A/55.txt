
Change is one of the few constants in modern life. Technologies evolve, industries transform, educational methods change, and problems that seemed distant can become immediate. For a student preparing to enter a field such as computer science and artificial intelligence, this reality is impossible to ignore. I have gradually learned that preparation for the future does not mean predicting every change. It means developing the ability to adapt when change arrives.

This understanding has become an important part of the way I approach education.

When I first began learning programming, I believed that becoming proficient in a particular technology would be enough. If I learned a programming language well, I thought I would have acquired a permanent skill.

Over time, I realised that technology does not remain still.

Frameworks change.

Libraries are updated.

Development practices evolve.

New programming languages become popular.

Cloud platforms introduce new tools.

Artificial intelligence changes the way software is developed.

A skill that is valuable today may eventually become less important.

This initially seemed challenging.

Later, I understood that this is also an opportunity.

Technology changes because people discover better ways of solving problems.

The important skill is therefore not memorising one tool.

It is learning how to learn new tools.

This distinction has influenced my educational goals.

I want to build strong fundamentals rather than depend entirely on current trends.

If I understand algorithms, data structures, databases, software architecture, mathematics, and computational thinking, I can adapt when technologies change.

The specific tools may be different in the future, but the underlying principles will remain useful.

This is one of the reasons I value fundamental education.

A strong foundation provides stability in a changing environment.

My experience with software projects has repeatedly demonstrated this.

There have been situations where a technology I expected to use did not behave as anticipated.

A library may have limitations.

A dependency may be incompatible.

A deployment environment may differ from local development.

An approach that worked in a small prototype may not work when the project becomes larger.

In each situation, I had two choices.

I could remain attached to the original plan.

Or I could understand the actual problem and adapt.

I have learned that the second approach is usually more productive.

Adaptation does not mean abandoning goals whenever something becomes difficult.

It means remaining committed to the objective while being willing to change the method.

This distinction is important.

If the goal is to build a reliable application, the specific framework should not become more important than reliability.

If the goal is to develop an accurate model, a particular algorithm should not become more important than the evidence.

If the goal is to solve a user's problem, a planned feature should not become more important than the user's actual needs.

The objective should guide the technology.

Not the other way around.

This principle has become central to my thinking.

Adaptability also requires accepting that initial assumptions may be wrong.

When beginning a project, I may have an idea about how users will interact with it.

After testing, their behaviour may be different.

I may expect a particular feature to be useful.

Users may ignore it.

I may believe a model will perform well.

Testing may show otherwise.

I may expect a particular workflow to be efficient.

Real-world usage may reveal unnecessary steps.

The ability to adapt requires accepting these discoveries without becoming defensive.

Evidence should be stronger than attachment to an original idea.

This is one of the most valuable lessons I want to carry into higher education.

University will introduce challenges that I cannot completely predict.

Some subjects may be harder than expected.

Some projects may require unfamiliar skills.

Some research questions may change direction.

Some opportunities may not work out.

I want to respond to these situations with flexibility.

If a subject is difficult, I can change my study method.

If a project is failing, I can reconsider the approach.

If a research direction becomes less promising, I can investigate alternatives.

If I receive criticism, I can use it to improve.

Adaptability gives me options.

It prevents one setback from becoming a permanent limitation.

This does not mean I expect change to be easy.

Change can be uncomfortable.

It often requires leaving familiar routines.

Learning a new technology means being a beginner again.

Changing an approach means admitting that the previous approach was not sufficient.

Entering a new environment requires adjusting to unfamiliar people and expectations.

But discomfort is often part of development.

A person who never leaves familiar territory may become comfortable but not necessarily capable of handling new challenges.

I want to become comfortable with being temporarily uncomfortable.

This is particularly important in computer science.

The field changes at an extraordinary pace.

Artificial intelligence has already changed many assumptions about software development.

Tasks that once required extensive manual implementation can increasingly be assisted by intelligent tools.

This does not make programming irrelevant.

Instead, it changes which programming skills become most valuable.

Developers may need to focus more on system design, problem definition, verification, security, evaluation, and understanding user needs.

The ability to adapt becomes essential.

I do not want to fear these changes.

I want to understand them.

Whenever a new technology appears, I want to ask what problem it solves, how it works, what its limitations are, and where it can be useful.

This allows me to respond thoughtfully rather than simply following trends.

Adaptability also requires critical thinking.

Not every new technology is automatically better.

A new framework may be faster but less stable.

A new AI model may produce impressive results but require more resources.

A new development tool may increase productivity while introducing security concerns.

A new platform may become popular without being appropriate for every project.

Adapting does not mean adopting everything.

It means evaluating change.

I want to develop this judgment.

A strong engineer should be able to say both:

"This is new, so I should learn about it."

and

"This is new, but I need evidence before using it."

Both statements can be responsible.

Adaptability therefore depends on curiosity and skepticism together.

Curiosity encourages exploration.

Critical thinking determines whether the exploration should lead to adoption.

I want to develop both qualities.

Change also affects education.

Students today have access to information on a scale that previous generations could not easily imagine.

Online courses, documentation, research papers, open-source projects, technical communities, and AI-assisted tools make independent learning easier.

But this abundance creates another challenge.

Students must decide what to learn.

They must distinguish reliable information from unreliable information.

They must decide which resources are worth their time.

They must avoid constantly switching between topics without developing depth.

Adaptability therefore requires learning discipline.

I want to become intentional about what I learn.

If a technology is relevant to a project, I can study it deeply enough to use it.

If a subject is foundational, I can invest more time in understanding it.

If a topic is interesting but not immediately relevant, I can explore it without allowing it to distract from important priorities.

This balance will help me remain adaptable without becoming unfocused.

Adaptability also requires emotional awareness.

When plans change unexpectedly, frustration is natural.

A project may be delayed.

A planned opportunity may disappear.

A solution may fail.

A team may change direction.

The initial reaction may be disappointment.

But remaining focused on that disappointment does not solve the problem.

The useful question becomes:

What can I do now?

This shift from what was supposed to happen to what can happen next is an important part of resilience.

I want to develop that habit.

My experiences with failure have reinforced it.

A failed project can create two very different responses.

One response is to remain focused on what went wrong.

The other is to understand what happened and determine what should change.

The second response creates movement.

Adaptability turns failure into a change of direction rather than a dead end.

This is especially relevant to research.

An experiment may produce unexpected results.

A dataset may be inadequate.

A model may fail to generalise.

A rigid researcher may become attached to the original plan.

An adaptable researcher can use the evidence to improve the investigation.

I want to develop the second approach.

Adaptability also requires knowing when not to change.

Constantly changing direction can be as harmful as refusing to change.

If every small difficulty causes a project to be abandoned, meaningful progress becomes impossible.

Therefore, adaptability is not the same as instability.

A person should distinguish between a temporary obstacle and a fundamental problem.

If the approach is sound but difficult, persistence may be necessary.

If evidence shows that the approach is fundamentally flawed, adaptation may be necessary.

Developing this judgment takes experience.

I want to gain that experience through projects and research.
