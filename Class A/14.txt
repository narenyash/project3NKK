
One of the most exciting questions in technology is:

**Can we build it?**

It is the question that starts projects.

It motivates engineers.

It leads to experiments.

It turns ideas into products.

But the more I learned about technology, the more I realized that another question can be even more important:

**Should we build it?**

I did not always think this way.

When I first became interested in programming, I was fascinated by possibility. If I could make something work, I considered that a success. I wanted to learn new tools, build applications, experiment with artificial intelligence, and see what computers could accomplish.

The excitement came from making an idea real.

But artificial intelligence changed the way I thought about that excitement.

AI systems can generate text, recognize images, make predictions, recommend information, automate tasks, and interact with people. The capabilities are impressive.

They are also powerful.

And power creates responsibility.

I began realizing that the most difficult part of building technology is not always making it work.

Sometimes the difficult part is deciding how it should be used.

That question has become increasingly important to me.

A technology can be technically successful and still create problems.

A recommendation system can optimize engagement while encouraging unhealthy behavior.

A facial recognition system can identify people accurately while raising serious privacy concerns.

An automated decision system can be efficient while reproducing biases in its data.

A language model can produce fluent answers while confidently giving incorrect information.

None of these problems can be solved simply by writing more code.

They require judgment.

That realization changed what I think it means to be an engineer.

Engineering is not only about creating capabilities.

It is also about understanding consequences.

This does not mean that every technology should be rejected because it might be misused.

Almost every useful technology can be misused.

The challenge is learning how to identify risks, reduce them, and make responsible decisions about deployment.

That requires technical knowledge.

But it also requires something beyond technical knowledge.

It requires asking questions about people.

Who will use the system?

Who could be affected by it?

What happens when it makes a mistake?

Who is responsible for correcting that mistake?

What information does it need?

What information should it never collect?

How should users know when they are interacting with an automated system?

These questions became especially meaningful to me when I began working with AI.

At first, I was primarily interested in performance.

How accurate is the model?

How quickly does it respond?

How large is the dataset?

Can the model handle more complex inputs?

These are useful questions.

But they are incomplete.

Suppose a model achieves very high accuracy.

If the dataset does not represent the people who will use the system, is that accuracy enough?

Suppose an AI assistant produces answers quickly.

If users cannot distinguish reliable answers from uncertain ones, is speed actually an advantage?

Suppose an automated system reduces costs.

If the reduction comes from making it impossible for users to challenge a decision, is the system genuinely better?

These questions made me understand that metrics need context.

Numbers describe performance.

They do not automatically describe value.

This is one reason I became interested in responsible AI.

I want to understand how technical systems can be designed to account for uncertainty, bias, privacy, and human oversight.

I also want to understand where responsibility belongs.

It is easy to say that users are responsible for how they use a technology.

It is also easy to say that developers are responsible for everything that happens after deployment.

Reality is more complicated.

Responsibility is often distributed across developers, organizations, users, policymakers, and institutions.

Understanding those relationships is important because technology rarely operates in isolation.

An AI model exists inside a larger system.

That system exists inside an organization.

The organization operates inside a society.

A technical decision can therefore have consequences far beyond the code itself.

This became clearer to me as I learned about data.

Data is often treated as a resource.

Collect more data.

Train the model.

Improve performance.

But data represents people, behavior, decisions, and experiences.

It is not simply a collection of numbers.

If information about people is collected without adequate safeguards, privacy can be compromised.

If historical data contains unfair patterns, a model trained on it may reproduce those patterns.

If users do not understand how their data is being used, consent becomes complicated.

These issues made me more careful about the datasets I work with.

I began asking where the data came from.

What does it represent?

What does it fail to represent?

What assumptions are embedded in it?

How should it be stored?

Who should have access?

These questions do not always have simple answers.

That is precisely why they are important.

I have also become more aware of how easily technology can create an illusion of objectivity.

A computer produces a number.

A model produces a prediction.

An algorithm produces a ranking.

Because the output is numerical, it can appear neutral.

But the system behind the output was created by people.

People selected the data.

People chose the objective.

People decided what should be measured.

People decided what counted as success.

The final number may therefore contain human assumptions even when no person directly made the final decision.

Understanding this has changed how I interpret automated systems.

I no longer assume that an algorithm is neutral simply because it is mathematical.

Mathematics can describe a process precisely.

It does not decide whether the objective of that process is fair.

That decision requires human judgment.

This distinction is one of the reasons I want an interdisciplinary education.

Computer science can teach me how systems work.

Mathematics can teach me how models are constructed.

But ethics, philosophy, social science, and law can help me understand the broader consequences of deploying those systems.

I do not want to study these subjects as separate worlds.

I want to understand where they intersect.

The future of AI will not be determined only by better models.

It will also be determined by the choices people make about how those models are used.

This perspective has influenced my approach to projects.

When I build an application, I now think about security earlier.

I consider what information the system actually needs.

I think about what happens if a user enters unexpected information.

I consider whether an automated process should be allowed to make a decision without human review.

I think about how errors should be communicated.

These may seem like ordinary engineering practices.

But they reflect a larger principle:

The safest system is not necessarily the one with the most features.

Sometimes safety comes from limiting what a system is allowed to do.

This idea is particularly interesting to me in the context of autonomous AI systems.

As AI agents become more capable, they may be able to plan tasks, call tools, interact with software, and take actions with less direct human involvement.

That creates enormous possibilities.

It also changes the risk.

A chatbot that gives an incorrect answer is one problem.

An autonomous system that takes an incorrect action is another.

The difference is agency.

When a system can act, developers need to think carefully about permissions, constraints, verification, and recovery.

This has made me interested in the architecture of trustworthy AI systems.

How should an agent know what it is allowed to do?

When should it ask for approval?

How can actions be verified?

How can systems recover from mistakes?

How can users understand why an action occurred?

These are engineering questions with ethical consequences.

I find that intersection particularly compelling.

It shows that ethics is not something that should be added at the end of a project.

It can be built into the architecture.

Permissions can be designed.

Logs can be created.

Human approval can be required.

Sensitive information can be protected.

Models can be evaluated under different conditions.

Failure modes can be tested before deployment.

Responsible design is therefore not simply a philosophical idea.

It can become an engineering practice.

That is an important lesson I want to carry into college.

I want to learn how to build systems that are not only capable but dependable.

I want to understand security.

I want to understand privacy.

I want to understand model evaluation.

I want to study how intelligent systems fail.

And I want to understand how technical choices influence human outcomes.

This does not mean I expect technology to be perfect.

I do not think perfection is realistic.

Complex systems will always have limitations.

The goal should be to understand those limitations well enough to manage them responsibly.

That requires humility.

Engineers can become deeply familiar with the systems they create, but familiarity can also create blind spots.

A developer knows how an application is supposed to work.

A user experiences how it actually works.

Those perspectives can be very different.

This is why I value testing with people rather than relying only on technical tests.

A system may pass every automated test and still confuse users.

An AI model may achieve excellent benchmark results and still fail in an unexpected environment.

A security mechanism may be technically strong but unusable.

Testing should therefore include the environment in which the system will actually operate.

This is another connection between engineering and responsibility.

We cannot evaluate a system only under the conditions that make it look good.

We need to understand how it behaves under realistic conditions.

That principle has influenced the way I approach machine learning.

I have become more interested in failure cases.

Which examples does the model classify incorrectly?

Are the errors concentrated in particular categories?

Does performance change when the input changes?

What happens when the data is noisy?

How confident is the model when it is wrong?

These questions tell me more than a single accuracy number.

They also make me think about how systems should communicate uncertainty.

A person can say, "I am not sure."

AI systems should be able to communicate uncertainty appropriately as well.

Sometimes the best answer is not an answer.

Sometimes it is a request for more information.

Sometimes it is a recommendation to consult a human.

Sometimes it is simply an acknowledgment that the system does not know.

This may seem less impressive than a confident response.

But responsible technology is not about appearing impressive.

It is about being useful without creating false confidence.

That principle has become important to me.

I want to build systems that people can trust for the right reasons.

Not because the interface looks sophisticated.

Not because the model speaks confidently.

But because the system has been tested, its limitations are understood, and its behavior is transparent enough for people to make informed decisions.

This is the standard I want to hold myself to as I continue learning.

My long-term goal is to work in artificial intelligence and software engineering.

I want to develop intelligent systems that solve meaningful problems.

But I also want to participate in conversations about how those systems should be designed and deployed.

I want to contribute to technology that improves people's capabilities without unnecessarily reducing their control.

I want to explore AI systems that support humans rather than simply replacing human judgment.

I want to understand when automation is appropriate and when human involvement is essential.

Most importantly, I want to remember that technical ability gives an engineer power.

Power should come with responsibility.

That is why I no longer think the most exciting question in technology is simply:

"Can we build it?"

The better sequence is:

Can we build it?

Why should we?

Who benefits?

Who could be harmed?

What could go wrong?

How would we know?

And what safeguards should exist before we allow the system to operate?

These questions do not make technology less exciting.

They make it more meaningful.

They force us to think beyond the demonstration.

They force us to consider the world in which the technology will actually exist.

That is where engineering becomes more than technical problem solving.

It becomes a form of responsibility.

I want my education to prepare me for that responsibility.

I want rigorous training in computer science, artificial intelligence, algorithms, and systems.

I also want opportunities to study ethics, human-computer interaction, privacy, and the social consequences of technology.

I want to work on projects where technical excellence and responsible design are treated as connected goals.

And I want to learn from people who disagree with me.

Because ethical questions rarely have one simple answer.

Different people can reasonably value different things.

One person may prioritize efficiency.

Another may prioritize privacy.

Another may prioritize accessibility.

Another may prioritize safety.

Good engineering requires understanding those perspectives and making decisions consciously rather than pretending that trade-offs do not exist.

This is another reason I value interdisciplinary learning.

Technology does not belong exclusively to technologists.

The people affected by a system should have a voice in how that system is designed.

Engineers need to listen.

They need to ask questions.

They need to understand consequences outside their area of expertise.

That is the kind of engineer I want to become.

Not someone who simply asks whether a system works.

Someone who asks what "working" actually means.

If a system is fast but unsafe, is it working?

If it is accurate but inaccessible, is it working?

If it is efficient but impossible to challenge, is it working?

If it produces impressive outputs but creates misleading confidence, is it working?

These questions have changed my definition of success.

I no longer think successful technology is simply technology that functions.

Successful technology should function **for a reason**.

It should solve a meaningful problem.

It should respect the people affected by it.

It should acknowledge its limitations.

And when the consequences are significant, it should include appropriate safeguards.

I know I still have much to learn.

I do not expect to have perfect answers to these questions when I enter college.

In fact, I hope college gives me harder questions.

I want to encounter perspectives that challenge my assumptions.

I want to learn about ethical problems I have not considered.

I want to study technical systems deeply enough to understand where responsibility enters their design.

And I want to develop the judgment necessary to make decisions when there is no perfect solution.

The technology I build in the future may be very different from what I build today.

The tools will change.

The models will change.

The applications will change.

But one principle I want to keep constant is this:

Every capability creates a choice.

And every choice deserves consideration.

Technology gives us the ability to do more.

Education should teach us how to decide what is worth doing.

That is the kind of education I am seeking.

I want to become technically capable enough to build powerful systems.

But I also want to become thoughtful enough to question those systems.

Because the future of technology will not depend only on what engineers are capable of creating.

It will depend on what they choose to create, how they choose to use it, and whether they remember the people who will live with the consequences.

For me, that is the question after "Can we?"

It is the question that makes engineering worth doing.
