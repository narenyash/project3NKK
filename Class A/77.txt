My interest in data science developed while working on machine learning projects. Initially, I was mainly focused on the model. I wanted to understand how an algorithm could learn patterns from data and make predictions.

As I worked on more projects, I realised that the model is only one part of the process.

Before training a model, the data needs to be collected and prepared.

After training, the results need to be evaluated.

If the data is incomplete or inconsistent, even a technically advanced model may produce poor results.

This made me more interested in the complete process of working with data.

I started learning basic concepts such as data cleaning, data preprocessing, exploratory data analysis, statistics, visualisation, and machine learning.

These topics helped me understand how important it is to examine data before using it.

A dataset may contain missing values, duplicate records, incorrect information, or unusual observations.

If these issues are ignored, they can affect the final results.

I learned that data preparation is often one of the most time-consuming parts of a machine learning project.

This changed the way I approach AI projects.

Earlier, I would think about which model to use first.

Now I try to understand the data and the problem before deciding on the model.

I believe this is an important improvement in my approach to technical projects.

Another area I find interesting is exploratory data analysis.

Visualisations can show relationships between variables, changes over time, unusual values, or differences between groups.

These patterns can help determine which features may be useful and what questions should be investigated.

I enjoy this process because it combines analytical thinking with practical problem-solving.

Statistics has become an important part of my interest in data science.

I want to strengthen my understanding of statistics because I believe that machine learning should not be treated as a black box.

Understanding the data and the mathematical principles behind the analysis can help produce more reliable conclusions.

Another important lesson I have learned is that correlation does not automatically mean causation.

Two variables may appear to be related without one directly causing the other.

This is important when interpreting data.

A model may identify a relationship, but additional analysis may be required to understand what the relationship actually means.

I find this distinction useful because it encourages careful thinking rather than assuming that every pattern has an obvious explanation.

Another important concept is bias.
