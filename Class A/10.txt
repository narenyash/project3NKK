
One of the hardest lessons I have learned as a student is that having many interests is both an advantage and a problem.

I have always been curious.

When I discover something interesting, I want to learn more about it. If I encounter a new programming framework, I want to experiment with it. If I read about artificial intelligence, I want to understand how the models work. If I come across an interesting scientific question, I want to investigate it. If I see someone building something impressive, I immediately begin wondering whether I could build something similar.

Curiosity has opened many doors for me.

It has also created a problem.

There are too many doors.

As I became more involved in technology, I started collecting interests faster than I could develop them. I wanted to learn web development, artificial intelligence, machine learning, computer vision, cloud computing, mobile development, cybersecurity, and many other areas.

At first, I thought this was simply ambition.

Eventually, I realized that ambition without prioritization can become distraction.

I could spend an entire day learning something new and still finish the day feeling as though I had not moved forward.

That realization forced me to think differently about productivity.

I had previously assumed that productive people were the people who did the most.

Now I believe productive people are often the people who choose what not to do.

This was not an easy lesson for me.

I enjoy starting projects.

Starting is exciting.

There is a blank screen, a new idea, and unlimited possibilities.

The difficult part comes later.

Once the initial excitement disappears, the project requires consistency.

There are bugs to fix.

Documentation to read.

Features that need testing.

Small details that take much longer than expected.

The work becomes less exciting and more repetitive.

That is where discipline becomes more important than enthusiasm.

I learned this through my own projects.

I would sometimes begin with an ambitious idea, spend a great deal of time designing it, and then discover that I had underestimated how much work was required.

Instead of completing one project, I would become interested in another.

The new project seemed easier.

It was also more exciting because everything was still possible.

This created a cycle.

Start.

Explore.

Become interested in something else.

Start again.

Eventually, I realized that the problem was not a lack of motivation.

It was a lack of commitment to finishing.

That was an uncomfortable realization because starting many projects can look like productivity.

A list of unfinished ideas can feel impressive.

A finished project is different.

It forces you to make decisions.

It forces you to remove features.

It forces you to deal with imperfect results.

It forces you to confront the difference between what you imagined and what you can actually build.

Finishing became a skill I wanted to develop.

I began changing the way I approached projects.

Instead of asking, "What could this become?" I started asking, "What is the smallest useful version I can finish?"

That question changed everything.

A large application could become one working feature.

A complicated AI system could become a simple prototype.

A huge research idea could become one experiment.

Once the smaller version worked, I could decide whether expanding it was worthwhile.

This approach helped me understand the importance of scope.

A project does not become valuable because it contains the maximum number of features.

It becomes valuable when it achieves its purpose effectively.

This lesson also influenced my academic work.

When studying a difficult subject, I sometimes wanted to understand everything immediately.

That is impossible.

Trying to master an entire field at once creates unnecessary pressure.

I began dividing large subjects into smaller questions.

Instead of trying to "learn machine learning," I would study one concept.

Instead of trying to "become good at algorithms," I would focus on one class of problems.

Instead of trying to understand an entire software architecture, I would trace one component at a time.

This approach made difficult subjects feel more manageable.

It also taught me something important about depth.

There is a difference between being exposed to many subjects and understanding them.

Watching a tutorial does not mean mastering a technology.

Building a small project does not mean understanding an entire field.

Knowing the name of an algorithm does not mean understanding its trade-offs.

I began to value depth more.

Breadth gives me curiosity.

Depth gives me capability.

I want both.

But I now understand that they must be developed at different times.

Sometimes I need to explore widely.

Sometimes I need to stop exploring and go deeper.

Knowing when to switch between those modes is part of becoming a better learner.

This has become particularly important in artificial intelligence.

AI is an enormous field.

There is computer vision, natural language processing, reinforcement learning, generative models, robotics, multimodal systems, recommendation systems, AI agents, and many other areas.

It would be impossible to master all of them simultaneously.

Instead of trying, I have learned to follow a smaller set of questions that genuinely interest me.

How can intelligent systems interact with people?

How can AI become more useful in real-world applications?

How can multiple systems cooperate?

How can we make AI reliable rather than merely impressive?

These questions give direction to my learning.

They help me decide which technologies are worth exploring deeply and which can remain areas of general awareness.

Prioritization has also changed the way I think about time.

Time is one of the few resources that cannot be recovered.

A computer can gain more storage.

A company can raise more money.

A project can receive additional funding.

But yesterday cannot be recovered.

This realization made me more conscious of how I spend my time.

I began noticing how easily small distractions can consume large amounts of time.

Ten minutes of checking something can become an hour.

One interesting video can become a long chain of unrelated content.

A small change to a project can become an entire evening.

Technology gives us enormous access to information, but access does not automatically produce learning.

Learning requires attention.

That is increasingly important in a world where information is constantly competing for it.

I have therefore started thinking about attention as a resource.

What deserves my attention?

What can wait?

What is genuinely helping me learn?

What am I doing simply because it is easy?

These questions have helped me become more intentional.

I am not perfectly disciplined.

I still get distracted.

I still start things that I later abandon.

I still underestimate how long some tasks will take.

But I now recognize these patterns more quickly.

That awareness itself is progress.

Self-management is not about creating a perfect schedule and following it without exception.

It is about understanding how I work and designing conditions that help me work better.

I have discovered that I learn best when I can connect theory to something practical.

If I study an algorithm, I want to implement it.

If I learn about a machine learning technique, I want to experiment with it.

If I read about a system, I want to understand how I could build a simplified version.

Practical work gives abstract ideas somewhere to go.

At the same time, I have learned that projects cannot replace fundamentals.

There were times when I wanted to skip theoretical material and immediately build something.

Eventually, I would reach a problem that I could not solve because I lacked the underlying knowledge.

That experience taught me to respect fundamentals.

Theory and practice are not competitors.

They strengthen each other.

Theory helps me understand what I am building.

Projects help me understand why the theory matters.

I want my college education to combine both.

I want rigorous courses that challenge my understanding.

I also want opportunities to apply those concepts through research, projects, competitions, and collaboration.

Another lesson I have learned about time is that meaningful work often takes longer than expected.

Modern technology creates the illusion of speed.

A tool can generate code instantly.

A model can produce an answer in seconds.

A tutorial can explain a concept in minutes.

But understanding remains slower.

You cannot always compress deep learning into a shortcut.

Sometimes a concept needs to be revisited several times.

Sometimes a project needs weeks of iteration.

Sometimes a research question requires months before the right approach becomes clear.

I have become more comfortable with that pace.

I no longer believe that learning slowly means learning badly.

Some of the concepts I understand best are the ones I struggled with initially.

The struggle forced me to build a stronger foundation.

This has changed my expectations for college.

I know I will encounter subjects that cannot be mastered overnight.

There will be difficult mathematics.

There will be complex algorithms.

There will be technical papers that require multiple readings.

There will be projects that take longer than expected.

Instead of looking for the fastest possible path through everything, I want to learn how to stay with important problems.

That ability to persist is something I value more than speed.

Speed is useful.

But direction matters more.

You can move quickly and still go nowhere.

I have experienced this personally.

There were periods when I was learning many things at once and felt extremely busy.

Looking back, I realized that I had accumulated activity without enough progress.

That experience taught me to distinguish between motion and movement.

Motion is doing things.

Movement is doing things that take you closer to something meaningful.

This distinction now influences how I set goals.

I try to ask not only what I want to accomplish but why.

If I want to learn a technology, what will I use it for?

If I want to build a project, what problem will it solve?

If I want to participate in a competition, what do I want to learn from it?

If I want to study a subject, how will it strengthen the direction I am pursuing?

These questions help me avoid collecting experiences simply for the sake of having them.

They also help me think about my future more clearly.

I know that I want to work in technology.

I am particularly interested in artificial intelligence and software engineering.

I enjoy building systems and exploring new technical ideas.

But I also want a career that allows me to continue learning.

The technology industry changes too quickly for a person to stop learning after graduation.

The education I receive in college will therefore not be the final stage of my learning.

It will be preparation for a lifetime of learning.

That is another reason I care about fundamentals.

A specific framework may become obsolete.

The ability to understand new frameworks will not.

A particular AI model may be replaced.

The mathematical and computational principles behind learning systems will remain useful.

A specific programming language may become less popular.

The ability to reason about algorithms and systems will remain valuable.

I want to develop those durable abilities.

I also want to protect my curiosity.

Prioritization should not mean becoming rigid.

If I become so focused on one goal that I never explore anything unexpected, I could miss important opportunities.

Some of my interests have emerged from completely unexpected discoveries.

A random article can lead to a research question.

A conversation can lead to a project.

A failed experiment can lead to a new field of interest.

Curiosity requires room for those moments.

The challenge is balancing exploration with commitment.

I have begun thinking of my learning as having two modes.

There is exploration, where I follow questions and discover possibilities.

Then there is execution, where I choose one possibility and commit to developing it.

Both are necessary.

Exploration without execution produces endless ideas.

Execution without exploration can produce work without imagination.

I want to become good at moving between them.

That balance will matter in college.

I want to explore different research areas before choosing deeper specialization.

I want to meet people from different disciplines.

I want to experiment with technologies I have never used.

But once I find a problem worth pursuing, I want the discipline to stay with it.

That is the kind of growth I am looking for.

Not simply becoming someone who knows more.

Becoming someone who can choose better.

Choosing which questions deserve attention.

Choosing which projects deserve time.

Choosing which ideas should be tested.

Choosing when to continue.

Choosing when to stop.

Choosing when to simplify.

These decisions are part of engineering, but they are also part of life.

Perhaps that is the most important lesson I have learned from having too many interests.

I used to think the challenge was finding enough time to do everything I wanted.

Now I understand that there will never be enough time to do everything.

The real challenge is deciding what matters enough to deserve the time I have.

That realization has made my goals more focused.

I want to develop deep expertise in computer science and artificial intelligence.

I want to build useful systems.

I want to participate in meaningful research.

I want to collaborate with people who challenge my thinking.

I want to continue exploring science and technology.

And I want to remain curious beyond the requirements of a course or a career.

There will always be another technology to learn.

Another project to start.

Another idea to explore.

Another problem to solve.

I cannot pursue all of them.

But I can choose the ones that matter most.

That is what I am learning now.

Discipline is not the opposite of curiosity.

It is what allows curiosity to become something real.

Curiosity gives me possibilities.

Discipline helps me choose one.

Focus allows me to develop it.

Reflection tells me whether it was worth pursuing.

Then curiosity begins again.

That cycle is how I want to continue learning.

I do not want to become the person who has done everything.

I want to become the person who has done a few meaningful things deeply, learned from them honestly, and remained curious enough to begin something new.

There will always be more things I could do.

More skills I could learn.

More projects I could build.

More technologies I could explore.

But my time is limited.

So I am learning to choose.

And I am beginning to understand that choosing what matters is not giving up possibilities.

It is how possibilities become achievements.
