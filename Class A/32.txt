
The world in which students begin their education is not necessarily the world in which they will build their careers. Technologies change, industries evolve, and new forms of work emerge continuously. Skills that are highly valuable today may be replaced or transformed tomorrow. This reality has shaped my understanding of education. I no longer see education simply as preparation for a particular job. I see it as preparation for continuous change.

My interest in computer science has made this idea especially clear to me. Technology develops at a remarkable pace. Programming frameworks change, artificial intelligence advances, cloud platforms evolve, and new tools appear regularly. Trying to predict every technology I will use throughout my career would therefore be unrealistic. Instead, I want to develop the ability to understand new technologies, adapt to changing circumstances, and continue learning throughout my professional life.

This belief has gradually become one of the central principles of my academic journey.

When I first became interested in computers, I thought learning a technology meant becoming familiar with its commands and features. If I learned a programming language, I believed I had learned programming. If I learned a framework, I believed I had learned software development.

With experience, I realised that this understanding was incomplete.

Programming is not the same as knowing the syntax of a particular language.

Software engineering is not the same as knowing a particular framework.

Artificial intelligence is not the same as knowing how to call an AI model.

The deeper skill is understanding principles that remain useful even when specific technologies change.

This realisation changed the way I learn.

Instead of trying to memorise every tool, I began focusing more strongly on fundamentals. Algorithms, data structures, mathematics, logical reasoning, software architecture, databases, and problem-solving principles provide knowledge that can be transferred across technologies.

If a framework changes, the underlying software engineering principles remain.

If a programming language changes, computational thinking remains.

If a machine learning library changes, the mathematics behind the model remains.

This gives fundamental knowledge a lasting value.

I want my higher education to strengthen these foundations.

At the same time, I do not want to become so focused on fundamentals that I ignore new developments. Adaptability requires both depth and openness.

A strong foundation provides stability.

Curiosity provides movement.

Together, they allow a person to adapt without losing direction.

My experiences with different technologies have taught me this balance.

When I encounter a new tool, I try to understand what problem it is designed to solve. I then compare it with approaches I already know.

What is different?

What is improved?

What are the limitations?

Where is the technology useful?

Where is it unnecessary?

This approach helps me learn more effectively because I am not studying a tool in isolation.

I am connecting it to existing knowledge.

This has become particularly important with artificial intelligence.

The field has developed rapidly, with new models, architectures, tools, and applications appearing frequently. It would be impossible to build a career around one specific technology without expecting that technology to evolve.

Therefore, I want to understand the principles behind AI.

I want to understand how models learn from data.

I want to understand how algorithms are evaluated.

I want to understand optimisation.

I want to understand probability and statistics.

I want to understand how data influences model behaviour.

I want to understand the limitations of intelligent systems.

These foundations will allow me to adapt when new methods emerge.

Adaptability, however, is not only a technical skill.

It is also a mindset.

A person can know many technologies and still struggle when circumstances change.

Adaptability requires willingness to accept that previous knowledge may no longer be sufficient.

This can be uncomfortable.

People naturally become comfortable with familiar methods. Once a particular workflow becomes efficient, changing it can seem unnecessary.

However, technological progress often requires exactly this willingness to change.

A better method may initially feel unfamiliar.

A new tool may require time to learn.

An improved process may challenge habits that have been established over years.

An adaptable person must be willing to go through that temporary discomfort.

I have experienced this while learning new technologies.

Whenever I move into an unfamiliar area, there is an initial period in which progress feels slow. Concepts that seem obvious to experienced developers can be confusing to beginners. Documentation may contain unfamiliar terminology. Errors may not immediately make sense.

The easiest response would be to return to something familiar.

Instead, I have learned to treat the unfamiliar period as part of learning.

At first, I may not understand.

Then I understand individual pieces.

Eventually, those pieces connect.

Finally, I can apply the knowledge independently.

This process has made me more confident about entering unfamiliar technical areas.

I no longer expect to understand everything immediately.

I expect to learn.

This distinction is important.

Confidence does not have to mean believing that I already know the answer.

It can mean believing that I am capable of learning what I do not know.

I consider this a more sustainable form of confidence.

It will also be important in higher education.

University will expose me to subjects that are more advanced than those I have studied independently. Some concepts may initially appear difficult. Some may require mathematical preparation. Others may require approaches to problem solving that are unfamiliar to me.

I want to approach these challenges with curiosity rather than fear.

If I do not understand a topic, I want to identify what I am missing.

If my first approach fails, I want to try another.

If I receive criticism, I want to use it to improve.

If another student understands something better than I do, I want to learn from them.

Adaptability requires humility because learning often begins by accepting that there is something we do not know.

This is particularly important in collaborative environments.

Modern engineering is rarely performed by one person working independently.

Teams bring together people with different expertise.

One person may understand machine learning deeply.

Another may be skilled in system architecture.

Another may have stronger knowledge of user experience.

Another may understand the business or social context.

The team becomes stronger when members are willing to learn from one another.

I want to develop this ability during university.

I want to be comfortable being both a contributor and a learner.

Sometimes I may be the person with the strongest technical knowledge in a particular area.

At other times, someone else may know significantly more.

Both situations are valuable.

When I know more, I can contribute.

When someone else knows more, I can learn.

This attitude creates a healthier environment for collaboration.

Adaptability also means being able to change direction when evidence requires it.

This is particularly important in projects.

A project may begin with a clear plan.

After implementation starts, new information may appear.

A particular technology may not perform as expected.

Users may provide unexpected feedback.

The available data may be insufficient.

The original design may prove difficult to scale.

At this point, continuing with the original plan simply because it was the original plan may be inefficient.

The team may need to change its approach.

I have learned that changing direction does not necessarily mean failure.

Sometimes it means learning.

A project plan is a hypothesis about what will work.

Implementation provides evidence.

If the evidence contradicts the plan, the plan should change.

This way of thinking has made me more comfortable with iteration.

It has also strengthened my interest in research.

Research is fundamentally uncertain.

A researcher begins with an idea but cannot know exactly what the evidence will show.

A hypothesis may be supported.

It may be rejected.

A completely unexpected result may emerge.

The researcher must adapt to the evidence.

This is one of the reasons I believe research can develop strong intellectual flexibility.

It teaches a person to separate personal expectations from observed results.

If I strongly believe that a particular approach should work, I may be disappointed when it fails.

But the failure can be informative.

It tells me something about the limits of the approach.

It may suggest a new hypothesis.

It may reveal a hidden variable.

It may indicate that the original question needs to be reconsidered.

Adaptability turns uncertainty into opportunity.

This mindset is especially valuable in artificial intelligence.

AI models can behave differently when conditions change.

A model trained on one dataset may not generalise to another.

A system developed in a laboratory may perform differently in the real world.

Data distributions can change.

User behaviour can change.

The environment can change.

Therefore, AI systems need to be evaluated not only under ideal conditions but also under changing conditions.

This has made me interested in robust and reliable AI.

I want to understand how systems can remain useful when the environment is not perfectly predictable.

I want to study how models can be evaluated for generalisation.

I want to understand how uncertainty can be represented.

I want to explore methods that make systems more efficient and adaptable.

These questions connect technical research with practical engineering.

They also have important implications for India.

India's technological environment is diverse and constantly evolving. Digital services must operate across different levels of infrastructure, language preferences, economic conditions, and technological familiarity.

A system designed under one set of assumptions may need to adapt before it can be widely useful.

This creates an important opportunity for engineering.

Rather than assuming that every user operates under ideal conditions, developers can design systems that are flexible.

For example, applications can be designed to work under limited connectivity.

Interfaces can support multiple languages.

Systems can be optimised for different levels of hardware.

AI models can be evaluated across diverse datasets.

These are examples of adaptability at the system level.

I want to understand how such design principles can be incorporated into technology from the beginning.

This connects adaptability with accessibility.

A flexible system can serve more people.

If technology is designed around one narrow user group, others may struggle to use it.

If designers consider different needs early, the final system can become more inclusive.

This is one reason I believe adaptability should be considered an engineering principle rather than merely a personal quality.

Systems should adapt to changing requirements.

Engineers should adapt to changing technologies.

Organisations should adapt to changing markets.

Researchers should adapt to new evidence.

Students should adapt to new knowledge.

All of these forms of adaptability are connected.

My own academic journey has taught me that learning does not always follow a straight line.

There have been subjects that initially interested me but later became less important.

There have been technologies that I expected to use but eventually moved away from.

There have been areas that I did not initially understand but later became interested in.

These changes are not necessarily signs of inconsistency.

Perhaps a research project will introduce me to a new area.

Perhaps an elective course will change my perspective.

Perhaps working with another discipline will reveal an application of AI that I have never considered.

I want to allow these experiences to influence my development.

Having a long-term goal does not mean every step must be predetermined.

A direction is useful.

Flexibility is necessary.

Research ability.

Critical thinking.

Continuous learning.

These qualities can support different career paths.

This is why I see higher education as preparation for adaptability rather than preparation for one fixed occupation.

A degree should provide a foundation from which a person can continue developing.

It should teach students how to think, investigate, communicate, and learn.

Technical knowledge is essential, but the ability to acquire new knowledge may be even more valuable over a long career.

This is especially true in computer science.

A programming language learned today may not be central to my work years later.

But the ability to understand programming concepts will remain useful.

A particular machine learning architecture may eventually be replaced.

I also want to develop the ability to recognise when change is necessary.

Not every new technology is automatically better.

The appearance of something new should not be enough reason to adopt it.

A responsible engineer should evaluate whether it actually solves the problem better.

Does it improve performance?

Does it reduce cost?

Does it increase reliability?

Does it improve accessibility?

Does it introduce new risks?

Does the organisation have the resources to maintain it?

These questions prevent change from becoming change for its own sake.

Adaptability does not mean accepting every new idea.

It means being willing to evaluate new ideas fairly.

This distinction is important in artificial intelligence.

AI is developing rapidly, and new tools appear continuously.

It can be tempting to adopt every new model simply because it is more recent.

However, a newer system may not always be the best solution for a particular application.

Sometimes a smaller model may be more efficient.

Sometimes a simpler algorithm may be easier to maintain.

Sometimes a non-AI approach may be more appropriate.

Good engineering requires judgment.

I want to develop that judgment.

This mindset gives me a sense of direction even when circumstances are uncertain.

I want to carry this into university and my future career.

Reflection converts experience into knowledge.
