
My strongest academic habit did not begin with a textbook, a classroom, or an examination. It began with a question.

Why?

Why does a system behave in a particular way?

Why does a program produce one result instead of another?

Why does a technology that works well for one person fail for someone else?

Why does a simple idea sometimes require a complicated solution?

Why does an apparently successful system fail under different conditions?

I have gradually realised that my habit of asking "why" has influenced almost every important part of my education. It has shaped my interest in computer science, artificial intelligence, research, and engineering. More importantly, it has changed the way I approach learning. I no longer want to learn only what something does. I want to understand why it works.

This distinction became clear when I first started learning programming.

At the beginning, programming seemed like a collection of instructions. I learned syntax, wrote small programs, and tried to reproduce examples from tutorials. When a program worked, I was satisfied.

But eventually I began encountering situations where simply following examples was not enough.

A program could produce an unexpected output.

A small change could cause a different behaviour.

Two approaches could solve the same problem but perform differently.

I began asking why.

Why did one approach require more time?

Why did one solution use more memory?

Why did a particular error occur?

Why did changing one part of the program affect another?

These questions pushed me beyond memorisation.

I began looking for underlying principles.

This made learning slower in some situations, but deeper.

Instead of remembering one solution, I could understand the reasoning behind it.

If I understood the principle, I could apply it to a new problem.

That became one of the most valuable lessons of my education.

Knowledge becomes more useful when it can be transferred.

A student who memorises one algorithm can solve one familiar problem.

A student who understands why the algorithm works can recognise situations where the same idea may apply.

This is why I value conceptual understanding.

Computer science is particularly suited to this way of thinking.

Algorithms are not simply collections of steps.

They represent ways of approaching problems.

Data structures are not simply programming features.

They provide different ways of organising information depending on what operations are important.

Programming languages are not merely collections of syntax.

They provide different abstractions for expressing computation.

Understanding these ideas allows a student to move beyond individual tools.

This matters because technology changes rapidly.

A programming framework learned today may be replaced by another tomorrow.

A particular library may become obsolete.

An AI model may be replaced by a newer architecture.

But the underlying ability to understand problems remains valuable.

If I understand principles, I can learn new tools.

That is why I want my education to focus strongly on fundamentals.

My curiosity became even stronger when I encountered artificial intelligence.

AI systems can produce outputs that sometimes seem surprising.

A model can recognise patterns that are difficult for humans to describe explicitly.

It can make predictions from large amounts of data.

But this raises questions.

Why does the model make this prediction?

What information influenced it?

How reliable is the prediction?

What happens when the input changes?

What kinds of data were used to train it?

What does the model fail to understand?

These questions make AI intellectually fascinating to me.

The more I learn, the more questions appear.

That is one of the characteristics I value most about the field.

Learning does not eliminate curiosity.

It creates better questions.

When I knew little about machine learning, I was mostly interested in making models work.

After learning more, I became interested in how models generalise.

Then I became interested in data quality.

Then evaluation.

Then bias.

Then interpretability.

Then efficiency.

Each answer created another question.

I do not see this as a sign that learning is incomplete.

I see it as evidence that knowledge has expanded.

This is how I want to approach higher education.

I do not expect university to provide every answer.

I want it to teach me how to ask better questions.

A good professor may answer a student's question.

An excellent professor may help the student discover a deeper question behind it.

I want to experience that kind of learning.

Research particularly appeals to me for this reason.

Research begins where certainty ends.

There is something we do not fully understand.

The researcher asks a question.

They examine existing knowledge.

They develop a hypothesis or research direction.

They design experiments.

They analyse evidence.

They revise their understanding.

The process is driven by curiosity.

I want to participate in that process.

I want to experience the difference between learning an established concept and investigating something that is not yet fully understood.

This requires patience.

Research questions rarely have immediate answers.

Experiments may fail.

Data may be noisy.

Results may be inconclusive.

A hypothesis may be wrong.

But each outcome can provide information.

The researcher continues asking questions.

This is similar to debugging.

When a program fails, the useful question is not simply, "How do I make the error disappear?"

It is, "Why did this happen?"

The same mindset can be applied to research.

Why did the experiment produce this result?

Why did the model perform differently on another dataset?

Why did the expected relationship not appear?

Curiosity turns failure into investigation.

I want to maintain that habit.

Curiosity also changes how I learn outside formal education.

When I encounter a new technology, I want to understand how it works.

When I read about a new AI system, I want to know what makes it different.

When I see an interesting product, I wonder how its architecture might be designed.

When I encounter a difficult problem, I want to understand its underlying structure.

The internet provides enormous opportunities for this kind of independent learning.

Documentation, research papers, tutorials, lectures, technical communities, and open-source projects allow students to explore beyond classroom material.

I want to use these resources actively.

However, independent learning also requires judgment.

Not every source is equally reliable.

A tutorial may simplify a concept too much.

A social media post may contain incorrect information.

A code example may work without explaining why.

A research claim may require closer examination.

Curiosity must therefore be combined with critical thinking.

I want to learn how to distinguish evidence from opinion.

I want to compare sources.

I want to verify technical claims.

I want to understand the assumptions behind conclusions.

This will be especially important in artificial intelligence.

The field changes quickly, and new claims appear constantly.

A model may be described as revolutionary.

A benchmark may show impressive results.

A new technique may receive significant attention.

But I want to ask what the evidence actually demonstrates.

What dataset was used?

What was the baseline?

How was the evaluation conducted?

What are the limitations?

Does the result generalise?

These questions help separate genuine progress from excitement.

Curiosity therefore protects against passive acceptance.

It encourages investigation.

This is one reason I believe curiosity is an academic strength.

It makes a student less satisfied with incomplete explanations.

However, curiosity must also be disciplined.

It is possible to ask endless questions without completing anything.

A student can move from one interesting topic to another without developing depth.

I have learned that curiosity needs direction.

When I encounter an interesting question, I want to determine whether it is worth investigating deeply.

What would I learn?

What resources are available?

Can I test the idea?

Does it connect to my goals?

Can I complete the investigation within my available time?

These questions help turn curiosity into productive learning.

This balance between exploration and focus will be important in university.

I want to explore new areas without losing sight of my main academic direction.

Computer science and artificial intelligence will remain my foundation.

Within that foundation, I want to explore research areas that interest me.

I may investigate machine learning.

I may explore natural language processing.

I may work with computer vision.

I may study intelligent systems.

I may discover an area I have not yet considered.

I want to remain open.

Curiosity also influences how I approach mathematics.

Mathematics can sometimes seem difficult when it is taught only as a set of procedures.

But when I understand why a mathematical relationship exists, the subject becomes more meaningful.

For example, probability is not simply a collection of formulas.

It provides a framework for reasoning about uncertainty.

Statistics is not simply calculation.

It provides tools for understanding data.

Linear algebra is not merely matrix manipulation.

It provides mathematical structures that appear throughout machine learning.

Optimisation is not simply an algorithmic technique.

It provides methods for finding better solutions under constraints.

Understanding these connections makes mathematics more interesting.

I want to develop stronger mathematical foundations because they will help me understand AI more deeply.

Rather than treating mathematics as something separate from computer science, I want to understand how the two interact.

This is another example of why I ask questions.

Why is this equation useful?

Why does this method converge?

Why does changing this parameter affect the result?

Why does this representation make the problem easier?

Questions transform formulas into concepts.

Curiosity also helps me understand people.

Technology is ultimately created for people, so understanding their experiences matters.

When someone struggles with an application, I want to know why.

Is the interface confusing?

Is the language unclear?

Is the process too long?

Is the user unfamiliar with the technology?

Is the system failing to meet their actual need?

These questions can lead to better design.

This is why human-centred engineering interests me.

A developer should not assume that the user's experience is obvious.

They should investigate it.

Ask questions.

Observe behaviour.

Collect feedback.

Test alternatives.

Improve.

Curiosity therefore supports empathy.

It encourages us to ask what another person is experiencing rather than assuming we already know.

This is especially important when designing technology for diverse communities.

Different users may interact with the same system differently.

A feature that is convenient for one group may be difficult for another.

A language assumption may exclude users.

A hardware requirement may create an access barrier.

A complex interface may discourage beginners.

Curiosity encourages engineers to notice these differences.

Why is this user struggling?

What assumption did we make?

How could the system adapt?

I want to develop this perspective.

Curiosity also supports creativity.

When a person asks whether something can be done differently, they create the possibility of innovation.

Many technological improvements begin with questions that challenge existing assumptions.

Can this process be faster?

Can this device use less energy?

Can this system be more accessible?

Can this model require less data?

Can this interface be simpler?

Can this repetitive task be automated?

These questions do not guarantee useful innovations.

But without questions, existing systems remain unchallenged.

I want to develop the courage to ask them.

At the same time, curiosity should not mean rejecting existing knowledge simply because it is established.

Established methods often exist for good reasons.

A mature learner asks why something is done a certain way before deciding whether it should change.

They examine the evidence.

They understand the constraints.

They compare alternatives.

Only then do they propose improvements.

This is another reason I value fundamentals.

Understanding existing methods makes meaningful innovation more likely.

Curiosity creates these exchanges.

I would rather admit that I do not know something and learn it.
