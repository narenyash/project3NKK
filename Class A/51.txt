
Many problems do not announce themselves as problems. They appear as small inconveniences, repeated mistakes, unnecessary delays, confusing processes, or tasks that people simply accept because they have become accustomed to them. I have gradually developed an interest in noticing these situations and asking whether they can be improved. This habit of observation has become one of the strongest influences on my interest in engineering.

My attraction to computer science began with curiosity about technology, but it developed further when I started seeing technology as a tool for solving practical problems.

Programming initially interested me because I could give instructions to a computer and see a result. I enjoyed experimenting with small programs and understanding how different commands affected the output. Over time, however, I became less interested in building something merely because I could and more interested in building something because someone could benefit from it.

This distinction changed the way I approached projects.

Instead of beginning with a technology, I began trying to understand a problem first.

Who experiences the problem?

What makes it difficult?

How is it currently being handled?

Which part of the process is inefficient?

Can technology genuinely improve it?

These questions became the beginning of my problem-solving process.

Observation is often the first step.

A person cannot solve a problem they do not understand.

If a process appears inefficient, it is tempting to immediately automate it. But automation without understanding can simply make an inefficient process happen faster.

For example, if an organisation has difficulty managing information, creating another application may not automatically solve the issue. The real problem may be that information is collected inconsistently, that responsibilities are unclear, or that users do not know where to find it.

Therefore, I have learned that the first responsibility of an engineer is not always to build.

Sometimes it is to observe.

This has influenced how I approach software projects.

When developing an application, I want to understand how people currently perform the task.

What steps do they follow?

Which steps take the most time?

Where do mistakes happen?

What information do they need?

Which features would actually make the process easier?

These questions help distinguish genuine requirements from assumptions.

I believe this approach is especially important in today's technology environment because building software has become easier.

Modern frameworks, cloud platforms, libraries, and AI tools allow developers to create applications rapidly.

The challenge is no longer simply whether something can be built.

The challenge is whether it should be built and whether it solves the right problem.

This is one reason I am interested in human-centred engineering.

Technology should begin with people.

A technically impressive system that nobody needs has limited value.

A simple system that solves a real problem can be much more meaningful.

This principle has influenced my interest in artificial intelligence.

AI can perform impressive tasks.

It can analyse data, recognise patterns, generate content, and assist decision-making.

But the existence of a capability does not automatically justify its use.

Before applying AI, I want to ask whether AI is actually necessary.

Could a simpler algorithm solve the problem?

Would a rule-based approach be more reliable?

Does the available data support machine learning?

What happens when the model is wrong?

Will users understand its output?

These questions help prevent technology from becoming the objective instead of the tool.

Problem-solving therefore requires judgment.

Engineering is not simply about knowing many technologies.

It is about choosing the appropriate one.

This is a skill I want to strengthen through higher education.

I want to study computer science deeply enough to understand the strengths and limitations of different approaches.

Algorithms can provide efficient solutions.

Databases can organise information.

Distributed systems can support large-scale applications.

Machine learning can identify patterns.

Cloud computing can provide scalable infrastructure.

Each technology has a purpose.

A good engineer needs to understand when to use each one.

Observation also helps identify opportunities for innovation.

Many useful ideas begin with ordinary situations.

A repeated task may suggest automation.

A common source of confusion may suggest better interface design.

A recurring error may suggest improved validation.

A difficult manual process may suggest a digital system.

The important skill is noticing patterns.

Once a problem occurs repeatedly, it becomes worth investigating.

This does not mean every inconvenience requires an application.

Sometimes the simplest solution is a process change.

Sometimes better training is needed.

Sometimes communication is the problem.

Sometimes technology would introduce more complexity than it removes.

A good problem solver must be willing to reach these conclusions.

This is one of the reasons I want to develop interdisciplinary knowledge.

A technical solution may not always be the best solution.

Understanding business, education, psychology, economics, or environmental science can help identify alternatives.

Real-world problems do not exist inside computer science textbooks.

They exist within systems involving people, resources, institutions, and environments.

An engineer must understand enough of that context to make useful decisions.

This is particularly relevant in India.

The country has enormous diversity in infrastructure, technology access, languages, industries, and economic conditions.

A solution that works in one environment may not work in another.

For example, an application designed for high-speed internet may perform poorly in areas with limited connectivity.

A complex digital service may be difficult for first-time users.

An English-only interface may limit accessibility.

A system requiring expensive hardware may be unrealistic for resource-constrained environments.

These are not merely technical edge cases.

They can determine whether a solution is useful.

Therefore, problem-solving requires understanding constraints.

Constraints are often what make engineering interesting.

If resources were unlimited, many problems would be easier.

Real systems have limitations.

There may be limited computing power.

Limited bandwidth.

Limited budgets.

Limited time.

Limited data.

Limited personnel.

The engineer must work within these constraints.

This requires creativity.

A limitation can sometimes lead to a better solution.

For example, a system designed to operate with limited connectivity may become more efficient overall.

A model designed for lower computational requirements may become easier to deploy.

A simple interface may become easier for everyone to use.

Constraints can therefore encourage thoughtful design.

I want to become comfortable working within them.

Problem-solving also requires defining success.

A solution cannot be evaluated without knowing what improvement means.

If the goal is to reduce processing time, we need to measure time.

If the goal is to improve accuracy, we need appropriate evaluation metrics.

If the goal is to make an application easier to use, user feedback may be necessary.

If the goal is to reduce resource consumption, we need to measure resource usage.

This is where quantitative thinking becomes important.

Computer science provides tools for measurement.

Statistics provides methods for understanding data.

Research methodology provides ways to evaluate hypotheses.

I want to strengthen these skills.

Artificial intelligence makes evaluation particularly important.

A model can appear successful through a few examples while failing under broader testing.

Therefore, I want to understand validation carefully.

What data was used?

How representative is it?

What metrics were chosen?

What are the failure cases?

How does performance change under different conditions?

These questions can reveal whether a system actually solves the intended problem.

Problem-solving therefore involves more than producing an answer.

It involves verifying that the answer works.

This principle also applies to software engineering.

A feature is not complete simply because the code has been written.

It needs to be tested.

It needs to work with expected inputs.

It needs to handle unexpected inputs.

It needs to interact correctly with other components.

It needs to remain maintainable.

It needs to meet the actual requirement.

This broader definition of completion has changed how I think about development.

Writing code is only one part of engineering.

Understanding requirements, designing systems, testing, deploying, monitoring, and improving are equally important.

I want to develop competence across this process.

Observation also plays an important role in debugging.

When a system fails, the error message is only one piece of information.

The engineer needs to observe what happened.

What was the system doing immediately before the failure?

What input caused it?

Does the problem happen consistently?

Does it occur only under certain conditions?

Was anything changed recently?

These observations can narrow the possibilities.

Debugging is therefore a form of investigation.

I enjoy this aspect of programming because it combines logic with curiosity.

The goal is not simply to guess the fix.

It is to identify the underlying cause.

This mindset can be applied beyond software.

Whenever something does not work as expected, the same process can be useful.

Observe.

Define.

Hypothesise.

Test.

Analyse.

Improve.

This is essentially a scientific approach to everyday problem-solving.

I want to develop it as a habit.

Another important lesson is that problems often have multiple layers.

A visible problem may be only a symptom.

For example, if employees repeatedly enter incorrect information into a system, the obvious response may be additional validation.

But perhaps the interface is confusing.

Perhaps the instructions are unclear.

Perhaps the workflow requires unnecessary repetition.

Perhaps users were never trained.

The deeper problem may be process design rather than user behaviour.

Finding the root cause can produce a better solution.

This is why I want to avoid treating symptoms without understanding causes.

Root-cause analysis is valuable in both engineering and management.

It can prevent teams from repeatedly solving the same problem.

I want to develop this analytical habit.

Problem-solving also requires asking good questions.

A vague question produces vague answers.

"What is wrong with the system?" may be too broad.

"At which step does the process begin failing?" is more useful.

"Why does this error occur only for certain inputs?" is more specific.

"What changed between the working and failing versions?" narrows the investigation.

The quality of the question can determine the efficiency of the investigation.

This is another reason curiosity and problem-solving are connected.

Curiosity generates questions.

Analysis makes them precise.

Experimentation tests them.

Evidence determines the next step.

I want to strengthen each part of this process.

I also want to become comfortable with problems that do not have a single correct answer.

A faster system may cost more.

A cheaper system may be less scalable.

A highly accurate model may require more computation.

A simple system may be easier to maintain but less sophisticated.

A feature-rich application may become harder to use.

There is often no perfect option.

The engineer must decide which trade-offs matter most.

This requires understanding the priorities of the problem.

What matters most?

Performance?

Cost?

Reliability?

Accessibility?

Security?

Maintainability?

Energy efficiency?

The answer depends on the context.

I want to learn how to make these decisions systematically.

Problem-solving also requires communication.

A technically correct solution may fail if stakeholders do not understand it.

An engineer may identify a problem but struggle to explain why it matters.

A researcher may have strong evidence but present it unclearly.

A team may have a good idea but fail to coordinate implementation.

Communication connects problem identification with action.

I want to develop the ability to explain technical decisions in clear language.

This will help me work with both technical and non-technical people.

I also want to learn how to listen.

A user may describe a problem in non-technical language.

Instead of immediately translating it into a technical solution, I should first understand what they mean.

Their explanation may reveal an underlying issue.

Listening is therefore part of problem analysis.

This is especially important when designing systems for communities.

The people experiencing a problem often understand its practical details better than an outside developer.

Their input can reveal constraints that technical teams may overlook.

I want to involve users whenever possible.

This approach can also prevent wasted effort.

Building a solution without validating the problem can consume significant time.

A short conversation with users may reveal that the proposed feature is unnecessary.

Testing an early prototype can reveal that the interface is confusing.

These discoveries are valuable because they happen before large resources are invested.

I want to learn how to prototype and validate ideas early.

This is one reason I am interested in entrepreneurship and innovation.

A strong product begins with a problem worth solving.

It then requires validation, design, implementation, testing, and iteration.

The technology is only one part of the process.

This broader perspective will be valuable if I eventually build products of my own.
