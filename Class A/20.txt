
One of the first things I noticed about software is how strange its existence can be.

A program can be created by one person and eventually used by thousands.

An idea can begin as a small experiment and become part of someone else's work.

A piece of code written late at night can solve a problem for someone the developer will never meet.

A research paper can be read years after its authors have moved on to other questions.

This made me think about a question that has become increasingly important to me:

**What do we leave behind when we build something?**

At first, I thought building was primarily about the moment of creation.

You have an idea.

You write the code.

You solve the bugs.

You deploy the application.

You see it working.

The project feels complete.

But software rarely ends when the developer stops writing code.

Someone has to use it.

Someone has to maintain it.

Someone has to understand it.

Someone may eventually modify it.

Sometimes someone else will build something new on top of it.

That means every system has a life beyond its creator.

This realization changed the way I think about engineering.

I began to understand that writing software is also writing for the future.

A function I create today may be read by another developer months later.

A database structure may influence how an application evolves.

An API may become a dependency for another system.

A technical decision that seems minor during development can become difficult to change later.

This makes software engineering feel less like writing instructions and more like building infrastructure.

The decisions we make today can shape what becomes possible tomorrow.

That idea has influenced how I approach my projects.

Earlier, I focused primarily on making the application work.

If the feature functioned, I was satisfied.

Over time, I began asking additional questions.

Can someone else understand this code?

What happens if I am not available to fix it?

What happens when the data grows?

What happens when the requirements change?

What happens when another developer needs to add a feature?

What happens if a dependency becomes unavailable?

These questions forced me to think beyond the immediate result.

A working system is only the beginning.

A maintainable system is a different achievement.

This became particularly clear when I returned to older projects.

Code that seemed obvious when I wrote it could become surprisingly difficult to understand later.

I would look at a function and remember what I intended to do.

But the code itself did not always communicate that intention clearly.

That experience taught me something important.

Code is read far more often than it is written.

The few minutes saved by writing something quickly can create hours of difficulty later.

I began paying more attention to structure, naming, documentation, and simplicity.

Not because those things make a project look professional.

Because someone else may eventually need to understand what I built.

That "someone else" could even be me.

This realization extended beyond code.

I began thinking about documentation as a form of respect.

When I document a project clearly, I am making it easier for another person to learn from my work.

When I explain an architecture, I am reducing the amount of time someone else needs to spend reconstructing my decisions.

When I record why a particular approach was chosen, I preserve reasoning that might otherwise disappear.

Documentation therefore became more than an administrative task.

It became part of the contribution.

This idea connects strongly with my interest in research.

Research is one of humanity's most powerful ways of building on previous work.

A researcher studies what others have discovered.

They identify what remains uncertain.

They conduct experiments.

They publish their findings.

Another researcher reads the work.

Perhaps they challenge it.

Perhaps they reproduce it.

Perhaps they extend it.

Eventually, knowledge grows through this accumulation.

No single researcher owns the entire process.

Each person contributes a piece.

That model of progress appeals to me.

I do not expect to solve the biggest problems in computer science alone.

I want to contribute something useful to the larger body of knowledge.

That contribution might be a research finding.

It might be an algorithm.

It might be an open-source tool.

It might be a dataset.

It might be a system that other people can build upon.

The scale is less important to me than the usefulness.

This perspective has influenced my interest in open source.

Open-source software demonstrates an unusual idea about creation.

A person can release something publicly and allow other people to inspect it, modify it, improve it, and redistribute it.

The creator does not control every future use.

Instead, the work becomes part of a larger ecosystem.

That can be both exciting and uncomfortable.

Once something is released, other people may use it in ways the creator did not expect.

They may discover bugs.

They may propose changes.

They may disagree with design decisions.

They may create versions that look completely different.

The creator has to accept that the work no longer belongs entirely to them.

I find that idea valuable because it challenges the idea of individual achievement.

Technology is often presented through individual stories.

The brilliant programmer.

The visionary founder.

The researcher who made the breakthrough.

But almost every important technological achievement rests on thousands of previous contributions.

Programming languages exist because other people created them.

Libraries exist because developers shared tools.

Research exists because previous researchers published findings.

Infrastructure exists because engineers maintained it.

Knowledge is cumulative.

That means my own education is cumulative as well.

Everything I know is connected to something someone else taught, documented, discovered, or built.

Recognizing this makes me want to contribute rather than simply consume.

If I benefit from other people's knowledge, I want my own work to eventually become useful to someone else.

That does not mean every project needs to become famous.

Most contributions will not.

That is fine.

A small improvement can still matter.

A clear tutorial can help a beginner.

A useful library can save another developer time.

A well-designed dataset can help a researcher.

A carefully documented experiment can prevent someone else from repeating a mistake.

These contributions may be invisible.

But invisible does not mean insignificant.

This idea has changed how I define impact.

I used to associate impact with scale.

If something affected millions of people, it must be important.

Now I think impact can also be measured through depth.

Helping one person solve a problem they have struggled with for years can be significant.

Giving one student the confidence to continue learning can be significant.

Helping another developer understand a difficult concept can be significant.

Impact is not always visible in numbers.

Sometimes it exists in what another person is able to do because of something you created.

That idea motivates me.

I want to build things that increase other people's capabilities.

This is one reason I am interested in educational technology.

A good educational tool does not simply provide information.

It can help someone become capable of doing something independently.

If a student uses an application to understand a difficult concept and eventually no longer needs the application, that can be a success.

The goal is not dependence.

The goal is growth.

I want the systems I build to have that kind of effect.

I also think about this in relation to artificial intelligence.

AI systems can generate information quickly.

But the long-term value of AI may depend on whether it helps people become more capable or simply more dependent on automated answers.

I am interested in systems that support learning, decision-making, and creativity.

An AI assistant could help a researcher explore possibilities.

An educational system could adapt explanations to a student's needs.

A software tool could help developers understand complex code.

These systems can extend human capability.

But they should not remove the opportunity to understand.

That principle matters to me because knowledge becomes more valuable when it can be transferred.

If I solve a problem and nobody else can understand how I solved it, the solution has limited long-term value.

If I can explain the reasoning, another person can build upon it.

Teaching therefore became another form of engineering for me.

When I help someone understand a programming concept, I am not simply transferring information.

I am helping them develop a mental model.

If they later solve a different problem using that model, the original explanation has become something larger.

The knowledge continues.

This is one of the most satisfying parts of teaching.

You do not necessarily see the final outcome.

You may only see the first moment when something becomes clear.

But that moment can change what someone is capable of doing afterward.

I want to create more of those moments.

This also explains why I value communication.

A technical idea that cannot be communicated clearly is difficult to share.

A research result that cannot be reproduced is difficult to build upon.

A software system that cannot be understood is difficult to maintain.

A discovery that remains inaccessible may have less impact than it could have had.

Communication extends the life of an idea.

That is why I want to become a better technical writer, presenter, and teacher alongside becoming a better engineer.

I want to be able to explain complex systems without removing the important details.

I want to write documentation that another developer can actually follow.

I want to present research clearly.

I want to communicate limitations honestly.

And I want people to understand not only what I built but why I built it.

These skills will become increasingly important as technology becomes more complex.

The systems of the future will likely involve many people and many layers.

No individual will understand every component.

Collaboration will therefore depend on shared knowledge.

Good documentation and communication will become infrastructure themselves.

This is one reason I am attracted to software engineering as a career.

It combines individual creativity with collective progress.

I can work on a problem independently.

But the result becomes more valuable when others can use it.

I can design an architecture.

But it becomes stronger when other engineers can understand and improve it.

I can build a prototype.

But it becomes meaningful when someone uses it to solve a real problem.

That relationship between individual effort and collective progress is something I want to experience throughout my career.

My interest in research has a similar foundation.

Research rewards people who are willing to work on questions whose answers may not be immediately visible.

The final publication may contain only a few pages.

Behind those pages may be months of reading, experimentation, failed approaches, debugging, and revision.

The final result can appear clean.

The process is not.

I find that process meaningful because it creates something that can outlive the experiment itself.

A result becomes part of the scientific record.

Someone else can examine it.

They can challenge it.

They can extend it.

They can learn from it.

That is a powerful form of continuity.

It means that the work of one person can become the starting point for another.

This is how I want to think about my education.

I am not studying only for an examination.

I am building a foundation for future work.

The concepts I learn today may become tools for solving problems years from now.

The habits I develop now may influence the way I approach research later.

The projects I build now may teach me principles that become useful in much larger systems.

Education is therefore an investment in future possibilities.

I want to make that investment seriously.

I want strong foundations in computer science.

I want to understand algorithms, artificial intelligence, machine learning, systems, databases, and software engineering.

I want to develop mathematical reasoning.

I want to learn how to conduct research.

I want to become a better communicator.

And I want to develop the discipline required to build systems that other people can rely on.

But I also want to preserve something less technical.

I want to preserve the desire to make things that matter.

It is easy to build for the sake of building.

Technology gives us endless possibilities.

But I want to be selective.

I want to ask whether the work deserves to exist.

Does it solve a real problem?

Does it make something easier?

Does it help someone learn?

Does it enable something that was previously difficult?

Does it contribute knowledge?

Does it create a foundation for someone else's work?

These questions help me decide what kind of projects are worth pursuing.

They also help me think about legacy.

Legacy is a word that can sound overly ambitious when used by a student.

I am not talking about becoming famous.

I am talking about leaving something useful behind.

A piece of knowledge.

A tool.

A method.

A system.

A lesson.

A contribution.

Something that remains valuable after I have moved on to another problem.

That idea gives me a different perspective on engineering.

I am not simply writing code for the present.

I am making decisions that may affect the future of the system.

I am not simply studying concepts for an examination.

I am developing tools that may shape the way I think later.

I am not simply participating in research.

I am contributing to a chain of knowledge that began before me and will continue after me.

That continuity is motivating.

It makes ordinary work feel more meaningful.

A well-written function may seem insignificant.

But if it makes another developer's work easier, it matters.

A clear explanation may seem small.

But if it helps someone understand a difficult concept, it matters.

A carefully designed experiment may seem narrow.

But if another researcher can build upon it, it matters.

Impact often begins with small contributions.

I want to become someone who makes those contributions consistently.

College will be an important part of that journey.

I want to learn from professors who are creating knowledge.

I want to work with students who bring different perspectives.

I want to participate in research and technical projects.

I want to contribute to open-source communities.

I want to build systems that can be tested in real environments.

And I want to learn how to take an idea from an initial question to something that other people can use.

I know that many of the things I build will eventually become outdated.

That is inevitable.

Technology changes.

Applications are replaced.

Frameworks disappear.

Systems are redesigned.

Even successful projects eventually reach the end of their useful life.

But the knowledge gained from building them does not have to disappear.

The lessons remain.

The mistakes remain as experience.

The design decisions become understanding.

The technical skills become foundations for future work.

And sometimes, something survives longer than expected.

A concept becomes a method.

A method becomes a tool.

A tool becomes part of another system.

A student who learned from that tool becomes a developer.

The chain continues.

That is the kind of impact I find meaningful.

Not necessarily being remembered.

Being useful.

I want my work to make someone else's work slightly easier.

I want my ideas to give someone else a starting point.

I want my mistakes to become lessons rather than repeated mistakes.

I want the things I build to create possibilities for people I may never meet.

That is one of the reasons I love technology.

It can travel.

An idea does not need to remain where it was created.

Code can cross borders.

Research can cross disciplines.

Knowledge can cross generations.

A small contribution can become part of something much larger.

I want to participate in that process.

When I think about my future, I therefore do not only ask what I want to achieve.

I ask what I want to contribute.

I want to contribute to artificial intelligence that is useful and responsible.

I want to contribute to software that people can understand and rely on.

I want to contribute to research that answers meaningful questions.

I want to contribute to educational tools that help people become more capable.

And I want to contribute to communities where knowledge is shared rather than kept isolated.

I know I am still at the beginning.

There are technologies I have not learned.

There are problems I have not encountered.

There are research questions I have not yet discovered.

There are mistakes I have not yet made.

But that is exactly what makes the future exciting.

I have many things left to build.

And every project gives me an opportunity to learn something that can become part of the next one.

Perhaps the most meaningful way to think about engineering is not as the creation of finished objects.

It is the creation of starting points.

A system becomes the starting point for an improvement.

A research result becomes the starting point for another question.

A lesson becomes the starting point for another person's understanding.

A piece of code becomes the starting point for a larger application.

An idea becomes the starting point for something neither the original creator nor anyone else could have predicted.

That is what I want my work to do.

I want it to continue.

Not because I need my name attached to everything I create.

But because I want to know that something useful came from the time I spent learning and building.

Years from now, I hope I can look back and see a chain of contributions rather than a collection of achievements.

Some may have succeeded.

Some may have failed.

Some may have been replaced.

Some may have become the foundation for something better.

That is enough.

Because the most meaningful work does not always remain visible.

Sometimes it survives as knowledge.

Sometimes as a method.

Sometimes as a tool.

Sometimes as a person who learned something because you taught them.

And sometimes as an idea that continues growing after you have moved on.

That is what I want to build.

Not just things that work today.

Things that give someone else a better starting point tomorrow.
