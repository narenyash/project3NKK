
I used to think leadership was primarily about taking charge. A leader, in my early understanding, was the person who made decisions, assigned responsibilities, and stood at the front of a group. My experiences in academics, projects, competitions, and collaborative work have gradually changed this definition. I now believe leadership begins much earlier than decision-making. It begins with responsibility.

A person demonstrates leadership when they notice something that needs to be done and take initiative.

They demonstrate it when they accept responsibility for a mistake instead of searching for someone to blame.

They demonstrate it when they help another person understand something difficult.

They demonstrate it when they remain committed to a task even when there is no immediate recognition.

This understanding of leadership has influenced the kind of student and professional I want to become.

My interest in engineering has given me many opportunities to work in teams.

Technical projects rarely depend on one person.

A software application may require frontend development, backend development, database management, testing, deployment, design, and documentation.

An artificial intelligence project may require data collection, preprocessing, model development, evaluation, and integration.

Each part depends on the others.

A team can therefore succeed only when individuals understand their responsibilities while also considering the larger objective.

This taught me that leadership is not simply assigning tasks.

It is understanding how individual contributions fit together.

When I work on a project, I want to know not only what I am responsible for but also how my work affects others.

If the backend is delayed, the frontend may be blocked.

If the data pipeline is unreliable, the machine learning model may produce misleading results.

If requirements are unclear, developers may build features that do not solve the intended problem.

Leadership requires awareness of these dependencies.

It requires communication.

It requires planning.

And it requires responsibility.

One of the first lessons I learned about teamwork was that technical ability alone does not guarantee success.

A group can contain talented individuals and still perform poorly if there is no coordination.

People may work on overlapping tasks.

Important requirements may be missed.

Deadlines may be misunderstood.

Problems may remain hidden until the final stage.

The solution is not necessarily more technical knowledge.

Sometimes it is better organisation.

This changed the way I approached group projects.

I began paying greater attention to task division, progress, communication, and deadlines.

I learned that a good plan does not need to be complicated.

Everyone should know what needs to be done.

Everyone should understand dependencies.

Problems should be communicated early.

Progress should be visible.

And the team should remain flexible when circumstances change.

These principles have become part of how I approach collaborative work.

Leadership also means being willing to take initiative.

Sometimes a problem is obvious but nobody has been specifically assigned to solve it.

A leader does not always wait for instructions.

They identify the need and act.

This does not mean taking control of everything.

It means recognising responsibility.

If documentation is missing, I can start it.

If a team member is struggling with a concept, I can offer help.

If a task has been overlooked, I can bring attention to it.

If a process is inefficient, I can suggest an alternative.

Small acts of initiative can prevent larger problems.

I want to develop this habit.

Initiative is especially important in technology because projects often involve uncertainty.

Requirements can change.

Libraries can behave unexpectedly.

Deployment environments can create new problems.

A new technical challenge may appear without warning.

A person who waits for someone else to provide every answer may struggle.

A person who investigates, asks questions, and proposes possible solutions can move the project forward.

I want to become the second kind of person.

However, initiative must be balanced with humility.

Taking initiative does not mean assuming that my idea is always correct.

A leader should be willing to listen.

Before changing a system, I should understand why it currently works the way it does.

Before making a decision, I should consider the perspectives of people affected by it.

Before rejecting another person's approach, I should understand their reasoning.

Leadership requires confidence without arrogance.

This balance is something I want to continue developing.

Another important lesson is that leadership includes accountability.

When something goes wrong, the easiest response can be to identify who caused it.

But blame rarely solves the underlying problem.

A stronger response asks what happened and what can be done next.

If I made a mistake, I should acknowledge it.

If my decision contributed to a problem, I should examine it.

If the process was unclear, the team should improve it.

Accountability creates trust.

People are more willing to work with someone who accepts responsibility than someone who always searches for excuses.

I want to become someone who can say, "This was my mistake. Here is what I will do differently."

That sentence represents a form of leadership.

It demonstrates honesty.

It creates space for improvement.

And it shows respect for the team.

Leadership also means protecting the team's larger objective.

Sometimes individuals become focused on their own tasks.

They complete what was assigned but lose sight of whether the overall project is succeeding.

A leader should continually return to the larger question:

Are we solving the problem we intended to solve?

This is particularly important in software development.

A team can spend weeks building features and still discover that users do not need them.

The project may technically be complete while practically failing.

Leadership therefore requires perspective.

It means stepping back from individual tasks and examining the whole system.

I want to develop this ability.

My interest in human-centred technology has reinforced this principle.

A project should not be considered successful simply because the team completed its planned features.

It should be evaluated based on whether users benefit.

This requires listening to feedback.

Sometimes users will dislike a feature the team worked hard to create.

Sometimes they will request something unexpected.

Sometimes they will reveal that the original problem was misunderstood.

A good leader should not treat this feedback as an attack.

It is information.

The team can use it to improve.

This requires emotional maturity.

Not every criticism should be accepted automatically.

But every criticism should be considered carefully.

I want to develop that judgment.

Leadership also involves helping others succeed.

A team leader who completes their own work perfectly but leaves everyone else struggling has not created a strong team.

A better leader helps remove obstacles.

They share information.

They encourage collaboration.

They make expectations clear.

They recognise contributions.

They help people develop their abilities.

This is one reason I value mentoring.

When I explain technical concepts to another student, I am not simply transferring information.

I am helping them become more independent.

The best outcome is not that they need my help forever.

It is that eventually they can solve similar problems themselves.

That is a form of leadership I admire.

Leadership through empowerment.

I want to practise it.

Education provides many opportunities for this kind of leadership.

Students can organise technical workshops.

They can mentor juniors.

They can coordinate project teams.

They can contribute to clubs.

They can participate in community initiatives.

These activities develop skills that classrooms alone may not provide.

They require planning, communication, delegation, conflict resolution, and responsibility.

I want to participate actively in such experiences during university.

I also want to learn from leaders around me.

A professor may demonstrate leadership through research.

A senior student may demonstrate it by supporting juniors.

A project manager may demonstrate it through coordination.

A community organiser may demonstrate it through service.

Leadership can take many forms.

It does not always require a formal title.

This is an important lesson.

A person can lead without being called a leader.

They can influence others through behaviour.

They can set standards through consistency.

They can create trust through reliability.

They can encourage others through support.

I want to focus on these qualities rather than titles.

Leadership also requires decision-making.

When a team faces multiple options, someone eventually needs to make a choice.

Good decisions require information.

A leader should understand the objective, consider alternatives, identify risks, and evaluate consequences.

But decisions cannot always wait for perfect information.

Real projects involve uncertainty.

At some point, a decision must be made with the information available.

This requires judgment.

I want to develop the ability to make thoughtful decisions without becoming paralysed by uncertainty.

At the same time, I want to remain willing to change a decision when new evidence appears.

Changing direction should not automatically be viewed as weakness.

If circumstances change, adapting can be responsible.

A leader should be committed to the objective, not necessarily to one specific method.

This distinction is important to me.

It reflects the same iterative mindset I value in engineering.

Plan.

Act.

Observe.

Learn.

Adjust.

Leadership follows a similar process.

Another important part of leadership is prioritisation.

Resources are limited.

A team cannot do everything simultaneously.

There may be ten possible features but time to build only three.

There may be multiple technical problems but limited people to solve them.

A leader must identify what matters most.

This requires understanding impact.

Which task blocks others?

Which problem affects users most?

Which risk could cause the greatest damage?

Which feature is essential?

Which can wait?

These decisions determine how effectively a team uses its resources.

I want to become better at making such decisions.

Leadership also requires understanding people.

Different team members have different strengths.

One person may be highly analytical.

Another may be creative.

Another may communicate exceptionally well.

Another may be strong at implementation.

A good leader recognises these differences.

Responsibilities can then be assigned according to strengths while also providing opportunities for growth.

I want to learn how to do this without placing people into rigid categories.

People can develop.

Someone who struggles with presentations today may become a strong communicator through practice.

Someone who has limited technical experience may become an excellent developer with guidance.

Leadership should create room for growth.

This is another reason I value supportive teams.

A team should challenge people while also allowing them to learn.

Mistakes should become opportunities for improvement.

Questions should be welcomed.

Feedback should be constructive.

Success should be shared.

I want to contribute to such an environment.

Leadership also involves managing conflict.

Disagreement is inevitable in collaborative work.

People may disagree about architecture.

They may disagree about priorities.

They may interpret requirements differently.

Avoiding disagreement does not solve it.

A leader should create a process for resolving it.

What is the actual point of disagreement?

What evidence supports each position?

What are the consequences?

Is there a way to test the alternatives?

Can a compromise work?

These questions shift the discussion from personal preference to problem-solving.

I want to develop this approach.

Respect is essential.

A person can disagree strongly with an idea while respecting the person who proposed it.

This distinction creates healthier teams.

I want to practise it throughout my academic and professional life.

Another aspect of leadership is reliability.

People should be able to trust that commitments will be taken seriously.

If I agree to complete a task, I should make a reasonable effort to complete it.

If I realise I cannot meet a deadline, I should communicate early.

If I make a mistake, I should report it.

Reliability may seem ordinary, but it is fundamental to teamwork.

A talented person who is unreliable can create significant problems for a team.

A moderately experienced person who consistently communicates and delivers can become extremely valuable.

I want to build that reliability.

Leadership also involves resilience.

Projects can fail.

Plans can change.

Teams can face unexpected obstacles.

A leader's response can influence how the team reacts.

If the leader becomes overwhelmed and stops communicating, uncertainty increases.

If the leader acknowledges the problem, identifies priorities, and focuses the team on the next step, the situation becomes more manageable.

I want to develop this ability.

This does not mean pretending that problems are insignificant.

It means responding to them constructively.

Calmness should not replace urgency when urgency is necessary.

Instead, it should create clarity.

I want to learn how to remain focused during difficult situations.

My interest in technology provides many opportunities to practise this.

Software projects often involve unexpected technical issues.

A deployment may fail close to a deadline.

A dependency may break.

A model may perform poorly.

A feature may not integrate correctly.

In these moments, technical problem-solving and leadership become connected.

Someone needs to understand the issue.

Someone needs to coordinate the response.

Someone needs to communicate the situation.

Sometimes one person performs all three roles.

I want to become capable of doing so.
