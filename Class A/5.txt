
I used to think that knowing something meant being able to explain it.

If I understood a programming concept, I assumed I could teach it. If I could solve a problem, I believed I could show someone else how to solve it. It seemed straightforward: understand the concept, explain the steps, and the other person should understand.

Then I tried teaching programming.

I discovered that knowing and teaching are very different skills.

The experience began when I started helping younger students who were interested in learning how to code. Some had never written a program before. Others had experimented with tutorials but did not understand what they were actually doing.

I expected the experience to be simple.

It was not.

My first explanation of a programming concept was technically correct. I used the proper terminology and walked through the syntax carefully. When the students looked confused, I assumed I had not explained it clearly enough.

So I explained it again.

They were still confused.

I changed the words but kept the same structure.

Still confused.

That was when I realized the problem was not the explanation.

It was me.

I was explaining the concept from the perspective of someone who already understood it.

To me, a variable was obvious. To a beginner, the word itself could be meaningless. I was starting from step five and expecting them to already understand steps one through four.

I needed to change the way I taught.

Instead of beginning with technical definitions, I began with familiar situations.

I explained variables using scores in a game. I explained conditions using everyday decisions. I explained loops through repetitive tasks. Rather than immediately asking students to memorize syntax, I asked them to predict what a program would do.

The difference was surprisingly large.

Students who had struggled with the original explanation began participating.

One student who had barely touched the keyboard started experimenting with the code independently.

That moment stayed with me.

It was the first time I understood that teaching is not about transferring information from one person to another.

It is about building a bridge between what someone already understands and what they are trying to learn.

That realization changed the way I viewed education.

It also changed the way I viewed myself.

Until then, much of my learning had been individual. I would find a tutorial, solve a problem, build a project, or study a concept on my own.

Teaching forced me to slow down.

When I learned something myself, I could move past confusing parts because I could search for another explanation or simply spend more time figuring them out.

When teaching someone else, I could not assume they would make the same mental connections I had made.

I had to understand the concept deeply enough to explain it in several different ways.

That made me a better learner.

It also made me more patient.

Programming had trained me to be persistent with computers.

Teaching trained me to be patient with people.

The difference is important.

A computer will execute instructions exactly as written.

A student will not.

A student brings previous experiences, misconceptions, fears, interests, confidence, and different ways of thinking.

Two students can hear exactly the same explanation and understand completely different things.

Once I recognized this, I became much more interested in the human side of technology.

Technology is often described as a tool for efficiency.

But education reminded me that efficiency is not always the most important objective.

Sometimes the slower process produces deeper understanding.

Sometimes allowing someone to make a mistake is more valuable than immediately giving them the correct answer.

Sometimes asking a question is more useful than providing an explanation.

I began changing how I helped people.

Instead of immediately fixing someone's code, I would ask what they expected the program to do.

Then I would ask what it actually did.

The difference between those two answers often revealed the problem.

This approach had an unexpected benefit: students began solving more problems themselves.

If I gave them the answer, the immediate problem disappeared.

If I helped them discover the answer, they gained a method they could use again.

That distinction became central to my understanding of leadership.

I had previously associated leadership with taking responsibility, making decisions, and guiding a team.

Now I saw another form of leadership: making other people more capable.

A leader does not always need to be the person with the most knowledge.

Sometimes the best leader is the person who helps everyone else contribute their knowledge.

This became especially important during team projects.

When working with others, I noticed that people often had different strengths.

One person might understand the technical architecture extremely well.

Another might be better at design.

Someone else might communicate naturally with users.

Another person might be good at organizing the work.

If one person tried to control every part of the project, the team became less effective.

When responsibilities were shared according to strengths, the result improved.

This taught me that collaboration is not about making everyone work in the same way.

It is about creating an environment where different abilities can work together.

My experiences teaching also changed the way I communicate.

Technical knowledge can sometimes make communication harder.

When someone becomes familiar with specialized terminology, it is easy to forget that those words are not universal.

I learned to ask myself:

"Would someone who has never seen this before understand what I am saying?"

That question has become useful far beyond teaching.

It helps when explaining projects to teammates.

It helps when presenting technical ideas.

It helps when talking to people who are not programmers.

And it helps when designing software.

If an interface requires a user to understand technical terminology before they can use it, perhaps the interface is asking too much from the user.

This connection between teaching and software design became particularly interesting to me.

A good teacher and a good interface have something in common.

Both should reduce unnecessary confusion.

A teacher should not make a concept harder than it needs to be.

A software application should not make a task harder than it needs to be.

Both need to understand the person on the other side.

This became one of the reasons I became interested in human-centered technology.

As I continued learning artificial intelligence, I became increasingly aware that powerful technology is not automatically accessible technology.

An AI system may be technically sophisticated, but if ordinary users cannot understand how to interact with it, its usefulness is limited.

This is especially important in education.

Artificial intelligence has the potential to provide personalized explanations, generate practice problems, identify learning gaps, and assist students with different learning needs.

But personalization is not simply a matter of giving everyone more information.

Students need explanations that match their current understanding.

They need feedback that helps them improve rather than simply telling them whether they are right or wrong.

They need tools that encourage independent thinking rather than replacing it.

My experience teaching programming gave me a different perspective on these possibilities.

If I were designing an AI tutor, I would not want it to immediately provide the solution to every problem.

I would want it to understand where the student is struggling.

I would want it to ask questions.

I would want it to provide hints.

I would want it to change its explanation when the first explanation did not work.

In other words, I would want the system to behave less like an answer generator and more like a teacher.

This idea has become one of my interests within artificial intelligence.

How can technology support learning without removing the process of learning?

How can intelligent systems recognize when a student needs an explanation, a hint, an example, or simply more time?

How can we design educational technology that strengthens curiosity rather than encouraging dependence?

I do not have complete answers to these questions.

That is exactly why I want to study them.

My experience with teaching also changed my attitude toward knowledge itself.

Knowledge can feel like something personal.

You study something difficult, understand it, and feel that you have gained something valuable.

But when you teach someone else, the knowledge changes.

It becomes useful beyond you.

A programming concept that exists only in my memory has limited impact.

The same concept understood by ten other students can become the foundation for ten different projects.

This made me realize that education has a multiplier effect.

One person's learning can influence many other people.

That idea motivates me.

I want my education to have that kind of effect.

I do not want to learn only for grades, certificates, or personal achievement.

Those things can matter, but they are not the entire purpose of learning.

I want the knowledge I gain to eventually become useful to other people.

Sometimes that may happen through software.

Sometimes through research.

Sometimes through teaching.

Sometimes through mentoring.

The form may change, but the principle remains the same: knowledge becomes more valuable when it creates opportunities for others.

This philosophy has influenced how I participate in technical communities.

When I discover something useful, I am more likely to document it, explain it, or share it with someone who may need it.

When I encounter a difficult problem, I try to remember how confusing it felt before I understood it.

That memory helps me explain it more patiently to someone else.

I have also learned to appreciate the people who taught me.

Before teaching others, I underestimated how much effort good teaching requires.

A good teacher notices when students are confused even when they do not say anything.

A good teacher changes the explanation rather than blaming the learner.

A good teacher knows when to step in and when to let someone struggle productively.

A good teacher remembers that confidence is part of learning.

These are qualities I now value deeply.

They have influenced the kind of person I want to become in college.

I want to be a student who contributes to the learning environment rather than simply benefiting from it.

I want to participate in technical communities, help peers when I can, and learn from people who know more than I do.

I want to be comfortable being both the person asking the question and the person helping answer it.

Those roles are not opposites.

They are part of the same cycle.

You learn.

You practice.

You understand.

You teach.

Someone asks a question you cannot answer.

You learn again.

That cycle is one of the things I enjoy most about technology.

There is always another question.

There is always another perspective.

There is always something that can be explained more clearly.

This is also why I value humility.

Teaching showed me that knowing more than someone else in one area does not make me better than them.

A student I was helping might know something about design, mathematics, electronics, or another subject that I had never studied.

The role could easily reverse.

Today I might explain programming.

Tomorrow I might be the person asking for help.

Recognizing this makes collaboration easier.

It allows me to learn without worrying about appearing inexperienced.

I believe this mindset will be important in college.

University will place me in environments where I will often be surrounded by people who are extremely capable.

Some will know more about algorithms.

Others will be stronger mathematicians.

Some will have research experience I do not have.

Others will have built products I have never imagined.

I do not want to compete with every person around me.

I want to learn from them.

At the same time, I hope to contribute what I know.

That exchange is what makes a community valuable.

My long-term goal is to work in artificial intelligence and software engineering, particularly on technology that can have practical human impact.

Education is one area that continues to interest me because it combines several of my interests: technology, problem solving, communication, and human behavior.

I am interested in the possibility of building intelligent systems that make high-quality learning more accessible.

But I also want to approach that problem carefully.

The goal should not be to replace teachers.

It should be to give teachers better tools and give students additional ways to learn.

Technology should expand human capability rather than simply remove humans from the process.

My experience teaching programming has made me believe this strongly.

The most rewarding moments were not when I gave a student the correct answer.

They were when the student stopped needing my answer.

That was the real measure of success.

Someone who initially asked, "What should I type?" eventually asked, "What do you think is wrong with my code?"

That change represented progress.

The student had moved from dependence to investigation.

That is what I want technology to encourage.

Curiosity.

Confidence.

Independence.

The willingness to try.

The willingness to fail.

And the ability to keep learning.

When I think about what I want from college, I therefore think beyond courses and degrees.

I want to become a better learner and a better contributor.

I want to deepen my understanding of computer science and artificial intelligence.

I want to conduct research.

I want to build meaningful projects.

I want to work with people whose experiences are different from mine.

And I want to share what I learn.

Because one of the most unexpected lessons technology has given me is that knowledge becomes more powerful when it moves.

An idea can begin with one person.

It can be explained to another.

That person can improve it.

Someone else can apply it to a different problem.

Eventually, the original idea can become something neither person could have created alone.

That is the kind of impact I hope to have.

I began teaching because I knew something about programming.

I continued because I discovered something about learning.

And I now see teaching as part of my own education.

Every time I explain something to someone else, I am forced to understand it more deeply.

Every question I cannot answer reminds me that there is still more to learn.

Every student who becomes more confident reminds me why knowledge should not be kept to oneself.

I entered the experience thinking I was going to teach programming.

Instead, I learned how to communicate, how to listen, how to collaborate, and how to help someone become independent.

That may be the most important lesson I have learned so far:

The value of knowing something is not only that you can use it yourself.

It is that you can use it to help someone else move forward.

That is the kind of student, engineer, and person I hope to become.
