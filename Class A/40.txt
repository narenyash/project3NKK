
Failure is an experience that most students would prefer to avoid. For a long time, I also viewed failure mainly as evidence that I had not performed well enough. A low result, an unsuccessful project, or an idea that did not work could feel disappointing because I naturally wanted my efforts to produce successful outcomes. With time, however, my understanding of failure has changed. I have learned that failure is not simply the opposite of success. It can be one of the most effective ways of discovering what needs to improve.

This lesson has influenced the way I approach academics, technology, projects, and my future goals.

My first experiences with failure were relatively simple. I would prepare for an examination, expect a particular result, and sometimes perform below my expectations. Initially, my response was mostly emotional disappointment. I would focus on the result itself rather than examining the process that produced it.

Gradually, I began asking different questions.

Did I misunderstand the subject?

Did I study the wrong material?

Did I spend my time effectively?

Did I practise enough?

Did I understand the concept or merely memorise it?

These questions changed my response to disappointing results.

Instead of seeing failure as a final judgment, I began treating it as information.

This change became even more significant when I started working on technical projects.

Programming provides many opportunities to fail.

A program may refuse to run.

A feature may behave differently from what was expected.

A database may not return the correct information.

An application may work on one device and fail on another.

An algorithm may produce poor results.

A machine learning model may perform well during training but poorly on unfamiliar data.

At first, these problems were frustrating.

I wanted my code to work immediately.

Eventually, I realised that debugging was not an interruption to programming.

It was part of programming.

Every error contained information.

A compiler error could identify a syntax problem.

An exception could reveal an incorrect assumption.

An unexpected output could indicate a logical mistake.

A failed test could reveal a weakness in the design.

The challenge was learning how to interpret that information.

This changed my attitude toward technical problems.

Instead of asking only, "How do I fix this?", I began asking, "Why did this happen?"

That second question is more difficult, but it often leads to deeper understanding.

Suppose a program produces an incorrect result.

I could change the code until the output becomes correct.

But if I do not understand why the original code failed, I may make the same mistake again.

If I investigate the underlying cause, I gain knowledge that can be applied to future problems.

This is one of the most important lessons failure has taught me.

A successful outcome solves one problem.

Understanding a failure can prevent many future problems.

This principle became particularly relevant to my interest in artificial intelligence.

Machine learning systems do not always behave as expected.

A model may achieve strong performance during development but struggle when given new data.

When this happens, it is tempting to immediately change the model.

However, the problem may not be the model itself.

The dataset may be too small.

The training examples may not represent real-world conditions.

The features may be inappropriate.

The evaluation method may be misleading.

The data may contain errors.

The model may have learned patterns that do not generalise.

Therefore, failure can reveal weaknesses in the entire development process.

This taught me to look beyond the immediate symptom.

When a result is disappointing, I want to understand the system as a whole.

This mindset has made me more interested in research.

Research rarely proceeds perfectly according to the original plan.

An experiment may fail to support a hypothesis.

A model may perform worse than expected.

A dataset may not contain enough useful information.

A proposed method may not produce an improvement.

These results can be disappointing, but they are not necessarily meaningless.

A failed experiment can eliminate one possible explanation.

It can reveal a limitation.

It can suggest a better methodology.

It can lead to a new question.

This is why I have come to appreciate the scientific value of failure.

Not every unsuccessful experiment is a mistake.

Sometimes it is evidence.

The important thing is to interpret it correctly.

This way of thinking has also affected how I approach competitions and projects.

When participating in technical challenges, there is often a strong focus on winning.

Winning is rewarding, and competition can provide motivation.

However, not every project can receive the highest position.

If the only value of the experience comes from winning, much of the learning opportunity is lost.

A project that does not win can still teach valuable lessons.

Perhaps the idea was not presented clearly.

Perhaps the implementation had technical limitations.

Perhaps another team solved the problem more effectively.

Perhaps the project addressed the wrong aspect of the problem.

Understanding these factors can improve future work.

I have therefore learned to evaluate experiences using more than one measure.

Did I learn something new?

Did I improve my technical skills?

Did I become better at presenting an idea?

Did I understand a problem more deeply?

Did I learn how to work better with a team?

These questions can turn disappointment into development.

Failure has also taught me about preparation.

Sometimes an unsuccessful result is not caused by bad luck.

It is caused by insufficient preparation.

Recognising this can be uncomfortable.

It is easier to blame circumstances than to admit that I could have prepared better.

But accepting responsibility creates an opportunity for improvement.

If I did not practise enough, I can change my preparation.

If I misunderstood the requirements, I can learn to analyse them more carefully.

If I underestimated the time required, I can improve planning.

If I lacked a technical skill, I can develop it.

Responsibility makes failure useful.

This does not mean every failure is entirely my fault.

External circumstances can matter.

Unexpected problems can occur.

Some situations are genuinely outside an individual's control.

But even then, I can usually control my response.

I can decide whether to learn from the experience.

I can decide whether to try again.

I can decide whether to change my approach.

This is where resilience becomes important.

Resilience is not pretending that failure does not hurt.

It is the ability to continue after acknowledging that it does.

There have been situations where I have invested considerable effort in an idea only to discover that it was not working.

The immediate reaction can be discouragement.

But after stepping back, I can examine what happened.

Sometimes the right decision is to repair the approach.

Sometimes it is to simplify it.

Sometimes it is to start again.

Sometimes it is to abandon the idea and pursue a better one.

Knowing when to continue and when to change direction is an important form of judgment.

Failure has also taught me patience.

Modern technology can create the impression that answers should be immediate.

Search engines provide information quickly.

Programming tools provide suggestions.

AI systems can generate explanations and code.

Yet meaningful learning still takes time.

Understanding a difficult concept cannot always be accelerated.

Developing strong technical skills requires practice.

Building a reliable system requires testing.

Conducting meaningful research requires persistence.

There are no shortcuts for everything.

Failure reminds me of this.

If I struggle with a concept, the solution may not be another quick explanation.

It may require returning to the fundamentals.

It may require practising simpler problems.

It may require approaching the subject from a different perspective.

This has made me more comfortable with slow progress.

Progress does not always appear immediately.

Sometimes several unsuccessful attempts are necessary before improvement becomes visible.

If I struggle, I want to identify the cause.

If I need help, I want to seek it.

If my study method is ineffective, I want to change it.

If I need more practice, I want to practise.

If I make a mistake, I want to understand it.

This approach allows failure to become part of a continuous improvement process.

Failure has also changed the way I receive criticism.

Earlier, criticism could feel personal.

If someone pointed out a weakness in my work, I might focus on the fact that the work had been criticised rather than on what could be improved.

With experience, I have learned to separate criticism of an idea from criticism of a person.

A project can have weaknesses without the person who created it being incapable.

In fact, feedback can be extremely valuable.

A reviewer may notice something I overlooked.

A teammate may identify a usability problem.

A professor may recognise a conceptual misunderstanding.

A user may reveal that the solution does not address their actual need.

Listening to this feedback requires humility.

It also requires curiosity.

Why does the other person see the problem differently?

What information do they have that I may have missed?

Is their criticism supported by evidence?

If it is, how can I improve?

These questions make feedback productive.

Failure has therefore taught me not to become too attached to my first solution.

This is particularly important in engineering.
