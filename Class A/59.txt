
I have always been more interested in solving a problem than simply identifying that a problem exists. Whenever something does not work as expected, my first instinct is usually to understand what is happening and think about what could be done differently. This habit has gradually influenced the way I study, the projects I work on, and the career I want to pursue.

For me, problem-solving is more than a technical skill.

It is a way of thinking.

It means being willing to look at a situation carefully, break it into smaller parts, understand the causes, consider different possibilities, and then act on the best available solution.

This way of thinking is one of the main reasons I became interested in engineering and computer science.

Before I became interested in programming, I did not think of everyday situations as engineering problems. A difficult process was simply something that had to be dealt with. A repetitive task was simply part of the routine. If something was inconvenient, I accepted it.

Programming changed this perspective.

When I began writing programs, I discovered that many problems could be broken down into smaller problems.

A large task could become a sequence of smaller tasks.

A complicated system could be divided into components.

A confusing error could be traced to a particular part of the program.

This way of thinking was new to me.

Instead of asking whether a problem was too complicated, I began asking how it could be divided.

That question has become increasingly important in my education.

One of the first lessons I learned from programming was that the first description of a problem is not always the actual problem.

A user may say that an application is slow.

But the cause may not be the interface.

It could be a database query.

It could be an inefficient algorithm.

It could be a network issue.

It could be unnecessary data processing.

Similarly, someone may say that a process is difficult.

The real problem may be that the instructions are unclear.

This taught me not to jump immediately toward solutions.

First, I need to understand the problem.

This is a surprisingly important skill.

A person can spend a great deal of time solving the wrong problem very efficiently.

Good problem-solving begins with asking the right questions.

What exactly is happening?

Who is affected?

When does it happen?

How frequently does it happen?

What are the constraints?

What has already been tried?

What does success look like?

These questions help transform a vague problem into something that can be investigated.

I want to continue developing this habit.

The next step is breaking the problem down.

Large problems can feel overwhelming because they contain several smaller problems at once.

Consider developing a software application.

At first, the task may appear to be simply "build the application."

But that description hides many individual challenges.

What should the application do?

Who will use it?

What information is required?

How should the interface work?

Where should the data be stored?

How should users authenticate?

How should errors be handled?

How should the application be tested?

How will it be deployed?

Once the problem is divided, it becomes more manageable.

Each component can be understood separately.

This approach is useful far beyond programming.

Academic subjects can also be approached this way.

A difficult examination topic may seem overwhelming when considered as a whole.

Breaking it into concepts, formulas, examples, and practice problems can make it easier to understand.

A research question can be divided into smaller questions.

A large assignment can be divided into research, planning, writing, revision, and final review.

Problem-solving therefore provides a general framework for learning.

It helps convert complexity into manageable steps.

However, breaking a problem down is not enough.

The smaller parts must still be connected.

This is where systems thinking becomes important.

A change in one component can affect another.

In software, modifying the database structure may affect the backend.

Changing an API may affect the frontend.

Changing the data preprocessing pipeline may affect a machine learning model.

Understanding these relationships is essential.

A solution that fixes one component while damaging another is not necessarily a good solution.

This has taught me to think beyond isolated tasks.

I want to understand systems as a whole.

This is one of the reasons I enjoy engineering.

Engineering combines individual technical skills with systems thinking.

An engineer cannot simply ask whether a component works.

They must ask whether the complete system works.

This distinction becomes especially important when technology interacts with real people.

A feature may function correctly from a technical perspective but still create problems for users.

A process may be efficient for the organisation but inconvenient for customers.

An AI model may perform well on a benchmark but poorly in real-world conditions.

Therefore, problem-solving requires context.

The technical solution must fit the actual situation.

This is something I have gradually learned through projects.

When building software, it is tempting to focus on features.

More features can make a project appear more impressive.

But more features do not automatically make a product more useful.

Sometimes the best solution is simpler.

If a user needs to complete a task quickly, unnecessary options can create confusion.

If a system is intended for beginners, complex interfaces may discourage them.

If a process can be completed in three steps, adding seven more features may make it worse.

Problem-solving therefore requires knowing what not to build.

This is a lesson I want to remember as I develop more complex systems.

Another important part of problem-solving is identifying constraints.

Every real problem has limitations.

There may be limited time.

Limited money.

Limited computing power.

Limited data.

Limited people.

Limited infrastructure.

Limited knowledge.

A solution must operate within these conditions.

This is particularly relevant in India.

A technology that works well in a high-resource environment may not be appropriate everywhere.

A system may need to function with limited connectivity.

A device may need to operate on affordable hardware.

An application may need to support multiple languages.

A service may need to work across different levels of digital literacy.

These constraints are not minor details.

They shape the solution.

I want to learn how to design technology around real conditions rather than ideal assumptions.

Problem-solving also requires prioritisation.

When there are many possible problems, not all can be addressed at once.

Which one matters most?

Which one affects the greatest number of users?

Which one blocks other work?

Which one carries the greatest risk?

Which one can be solved with available resources?

These questions help determine where effort should go.

This is important in both engineering and life.

Trying to solve everything simultaneously can result in solving nothing effectively.

I want to become better at identifying the highest-impact problems.

This is especially important when working on social or technological challenges.

A large problem may contain dozens of possible interventions.

A good problem solver identifies where a practical intervention can create meaningful improvement.

This requires both analysis and judgment.

Another aspect of problem-solving is creativity.

There is rarely only one possible solution.

Two engineers may approach the same problem differently.

One may improve an existing process.

Another may redesign it completely.

One may automate a task.

Another may eliminate the need for the task.

One may use a sophisticated model.

Another may discover that a simple rule-based system is sufficient.

Creativity allows people to consider alternatives.

But creativity should be balanced with practicality.

A creative solution that cannot be implemented may have limited value.

Therefore, I want to develop both imagination and evaluation.

I want to ask:

What else could work?

And then:

Which option is actually appropriate?

This balance is important in artificial intelligence.

AI can provide sophisticated approaches to many problems.

But using AI simply because it is available is not always sensible.

A problem may not require machine learning.

A simple algorithm may be faster, easier to maintain, and more reliable.

If AI is used, the model should solve a meaningful problem rather than simply make a project appear more advanced.

I want to develop this judgment.

Problem-solving also requires experimentation.

Sometimes it is impossible to know which solution will work without testing it.

Instead of spending too much time predicting the perfect answer, it can be useful to build a small prototype.

A prototype provides information.

It shows what works.

It reveals weaknesses.

It allows users to provide feedback.

It can prevent large investments in a poor idea.

This approach is particularly useful in software development.

Build something small.

Test it.

Learn.

Improve.

Repeat.

This process makes problem-solving more evidence-based.

I want to practise it throughout my education.

Experiments also teach humility.

A solution that looks excellent on paper may perform poorly in practice.

A model may fail on real data.

A user may behave differently from what we expected.

A system may encounter an edge case we did not anticipate.

These situations remind us that our assumptions are not facts.

Testing challenges assumptions.

That is why I value practical projects alongside theoretical learning.

Theory helps us understand possibilities.

Practice reveals constraints.

Both are necessary.

Another important skill is persistence.

Some problems cannot be solved immediately.

A programming error may take hours to understand.

A mathematical problem may require several attempts.

A research experiment may fail repeatedly.

A system may need multiple redesigns.

Giving up after the first failed attempt would prevent many discoveries.

At the same time, persistence should not mean repeating the same unsuccessful method indefinitely.

A good problem solver knows when to change strategy.

If an approach repeatedly fails, ask why.

Maybe the method is wrong.

Maybe the assumptions are incorrect.

Maybe more information is needed.

Maybe the problem itself needs to be reconsidered.

Persistence should therefore be combined with reflection.

Continue working, but continue learning.

This is a principle I want to develop.

Problem-solving also requires knowing when to ask for help.

There is sometimes a tendency among students to believe that solving a problem independently is always better.

I have learned that this is not necessarily true.

Another person's perspective can reveal an assumption that I did not notice.

A professor may explain a concept differently.

A teammate may identify a simpler approach.

A domain expert may point out a practical constraint.

Asking for help does not remove ownership of the problem.

It can improve the quality of the solution.

I want to become comfortable with collaborative problem-solving.

This is especially important because complex technological problems rarely belong to one discipline.

An AI engineer may need to work with doctors, educators, environmental scientists, designers, business professionals, or policymakers.

Each person understands a different part of the problem.

Combining these perspectives can produce stronger solutions.

I want to develop the ability to communicate across disciplines.

This requires listening.

Before proposing a technical solution, I need to understand the domain.

A software engineer may know how to build a system but not necessarily understand how a teacher runs a classroom or how a doctor makes a clinical decision.

Technology should support domain expertise rather than replace understanding.

I want to remember this.

Problem-solving also involves ethical judgment.

A technically possible solution is not automatically a responsible solution.

An AI system might be capable of making a decision automatically.

But should that decision be automated?

A company might be able to collect large amounts of user data.

But does it need all that information?

A system might be capable of replacing a human task.

But what consequences would that create?

These questions require more than technical knowledge.

They require considering people and society.

I want to develop that broader perspective.

This is particularly important as AI becomes more powerful.

The problems engineers solve can have consequences beyond the immediate project.

An AI system may influence what information people see.

A recommendation system may affect behaviour.

An automated decision may affect opportunities.

A security vulnerability may expose personal information.

Therefore, engineers must consider failure and misuse.

Problem-solving includes prevention.

It is not enough to ask how to make something work.

We should also ask how it could fail.

This is one of the most valuable questions in engineering.

What happens if the input is unusual?

What happens if the data is incomplete?

What happens if the user behaves unexpectedly?

What happens if the system is attacked?

What happens if the model is wrong?

Thinking about these cases improves reliability.

I want to develop this habit.

Problem-solving also requires measurement.

Without measurement, it is difficult to know whether a solution actually works.

A developer may believe an application is faster.

But how much faster?

A model may seem accurate.

But what is its performance on unseen data?

A process may appear more efficient.

But does it actually reduce time or cost?

Measurements provide evidence.

I want to learn how to define useful metrics.

This will be particularly important in AI research.

Model accuracy alone may not be enough.

Depending on the application, we may need to consider precision, recall, robustness, latency, resource consumption, fairness, interpretability, or other factors.

The correct metric depends on the problem.

This is another reason problem definition matters.

If the objective is unclear, measurement becomes difficult.

A strong problem solver therefore defines success before evaluating solutions.

This principle applies to personal goals as well.

If I want to improve my programming, I need to know what improvement means.

Solving more problems?

Building more projects?

Understanding algorithms?

Writing cleaner code?

Improving debugging ability?

A clear objective makes progress measurable.

I want to apply this principle to my education.

Problem-solving also teaches the importance of simplicity.

When faced with a difficult problem, there can be a temptation to create a complicated solution.

But complexity creates its own problems.

More components mean more points of failure.

More dependencies mean more maintenance.

More features mean more testing.

A simple solution that solves the problem effectively can be better than an elaborate solution that creates unnecessary complexity.

This principle has influenced the way I think about software design.

Before adding something, I want to ask whether it is necessary.

Before introducing a new technology, I want to understand what problem it solves.

Before increasing complexity, I want to consider whether the benefit justifies the cost.

Good engineering often involves removing unnecessary complexity.

Problem-solving is also connected to communication.

A technically correct solution can fail if it cannot be explained to the people who need to use or approve it.

An engineer may need to explain a system to a non-technical manager.

A researcher may need to present findings to a general audience.

A developer may need to explain limitations to users.

Clear communication makes solutions more useful.

I want to strengthen this skill.

I also want to learn how to write technical documentation clearly.

Good documentation allows other people to understand how a system works.

It reduces dependence on one individual.

It helps teams maintain projects.

It makes future problem-solving easier.

This may seem less exciting than developing new technology, but it is an important part of engineering.

Problem-solving is also closely connected to leadership.

When a team faces a difficult problem, someone needs to help organise the response.

The first step is usually defining the problem clearly.

Then responsibilities can be divided.

Possible solutions can be considered.

Evidence can be collected.

Progress can be reviewed.

This creates structure during uncertainty.

I want to develop this ability.

I do not think leadership means having all the answers.

It means helping a group move toward answers.

This is a distinction I find important.

The strongest person in a team is not necessarily the person who speaks the most.

Sometimes leadership comes from asking the right question.

"Are we solving the right problem?"

"What evidence do we have?"

"Could we simplify this?"

"What happens if this assumption is wrong?"

These questions can change the direction of an entire project.

I want to become someone who asks them.

This balance will be important in my future career.
