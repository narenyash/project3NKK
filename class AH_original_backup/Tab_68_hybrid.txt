Title: Pursuing a Career in Privacy-Enhancing Technologies: A Journey Towards a PhD at Carnegie Mellon University

My career aspirations lie in the intersection of mathematics, computer science, and privacy. I am captivated by cryptography, both its theoretical and practical applications, particularly Zero-Knowledge Proofs (ZKPs) and Private Information Retrieval (PIR). My passion lies in contributing to the development and implementation of novel ideas surrounding these fundamental primitives.

Intrigued by my work with Shay Gueron at Meta, and groundbreaking research such as [6], I find myself particularly drawn to ZKPs that offer exceptional performance on contemporary hardware platforms like CPUs, GPUs, or emerging hardware accelerators in data centers. Although there is existing work on designing hardware accelerators for ZKPs (e.g., [10]), a gap remains in schemes with desirable properties for such acceleration, including efficient memory access patterns and the ability to precompute or reuse costly intermediate results.

PIR also piques my interest, as I envision finding novel real-world applications for this technology and developing systems tailored for these use cases. While there are existing applications like blockchain light clients or privately accessed machine learning embeddings ([7]), I believe PIR's potential extends beyond these bounds, serving as a versatile solution to various other challenges. Moreover, expanding the range of use cases will provide valuable benchmarks and practical insights to foster the advancement of novel protocols for PIR.

My undergraduate research with Ari Juels' group has been rewarding. I take pride in the work I did on CanDID (IEEE S&P 2021 [8]), a system designed to address the challenge of decentralized identity by "bootstrapping" pseudonymous identities from real-world credentials, such as Social Security Numbers. Using Intel SGX, I implemented a component that generated these identities, which could be seamlessly integrated into our Multi-Party Computation (MPC) implementation. An interesting challenge I tackled was developing an elliptic curve-based hash function (Baby Jubjub) within the confines of an SGX enclave. Furthermore, I was involved in the "Clockwork Finance" project [2], which aimed to systematically analyze Market Execution Validity (MEV). In addition to my academic endeavors, I made contributions to open-source projects like Facebook's folly [9] and edited sections of Wikipedia related to cryptography and theoretical computer science.

Outside of academia, I have contributed to the cryptography community, working on various projects with the Cryptography Infrastructure team at Meta. Collaborating with Shay Gueron and Rafael Misoczki, I spearheaded creating a new mode of operation for the AES block cipher called "Double Nonce AES GCM," which was 2-3 times faster (depending on input size) than XChaCha20-Poly1305 on modern processors. I also participated in discussions about cryptographic system designs, such as NIST discussions surrounding Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC), block cipher modes of operation, applications of new Trusted Execution Environments (TEEs) at the company, or uses of Fully Homomorphic Encryption and MPC for serving ads. I also worked on a project to measure the security of code produced by different LLMs [3]. My work earned me a "Redefines Expectations" performance rating in 2022—an honor bestowed upon only 1-5% of engineers at Meta.

The allure of a PhD stems from my desire for more autonomy and freedom to explore ambitious or abstract projects. While I relish the aspects of my job that involve reading papers, optimizing algorithms, and offering design input on real-world systems, I yearn for opportunities to delve deeper into these tasks. Moreover, I miss the intellectual stimulation I experienced as a Teaching Assistant at Cornell during my 7 semesters there. Lastly, the rapid progress in privacy-enhancing technologies exhilarates me, and I am eager to contribute to this dynamic field.

Carnegie Mellon University (CMU) presents an appealing destination for my academic pursuits. The prospect of working with esteemed researchers like Elaine Shi, Riad Wahby, and Wenting Zheng captivates me. I was particularly intrigued by Elaine's talk about Piano [11] at Meta, which sparked a discussion about incorporating PIR in cloud authorization systems. Given our shared enthusiasm for similar cryptographic primitives (MPC or PIR), I believe we could foster a productive research partnership during my PhD.

Riad Wahby and I share a mutual passion for proof systems and secure computation, with his recent work on building efficient proof systems aligning closely with the type of graduate research I aspire to undertake (e.g., [5]). Furthermore, my industry experience working with various cryptographic hardware platforms such as proprietary Hardware Security Modules, Trusted Execution Environments (Intel SGX and AMD SEV-SNP), and hardware instructions for cryptography (Intel's AES-NI, GF-NI, and SHA extensions) complement Riad's research focus.

I am also drawn to collaborating with Wenting Zheng, given our shared interest in primitives like MPC or PIR. I believe that working together during my PhD would be mutually beneficial and enriching in these areas.

Beyond the appeal of specific advisors, CMU's CyLab seems like an ideal environment for nurturing research growth. Additionally, the opportunity to collaborate with Ph.D. student Quang Dao, whom I met at the Cornell Security Seminar, is exciting.

In conclusion, a PhD in Computer Science at Carnegie Mellon University offers me the perfect platform to continue my journey of impactful and intriguing work, ultimately positioning myself for a future as a researcher at a prominent institution or company. I am grateful for your consideration.
