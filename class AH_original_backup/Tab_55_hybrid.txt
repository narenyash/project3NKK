Title: Pursuing a Ph.D. to Bridge Theory and Practice in Python Data Science

My journey towards your esteemed Ph.D. program is driven by a passion for programming languages, formal methods, and their potential applications in the realm of data science, machine learning, artificial intelligence, and scientific computing. This ardor has been nurtured through my active participation in the open-source community, where I have honed my skills in integrating theoretical research with practical applications.

My focus lies in exploring the structural foundations that underpin the semantics of Python libraries within these domains. By delving into the design and implementation of abstractions for domain-specific libraries, I aim to foster a robust and self-sustaining ecosystem among decentralized multi-stakeholder Python projects. This research endeavor seeks to create a precise language that describes and manipulates the abstractions employed in our field, thereby bridging the gap between computer science, mathematics, and practical applications.

Engaging with open source, particularly JupyterLab and Vega, has provided me with unique experiences that have shaped my research interests. Initially, I embarked on a personal project to enhance collaboration within my research group, culminating in the integration of Vega-Lite visualization tools into JupyterLab. This collaboration extended to working alongside Dominik Moritz and Brian Granger, creators of the project, and ultimately led to the development of an open-source tool for Python data scientists to optimize their interactive visualizations based on user needs.

My role as a core JupyterLab developer allowed me to engage with clients integrating with the Jupyter ecosystem, share responsibilities in maintaining and enhancing the project, and secure grants, such as the Chan Zuckerberg Initiative grant to improve collaboration in Jupyter. I also led the integration of conflict-free replicated data types into JupyterLab, an experience that underscored the importance of navigating complex multi-stakeholder projects while addressing real-world user needs.

The open-source Python data science community has motivated my research on its intertwined technical and social challenges. The emergence of heterogeneous hardware has compounded the complexity of developing numeric libraries, leading to fragmentation in the ecosystem and centralization in single-stakeholder projects like Jax, PyTorch, and Mojo. I believe that these "exilic spaces," as defined by O’Hearn and Grubačić, are vital living alternatives to the market economy, and protecting them through tools that promote a bottom-up ecosystem can facilitate innovation and increase diversity within the ecosystem.

My academic journey began at Quansight, where I worked with Travis Oliphant, creator of NumPy, on researching a new foundation for the array ecosystem in Python. This led to collaborating with Lenore Mullin, whose 1980s work on the 'Mathematics of Arrays' formalizing APL with the lambda calculus proved instrumental. Together, we integrated her work into Python, resulting in the development of a generic framework, metadsl, for representing and optimizing expressions in Python. However, securing funding for such experimental technology proved challenging.

Post-Quansight, I joined Linea, a startup that aimed to bring database and programming language theory to assist Python data scientists. As one of their first engineers, I spearheaded the development of Linea’s abstract analysis engine, transforming Python code into a functional dataflow graph to support program slicing and lineage analysis. After leaving Linea, I shifted my focus back to developing a system for abstract interpretation in Python.

My ongoing work with e-graphs in Python offers a solid foundation for my research goals in pursuing a Ph.D. In egglog, the framework building e-graphs on top of datalog, I am intrigued by the potential of adding parameterized types and higher-order functions to improve expressivity and the relationship to existing compute engines, such as SQL and differential dataflow. Additionally, I want to explore how existing functional abstractions like the Mathematics of Arrays and the RSVDG can be implemented within the e-graph framework to provide a general-purpose compiler framework for numeric computation, AI, and data science.

More broadly, I am interested in studying, through the lens of human-computer interaction, how frameworks for type-driven programming can assist in cross-domain and language translation to foster inter-group collaboration between library developers. Furthermore, I am keen on delving into the foundations of mathematics to help us reason about the semantics of programming languages.

At the University of Washington, I envision collaborating with Zachary Tatlock, Gilbert Bernstein, Dan Grossman, and Luis Ceze on studying the abstract interpretation and compilation of machine learning code in Python. Moreover, I aim to bridge the gap between the academic programming language community and the Python ecosystem, a high-impact field that would benefit from a foundational approach to studying abstraction. By enrolling in your graduate program, I seek to develop longer-term working relationships with other researchers in an environment supportive of experimentation, thereby connecting abstract research with the needs of existing open-source communities.
