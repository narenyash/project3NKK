STATEMENT OF PURPOSE
CHENGLEI SI

From a tender age, I immersed myself in the realm of natural language processing (NLP), a journey that preceded my college years. Over the ensuing years, I have been privy to the swift progression of large language models (LLMs) and have cultivated an unwavering focus on high-level research questions that remain pertinent in the long term. My curiosity is particularly piqued by two pressing questions: (i) As LLMs demonstrate impressive empirical performance across a broad spectrum of downstream tasks, giving rise to numerous real-world applications, how can we ensure they are reliable for human users? (ii) In my dual capacity as a linguistics and computer science major, I often ponder: what insights can linguistics offer for the modeling and evaluation of language models? And conversely, what can language models contribute to our understanding of linguistics?

1. The Pursuit of Reliable Language Models

I am passionately invested in the impact of LLMs on human users, a concern that has driven me to delve into the realm of reliability—ensuring LLMs do not pose harm to humans and fostering effective collaboration between humans and AI on complex real-world tasks.

User-centric calibration: In our recent EMNLP Findings paper [2], I investigated how to calibrate model confidence to aid users in discerning accurate predictions from erroneous ones. I discovered that existing calibration metrics fail to account for the distinction between correct and incorrect predictions, while post-hoc calibration methods like temperature scaling generate ambiguous confidence scores for all predictions, making it challenging for humans to differentiate between the two. To remedy this shortcoming, I proposed an enhanced calibration metric, MacroCE, and a novel calibration method, ConsCal, which capitalizes on the consistency of training trajectory to calibrate model confidence. Our human study demonstrated that confidence scores calibrated by ConsCal significantly improve user decision-making when distinguishing correct from incorrect predictions, outperforming all other baselines; and our metric MacroCE aligns more closely with human preference.

Prompting GPT-3 for reliability: In another paper under review [5], I honed my focus on the cutting-edge GPT-3 model that is deployed in numerous real-life applications. I devised effective prompting strategies that render GPT-3 resilient against out-of-distribution examples, minimize social biases towards minority groups, generate calibrated confidence scores, and update outdated factual knowledge or flawed reasoning chains. Our methodologies not only serve as a practical guide for all users of GPT-3, but the results also offer fresh insights into the reliability of the burgeoning prompting paradigm with billion-scale LLMs.

The road ahead: The emergence of ever-larger LLMs and the in-context learning paradigm pose new challenges. For instance, how can we stress-test or identify failure cases for hundred-billion-scale LLMs without exorbitant searches? How can we teach them to detect and disregard malicious or misleading prompts; to recognize, prevent, or rectify harmful and hallucinated generations? Beyond targeted solutions for specific reliability issues and models, I am also interested in broader, principle-driven approaches that align language models with prosocial values and objectives. Additionally, inspired by Prof. Hal Daumé III's human-AI interaction seminar, I am eager to explore efficient and effective human-LLM collaboration strategies (such as eliciting feedback from humans and incorporating human feedback to improve models) on critical real-world tasks.

2. The Symbiotic Connection between Linguistics and NLP

Despite ongoing debates within the NLP community, I firmly believe that linguistics retains an essential role in helping us comprehend the capabilities of language models and potentially developing LLMs with better inductive biases. I am also actively contemplating how computational methods can contribute to psycholinguistics, such as reverse engineering human language intelligence.

Exploring linguistic biases in LLMs: In my other paper under submission [3], I delved into the linguistic biases of LLMs through the lens of spurious correlations. I compiled a comprehensive benchmark to quantify the extent to which LLMs favor various linguistic features (such as punctuation, lexicon, n-grams, phrase structure rules, and lexical overlap). For both supervised finetuning (on BERT) and few-shot prompting (on GPT-3), we observed consistent patterns: content word features, n-gram features, and lexical overlap features are favored more than function word features and syntactic features. Moreover, training a randomly initialized Transformer exploits all these features to a similar and substantial degree, suggesting that pretraining imposes inductive biases that prefer certain linguistic features over others.

Linguistic insights for superior modeling: Most LLMs employ sub-word units for language representation, which primarily aligns with the linguistic concept of morphemes (stem, suffix, and affix). However, this sub-word paradigm is inapplicable to ideographic Chinese, owing to the absence of morphological inflection. In our TACL paper [7], for the first time in Chinese NLP, I proposed decomposing Chinese characters into smaller sub-character units via their shape (radicals and strokes) and pronunciation, and composing the meaning of complex characters with the representations of these sub-character units. LLMs trained with this method demonstrated strong performance on downstream tasks, with significantly better efficiency and robustness than existing baselines.

Future directions: I have long been concerned with the construct validity of current benchmarks: many language datasets may not even necessitate true language understanding, as models might exploit shallow statistical cues in the datasets, as suggested by our analysis [4]). For future exploration, I aim to understand the extent and nature of linguistic knowledge required for our NLP benchmarks, construct new probing sets that avoid spurious biases if existing benchmarks fall short of this goal. These can then facilitate the development of better modeling designs or training objectives for LLMs to enhance their language understanding capabilities. Additionally, inspired by Prof. Philip Resnik and Prof. Naomi Feldman's computational psycholinguistics seminars, I am also interested in computational models that explain human behavior or neural data as a means to reverse engineer the mechanisms of human language processing.

3. Research Style and Goals

Working closely with my advisor Prof. Jordan Boyd-Graber over the past three years has shaped my research style and taste. I strongly advocate for evaluation: many of my works focus on improving existing evaluation practices and metrics (such as rectifying the flaws of popular calibration metrics [2], advocating for incorporating equivalent answer sets for QA evaluation [1], clarifying often-confused adversarial attack evaluation paradigms [6], constructing targeted stress tests [9], and when we find existing benchmarks unrealistic—we crowdsource our own [8]). I strive to ensure that our evaluation methods align with what we truly wish to measure (by meticulously examining the data and predictions). Moreover, I relish collaborating with researchers from diverse backgrounds, which cultivates an open-mindedness and appreciation for diverse perspectives. For example, I value linguistic insights as highly as modern statistical approaches, and I also read widely in human-computer interaction and cognitive science. I enjoy speaking to audiences outside of AI (such as at UMD’s Language Science Center) about the broader impact and connections of my work. I aspire to continue such collaboration and interdisciplinary discussions during my PhD.

Career Goals: My long-term career goal is to become a professor. I have savored collaborating with different individuals on topics that ignite my passion. The academic freedom offered in this profession captivates me, making academia an attractive pursuit. I derive immense satisfaction from staying abreast of the latest research advancements and making real-world impact through my research. Moreover, I have benefitted immensely from the mentorship of many faculty advisors. I aspire to pay it forward by helping the next generation of aspiring researchers.
