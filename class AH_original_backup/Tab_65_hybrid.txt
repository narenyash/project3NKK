Title: Cultivating Resilient Machine Learning Systems: A Journey of Discovery and Innovation

In the ever-evolving landscape of machine learning (ML), my passion lies in creating practical solutions that fortify ML systems against naturally occurring shifts in their environment, enabling them to thrive in the real world at scale. This pursuit has been a cornerstone of my undergraduate studies at IIT Delhi and subsequent tenure as a Research Fellow at Microsoft Research (MSR). Along this path, I've published three papers in prestigious conferences such as CVPR, ECML, MLSys, and submitted one to ICLR. In the following narrative, I delve into some of my past research experiences and the insights they've provided.

Navigating Robust Training with Missing Labels
---------------------------------------------

In medical and web domains, it is not uncommon for observed training data distributions to deviate from those we aim to learn about. This issue manifests as mislabeled data points or systematic missing labels due to inherent biases in the policies or user clicks used to curate datasets. In tasks such as Extreme multi-label Classification (XC), where only a few labels out of millions are relevant per data point, obtaining all relevant labels is impractical. Consequently, these datasets suffer from similar challenges [1]. Here, I discuss two approaches I developed at MSR to learn robust extreme classifiers that overcome these hurdles.

### Leveraging Inductive Biases

In long-tailed distributions, where the majority of labels have limited training points, missing labels pose a significant challenge, especially in applications like recommendation. The rare or tail labels often provide niche and informative results for users, but extreme classifiers can overfit to these labels, resulting in poor generalization on the majority label space. To bridge this performance gap on rare labels, I collaborated with Dr. Manik Varma. By observing that siamese-style encoders perform well on rare labels by aligning semantically similar items due to information sharing among labels, we devised a simple distillation framework to supervise extreme classifiers using the relevance probability scores of the siamese encoder amongst labels and data points. This approach resulted in substantial improvements (up to 5% absolute) in rare label accuracies across benchmark datasets [3], without compromising head label accuracies. Classifiers trained with this framework were deployed on Microsoft's Bing search engine for sponsored search, demonstrating its practical applicability at scale. This work is currently under review at ICLR 2024 and has received positive feedback (5,6,6,8 / 10 points). Through this endeavor, I learned how good inductive biases (semantically similar items close) can facilitate generalization, even with low-accuracy models like siamese encoders.

### Embracing Data-Centric Approaches

The aforementioned approach is limited because it fails to account for relevant missing pairs that have no textual or semantic similarity. For instance, data points such as "ferrari" are often associated with categories like "expensive cars" or "luxury vehicles." In response, I embarked on an ongoing research project, advised by Drs. Amit Sharma and Manik Varma, aiming to solve this root issue. Initially, we used Large Language Models (LLMs) to generate relevant pseudo-labels for data points, but they struggled to scale beyond a few thousand points. Smaller LMs lacked the parametric knowledge to generate diverse pseudo-labels and often failed to recognize categories like "hewlett packard" [7]. However, I discovered that data points usually have abundant associated information in an uncurated format. For example, search queries have webpage titles (and content) associated with them, while products have product descriptions. Thus, I proposed using a smaller LM to generate task-specific pseudo-labels for a data point using this uncurated information. The smaller LM is aligned/supervised using a more comprehensive LLM like GPT4 for a specific task accordingly. These generated unsupervised but task-specific pseudo-labels can now be used to supervise efficient models, potentially learning robust representations that generalize well to Out-Of-Distribution (OOD) labels due to the diversity of the generated pairs. Furthermore, I've learned that simple data-centric methods can yield powerful results in learning good representations.

Reliable Machine Learning: A Journey of Insights and Realizations
-------------------------------------------------------------------

During my undergrad at IIT Delhi, I collaborated with Prof. Chetan Arora on the problem of calibration in deep learning. Neural networks are considered calibrated when their predictions align with the actual probability of an event occurring. This is crucial for safety-critical applications such as medical diagnosis or self-driving cars where uncertainty estimates (probabilistic predictions) allow ML systems to establish trust and defer to humans when necessary. Training classifiers using hard one-hot labels can lead to overfitting and miscalibration [8, 9]. To counteract this, I realized that using pretrained models could be a promising approach for providing label distributions during training. By employing this insight, I used a pretrained but calibrated classifier to provide accurate uncertainty estimates in the form of probabilistic predictions for an image, supervising another classifier. This straightforward method resulted in more accurate and calibrated students that outperformed or matched current baselines [10]. We demonstrated that we could only distill calibrated classifiers from calibrated teachers, a fascinating discovery. Interestingly, we discovered that smaller calibrated teachers could also distill calibrated classifiers as long as they were calibrated. With the help of a fellow undergrad, I conducted extensive experiments across datasets and submitted this work to CVPR 2024. This work built upon my previous project with Prof. Chetan Arora, which was accepted as an oral presentation (top 4.2%) at CVPR 2022 [11], underscoring the importance of high-quality labels and supervision from other ML models to approximate them effectively.

Large-scale Machine Learning: Scaling for Impact
---------------------------------------------------

Being part of an industrial research lab afforded me the opportunity to collaborate with data scientists across various product groups within Microsoft, learning valuable engineering skills and good code practices in the process. I applied the distillation framework described earlier [4] on a real-world large-scale sponsored search dataset collected using Bing click logs containing nearly 80 million labels. This deployment resulted in a 25% increase in offline metrics and significant increases in clickthrough rates and user satisfaction online, demonstrating that simple ideas can yield impressive results on massive datasets.

Future Directions: A Research Agenda for Practical Solutions
-------------------------------------------------------------

My past experiences have informed my research interests and emphasized the importance of creating practical methods to handle distribution changes effectively, particularly at scale. During graduate studies, I aim to employ both theoretical and empirical approaches to develop practical methods tailored for real-world challenges. A potential direction where my previous research experience can contribute is exploring the role of data in robustness. One question directly relevant to my current work: Can we create algorithms that learn task-specific representations capable of generalizing to hard-to-characterize natural shifts, leveraging abundant uncurated or unlabeled data? Another direction could involve understanding foundational models' roles in robustness: a possible question is, how can we effectively exploit their general-purpose representations, learned from diverse naturally occurring data, in training task-specific models that can adapt when deployed in ever-changing real-world environments? Additionally, I would be interested in exploring the empirical study of fundamental ML model properties and utilizing these learnings to develop better methods for addressing distribution changes. Lastly, I aim to complement my research work with extensive theoretical courses during graduate studies to gain tools for conducting theoretical research work.
