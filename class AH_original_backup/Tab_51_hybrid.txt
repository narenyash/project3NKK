Growing up bilingual in Basque and Spanish, I was constantly reminded of the intricate challenges and opportunities presented by low-resource languages (LRLs). At the nexus of my passions – linguistics and technology – these languages have long held a captivating allure. As I delved deeper into my undergraduate studies, I stumbled upon computational linguistics (CL) and natural language processing (NLP), igniting a growing fascination with the interplay between language and technology.

My academic journey led me to participate in NLP seminars offered by the University of the Basque Country, where I honed my skills on various downstream tasks. It became evident that studying language need not be an isolated endeavor but rather a dynamic discipline that could help tackle the challenges I envisioned. To reduce language barriers and promote technological representativeness for minority languages, a Ph.D. would offer me the opportunity to explore this interdisciplinary area and unlock my full potential as a researcher.

A Multidisciplinary Approach. Initially drawn to semantics, pragmatics, and syntax, technology soon became an integral part of my journey, guiding me towards computational linguistics. I was particularly captivated by the poor performance of language technologies for Basque, and I found myself eager to develop innovative methodologies that could elevate LRLs. To achieve this, incorporating cognitive science holds great promise in unveiling more efficient solutions, ultimately increasing the effectiveness of smaller datasets – a common limitation for LRLs. A Ph.D. integrating linguistics, CL, and cognitive science will equip me with multidisciplinary skills essential for achieving my objectives.

My Research. Over the course of working on projects that involved LRLs, I have developed valuable techniques to address my primary goal: ensuring that these languages are heard. In my first study, I used language models (LM) code-switching reproduction to assess their low-resource multilingual capabilities using Basque-English as a benchmark [4]. I analyzed various factors, including output naturalness, code-switching aptitude, and hallucination frequency. This research, which I will present at EMNLP WiNLP 2023, revealed that some LMs coherently combined Basque and English syntax and morphology. Furthermore, they demonstrated the ability to code-switch with Spanish and French when asked to reproduce conversations between native Basque speakers, languages not mentioned in the prompts. This finding underscored their cross-lingual capabilities and knowledge about language location in the real world.

That sparked a profound curiosity within me regarding the hidden linguistic competencies of LMs. My discovery that models possess accurate information on language in contact led me to explore how prompting can influence model responses. To do so, I compared the perplexity, ROGUE scores, length, lexical diversity (MTLD), human evaluations, and self-attention maps of role-playing and standard prompts [5]. My findings showed that role-playing enhanced the quality of generated text across several dimensions. With this project, I gained deep technical insights into analyzing attention distributions of LMs and reaffirmed the importance of creativity in overcoming linguistic dead-ends, particularly for underrepresented languages.

This trajectory led me to exciting new research questions that highlight the role of LRLs in AI safety: how are LRLs and prompting techniques connected? My preliminary results suggest that combining role-play prompting and LRLs (e.g., Haitian Creole or Quechua) can bypass state-of-the-art LMs (e.g., generate hate speech). I also found a correlation between the degree of representativeness and attack success rates. I will submit this study to NAACL 2024.

In parallel to my academic path, I am currently working with Cohere for AI, a non-profit research lab led by Dr. Sara Hooker [3]. Here, I lead the Basque group in a project aiming to build a state-of-the-art, open-source, multilingual dataset. At Cohere, I have had the opportunity to collaborate with NLP experts, honing my skills and expanding my network, allowing for exchanges of ideas and collaboration opportunities. Furthermore, I have become adept at data curation and realized the importance of data quality. A fruit of my time at the lab has been the Visibility Project [6], a site I founded to shed light on underrepresented languages by offering insights into their linguistic features and presence in NLP research.

Collaborating with native LRL speakers in my research has deepened my understanding of linguistic diversity and its significance. Each of my experiences has contributed to my growth as a researcher and improved my comprehension of the research process. Collectively, these projects represent my commitment to advancing the field of CL, particularly for languages that have historically been marginalized in technology.

Future Projects and Broader Impact. Understanding language processing in the brain can open new research avenues for efficient modeling approaches for underrepresented languages. Incorporating generative adversarial networks (GANs) presents a transformative advantage due to their exceptional ability to generate high-quality, interpretable data from limited datasets [1], a vital capability for advancing language models in low-resource contexts. This interest leads me towards collaboration with Dr. Gašper Beguš. Building on his research, I aim to investigate these architectures, assessing their potential applications for LRL contexts. I would like to explore the similarities between predictive coding in speech perception [8] and the workings of GANs. Using an extension of the method proposed by Beguš and Zhao (2022) [2], I want to follow a comparative framework that maps EEG patterns to convolutional neural network feature maps. This study could reveal the extent to which GANs mimic human speech processing and how these models might be improved based on the brain’s approach to predictive coding.

I am also intrigued by exploring how neural models can help us learn more about language. As a linguist, I have always been fascinated with Universal Grammar and the Poverty of the Stimulus (PoS) argument. With Dr. Terry Regier [7], I want to investigate how LMs learn as linguistic inductive biases are increased. This could offer new perspectives on the PoS argument and inform us whether some grammatical features are more readily learned. In this framework, I am excited about experimenting with artificial languages [9].

Focusing on the dynamic intersection of linguistic representativeness, cognition, and computation, the projects I aim to develop at UC Berkeley articulate a pursuit of technological justice. I see my research interests as interconnected pieces that aim to achieve fair linguistic representation in language technologies.

Why UC Berkeley? As a leading institution for both computational and traditional linguistics, UC Berkeley offers an ideal environment to continue my education. At UC Berkeley, I am able to explore the intersection of linguistics and cognitive science while continuing to delve into low-resource languages. This integration aligns with my academic pursuits and provides multidisciplinary expertise, essential for adopting a comprehensive focus to tackle the problems I aim to solve. Engaging in research within separate subfields ensures rigorous training and allows me to explore the implications of my research in multiple fields, preparing me for a successful career in academia.

My work has amply prepared me for doctoral studies, not only in my research abilities but also in my excitement to contribute to UC Berkeley’s forward-thinking and innovative academic environment.

Grounded on my connection to low-resource languages, I am determined to make a lasting impact on the field and advocate for their fair representation in technology.
