Title: Fostering Innovation in Computational Biology: A Journey of Discovery and Impact

My ardent passion for computational biology, a dynamic field where my academic foundation in computer science and mathematics seamlessly intertwines due to the generation of vast quantities of data, has always been a driving force. This realm offers an opportunity to make a significant impact on human health by advancing our understanding and improving outcomes.

Through my computational research in genomics, I have gained priceless experience in developing and implementing analytical tools for large-scale genomic datasets. Moreover, this journey has honed my scientific communication skills, as evidenced by a first-author manuscript, a conference presentation, and two invited seminar talks.

In my pursuit of future research, I aim to leverage computing power and statistical methods to untangle the intricate puzzles behind genetic variation, ultimately enhancing health outcomes. Particularly captivating are the development of efficient algorithms and machine-learning methods for tasks such as sequence alignment, genome assembly, and variant effect prediction, as well as questions within the realm of evolutionary and population genomics. With my robust background in computer science, mathematics, and genomics, I am poised to embark on a Ph.D., continuing my research journey in computational and statistical genomics.

Embarking on a research expedition in Dr. Vagheesh Narasimhan’s lab, I found myself captivated by the way we decipher the mysteries of nature through the analysis of raw sequences of nucleotides. In my project, I delved into DNA extracted from ancient human remains to scrutinize the genomic implications of profound cultural evolution over the past ten millennia in Europe. By employing increased sample sizes and applying this method across temporal slices, we were able to pinpoint the timing of signals to three distinct periods for the first time. This breakthrough enabled us to formulate more informed hypotheses about the cultural transformations that may have precipitated these genetic adaptations. Remarkably, most of our uncovered signals of selection were novel and concentrated in earlier time periods (around 6,000 to 8,000 years ago), underscoring the significance of the "time stamp" embedded within ancient DNA data.

During this project, I managed all of our analyses, necessitating the creation of efficient code to process segments of large datasets in parallel on supercomputing resources. Post-analysis, I contributed to the interpretation and contextualization of our results by acquiring relevant knowledge in evolutionary and population genomics. Initially, my work focused on implementing a method developed by my colleagues; however, I later participated in the development of a new statistical tool designed to detect selection acting on complex traits. This dual role was both challenging and invigorating, as population geneticists have previously identified many caveats and obstacles to the interpretation of signals of selection on complex traits. The statistical tool I assisted in developing exhibited increased robustness to these challenges. I am the first author on our paper for this project, which has been preprinted and is currently under review at a journal. Furthermore, I presented this work at the American Society of Human Genetics 2022 Annual Meeting, as well as invited seminar talks for the Variant Effect Seminar Series and UT Austin RNA & DNA Club.

Prior to my genomics research, I eagerly anticipated applying my computational and statistical acumen to other research areas that required analyzing large and complex datasets. My inaugural research experience was as a student in the Freshman Research Initiative Computational Materials stream. My independent project investigated the use of Newton's method for finding stable states of a chemical system, which can fail due to areas of negative curvature on the potential energy surface. I devised various solutions to address this problem and compared their performances for the Lennard-Jones potential. This experience ignited my passion for performing scientific research, as I recognized the pleasure derived from formulating, designing, and executing research projects.

Continuing in the Computational Materials research stream, I developed software for a haptic device that simulates forces for chemical systems, providing users with an intuitive understanding of atomic interactions. Additionally, I worked on integrating machine-learning generated potential energy surfaces with simulation software. I presented a poster over this work at the 2019 Texas Advanced Computing Center Symposium for Texas Researchers, marking my first project involving machine-learning. This endeavor piqued my interest in learning more about machine-learning techniques that I now aspire to apply to biological data.

Later, through an individual research course to complete a certificate in Computational Science, I collaborated with Dr. David Halpern and Dr. Patrick Heimbach on a project in physical oceanography that evaluated the accuracy of the ocean currents produced by the Estimating the Circulation and Climate of the Ocean (ECCO) consortium’s predictive model. We aimed to learn how the ECCO model predictions could be improved for various current characteristics, as the model might potentially replace expensive moored measurements. I authored all the code and performed all the analyses, involving the analysis of large datasets, validating my programs on test datasets, formulating hypotheses for the differences we observed between datasets, and testing those hypotheses. Ultimately, we discovered that the ECCO model currents were not suitable substitutes for moored measurements; however, we devised equations that could augment the model to improve the use of model currents as proxies for moored measurements. I am the second author on a manuscript that is under review.

My immersion in diverse computational science research has fostered my ability to think interdisciplinarily and synthesize information across multiple fields, as well as analyze complex datasets and formulate research questions. I am thrilled about applying my computational and statistical knowledge to the development of novel methods that will advance our understanding of genomics in unprecedented ways, with the ultimate aim of improving healthcare outcomes. In addition to my research activities, I have enjoyed mentoring fellow students as they unravel complex concepts and establish strong foundations in fundamental skills during my tenure as a teaching assistant, tutor, and peer research mentor. For these reasons, I aspire to pursue a research career in computational biology as a professor.

I am drawn towards continuing my research journey at Harvard due to its interdisciplinary strength in biology, statistics, and computing, as well as my interest in the research being conducted by the Bioinformatics and Integrative Genomics faculty. Specifically, my background in computer science and mathematics makes me an ideal fit for projects in Dr. Heng Li's lab, where I would be interested in developing efficient and scalable algorithms for genome compression and sequence alignment. I am also intrigued by working with Dr. Shamil Sunyaev on problems related to the architecture and evolution of complex traits, a domain I would be well-suited to contribute to due to my past research analyzing natural selection on complex traits. Additionally, I am interested in the statistical methods developed in Dr. Po-Ru Loh's lab, including their linear mixed models algorithms for association analyses and work related to mosaic chromosomal alterations. I believe there is a strong alignment between my skills and interests at Harvard, and that the Bioinformatics and Integrative Genomics program would be an ideal environment for me to grow as a researcher and pursue my goals.
