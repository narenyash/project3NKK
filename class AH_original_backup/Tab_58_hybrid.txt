I am deeply passionate about crafting innovative solutions for resource management, scheduling, scalability in operating systems, memory and storage optimization, and the seamless integration of heterogeneous architectures. As a Master’s student at UC San Diego, I've discovered that curiosity is not just about seeking answers but also about uncovering questions one might not have been aware of.

My transformative journey began during my first quarter at UC San Diego in Prof. Yuanyuan Zhou’s Graduate Operating Systems course. This course veered away from the conventional approach of black-and-white solutions, instead delving into understanding various authors' rationales behind their chosen approaches—a philosophy that resonated with me deeply. I found myself captivated when authors opted for alternative solutions over the commonly accepted ones, as their reasoning often suited specific systems better. This experience illuminated the philosophical nature of computer systems, where absolute truth is replaced by a myriad of trade-offs.

My initial foray into research was with Prof. Amy Ousterhout, working on a project aimed at enhancing CPU efficiency by offloading datacenter tax—encryption, compression, and copying—to SmartNIC accelerators. To evaluate performance differences, I zeroed in on applications that consumed substantial CPU cycles due to datacenter tax and conducted comprehensive benchmarking analyses with and without offloading the tax. The project commenced with the Synthetic Web Service application developed by AIFM (Application-Integrated Far Memory). Given the need for performance benchmarking on a single machine, I extricated the application's data structures from their inherent far memory usage and then ported the Shenango-based application over to Caladan, a revolutionary CPU scheduler that superseded Shenango. Upon offloading compression tasks to a Mellanox ConnectX-5 SmartNIC, we observed significant performance improvements—specifically, a 3.5x boost in throughput and a 20% reduction in latency. This project piqued my interest due to its practicality: in today’s datacenters, there is an escalating demand for servers, exacerbated by the waning effects of Moore's Law. By offloading datacenter tax to hardware accelerators, we have a promising opportunity to boost CPU efficiency and curtail costs associated with scaling computer systems.

Another notable project I undertook was Brim, an innovative far memory system, in collaboration with Prof. Amy Ousterhout, Prof. Alex Snoeren, and Anil Yelam, a PhD student at UCSD. Most existing far-memory systems employ kernel-based paging, resulting in significant overhead and limiting application control over memory management. Brim features a hint API to notify the pager about anticipated cold memory accesses, all while preserving the conventional page-based memory interface. I contributed significantly to its evaluation by comparing the performance of memory-intensive applications such as DataFrame and AIFM’s applications with Brim and alternative far memory systems. Our findings revealed Brim's performance to be up to 150% higher than Fastswap, a result currently under review at a top-tier systems conference. This endeavor allowed me to delve into the intriguing realm of far memory systems and comprehend their importance in the first place. I also gained insight into the value of collaboration and the power of bringing together individuals from diverse disciplines to devise solutions. The professors' emphasis on the entire process of reproducing results underscored the significance of producing accurate and reliable findings.

As an undergraduate junior, I embarked upon teaching Computer Architecture and Python seminars, reinforcing my belief that true understanding precedes effective teaching. As a summer fellow at IIT Bombay, I crafted comprehensive lecture notes and animations for various topics in Advanced Mathematics under the guidance of Prof. Prabhu Ramachandran. This experience taught me the importance of precise citations, ensuring that readers have access to relevant and reliable sources of information. These teaching experiences instilled in me a desire to pursue a PhD, as I aim to make substantial advancements to systems research.

The pandemic provided an unexpected challenge when students were required to resubmit programming assignments on multiple disjoint platforms due to the limitations of each platform. Unintentionally, this situation violated the fundamental principle of programming—“Don’t Repeat Yourself.” To address this issue, I spearheaded the development of a unified web environment for graders to execute PAs with an incorporated pseudoterminal, perform plagiarism checks, and grade submissions. This project culminated in the establishment of SudoLMS, which attracted over 600 active users at its peak. This experience affirmed my enthusiasm for creating novel solutions that positively impact the academic community.

Pursuing a PhD will enable me to create systems by rethinking the foundations upon which current systems are built, while also exploring new avenues of systems research. The PhD program at UT Austin offers an ideal platform to explore these interests. I am particularly drawn to working with Prof. Chris Rossbach, Prof. Emmett Witchel, and Prof. Daehyeok Kim. Prof. Rossbach's work on machine learning for systems and unifying heterogeneous architectures aligns perfectly with my aspirations. I am also eager to collaborate with Prof. Witchel at the intersection of storage systems, cloud computing, and parallelism. Furthermore, Prof. Kim’s research on operating systems for datacenters and hardware accelerators resonates deeply with my experiences in these fields.

Upon completing graduate studies, I envision a career that encompasses both academia and industry contributions to systems research. I am driven by the prospect of teaching—the joy of conveying my understanding of ideas in a way that others can grasp truly fills me with happiness. A career in academia presents this opportunity to teach and potentially shape the academic journeys of future generations of brilliant minds.
