Tab 9
Reflect upon something substantial that you have undertaken to transform yourself, in either your professional or personal pursuits.

One radiant morning a few years past, I was commuting to work when I listened to Billy Collins, the former Poet Laureate of the United States, reciting his poem "Introduction to Poetry" on the radio. As he concluded, I halted my vehicle and sat idly on the sidewalk for a while. It was imperative that I did so. This is why I pen poems: to make others late for their engagements.

My childhood was marked by an aggravating speech impediment, a stutter that cast a long shadow over my formative years. A typical school morning's roll call would unfold like this: "...Roll number 21?" "Present!" "22?" "Present, miss!" "23?" "P...p...p..." "...24?"

Throughout my educational journey, I was student number 23. My speech impediment made even the most mundane activities challenging: asking a question in class was an ordeal, engaging in conversation seemed impossible.

My odyssey to conquer stuttering began when I discovered poetry as part of our school curriculum. In poetry, I found the courage and motivation that altered my life trajectory. P.K. Page's poem "This Heavy Craft" still evokes memories of the early days of my struggle: "I, Icarus, though grounded / in my flesh / have one bright section in me / where a bird / night after starry night / while I'm asleep / unfolds its phantom wings / and practices." In Icarus' journey, I recognized my own.

Drawn by its fluidity, I began reciting poetry to myself, mirroring my reflection. The slow, rhythmic cadence of verse compelled me to articulate clearly and helped me identify the letters I struggled with. I compiled a list of words that triggered my stutter and isolated the challenging sounds – "p," "d," "n," and "s." Through disciplined practice, I noticed that regulating my breath and composure when faced with offending words lessened my disfluencies.

As my confidence swelled, I pushed myself to step out of my solitary sanctuary and participate in class discussions. It was daunting at first, often resulting in embarrassment, but even my modest victories – a five-minute dialogue in a small group, a stanza recited to a classroom of 50 – sustained my resolve.

Having recited poetry for so long, the art of composition was the logical next step. Fearing self-isolation once more, I challenged myself to engage local writing communities wherever I resided. For the past eight years, I have managed an online poetry club that I initiated at IIT-Bombay. In Austin, I established the city's first poetry club, grew it from five to over 100 members in the year I oversaw its management, and organized monthly poetry readings at coffee shops and book clubs. Today, the club thrives.

Reading poetry aloud in public improved my speech, while collaborating constructively with fellow writers brought luster to my own verse. In 2009, I was awarded a National Poetry Prize in India – a testament not only to my poetic prowess but also to my unwavering commitment to self-improvement and resilience in the face of years of self-doubt.

As I ascended the stage to read my poem, my hands trembled with exhilaration. I was no longer afraid – neither of scorn, nor of failure. I closed my eyes; a crowd of 500 held their breath in anticipation. Once more, I found myself in my bedroom – a child gazing into the mirror. I was Icarus: wings spread, prepared for flight. I took a deep breath, and began.
