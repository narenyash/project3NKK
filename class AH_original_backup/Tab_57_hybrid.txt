A Journey into Fail-Slow Fault Tolerance
My initial foray into research focused on the underappreciated yet critical issue of fail-slow fault tolerance in distributed systems [1, 2, 3]. Fail-slow faults, such as sluggish hardware, can precipitate system-wide performance degradation but are challenging to reproduce and investigate under controlled conditions. Guided remotely by Prof. Tianyin Xu and Prof. Shuai Mu, I collaborated with fellow researchers to develop Slooo - a versatile and modular fault injection framework designed to automate the evaluation of system behavior under fail-slow faults. Slooo has been employed to study fail-slow faults [4], integrated into a graduate-level assignment at UIUC, and recently submitted to a premier SE conference. This project enriched my technical skills and knowledge base, allowing me to pursue computer science as a non-CS student with no prior background in the field.

Bridging Research and Real-world Applications
Inspired by the necessity for fault-tolerant computing services, I conceived the idea of "City Brain as Distributed HPC Containers" and led a team of four students to secure the top 3 spot out of 142 entries in a prestigious business competition for college students in China. Our proposal involved deploying citywide networks of wirelessly connected high-performance computing containers designed to accommodate both low latency and performance demands in emerging applications, such as autonomous driving, smart cities, and cloud gaming. This experience confirmed the immense potential impact that computer systems research holds - enhancing existing applications, enabling new ones, and fostering their reliability.

Ensuring the Integrity of Cluster Management Programs in Declarative Systems
During the development of Slooo, I recognized the tedious and error-prone nature of writing tests due to the output of Slooo being contingent on the state of external programs. This realization led me to embark upon my second research project under the guidance of Prof. Tianyin Xu and Prof. Owolabi Legunsen. Our goal was to create an automated testing tool for cloud management programs operating on Kubernetes - operators. Operators manage the full lifecycle of specific applications, making their correctness paramount due to their growing popularity in managing large application clusters. Our tool streamlines the testing process by employing automatic input exploration and oracle generation, effectively addressing the challenges posed by combinatorial explosion during exhaustive input exploration and codifying application-specific oracles. Our approaches have unearthed over 50 previously unidentified bugs in ten popular operators. I designed the input exploration strategies to test an operator’s error-handling logic, added additional checkers for detailed bug descriptions, and co-developed the core architecture supporting parallel testing and custom Kubernetes runtime. Beyond technical contributions, I mentored remote students and co-presented our project at an undergraduate event. For future work, we aim to decipher the semantic correctness of the generated input by linking the custom operator interface with native resources in Kubernetes, enabling more strategic and efficient input generation.
