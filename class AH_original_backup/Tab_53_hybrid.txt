Title: Enhancing Understanding and Usability of Language Models: A Journey Towards Adaptive, Controllable, and Efficient Models

My research revolves around investigating mechanisms to amplify the understanding and usability of contemporary language models. On the usability front, my role as a pre-doctoral researcher at Google Research has primarily focused on creating modular (non-monolithic) language models that are lightweight and adaptable for diverse downstream applications (such as knowledge and domain-specific tasks). I am passionate about expanding this modularity to improve efficiency and control over existing models. Currently, a generic large language model (LLM; approximately 10 billion parameters) equates to around 80 gigabytes of storage—impractical for storage on mobile devices. Given the rise of large models with multiple skills, I am eager to explore cost-effective methods for discovering sub-modules that satisfy both task and computational requirements: for instance, a translation module from an LLM, or a sticker generation module from an image generation model.

In terms of understanding, I am deeply invested in developing robust tools for identifying inductive biases in models, which can be utilized for enhanced evaluation, control, and modeling. During my undergraduate studies at the Technion Israel and long-term research collaborations with LTI CMU and CILVR Lab NYU, I delved into aligned problems.

Navigating Usability Challenges in Language Models

Today, large-scale pre-trained models, particularly LLMs, are extensively utilized for various applications like knowledge retrieval, content curation, and clinical advice, among others. However, their monolithic structure presents numerous challenges that hinder their usability. Specifically, such monolithic models are challenging to control, update with new knowledge, and serve downstream users. To illustrate, consider a conversational system designed for providing reliable clinical advice to rural mothers in India. For the system to be useful, it needs to be tailored towards specialized clinical knowledge for maternal care while remaining affordable to serve and use. Addressing these challenges is crucial for usability yet remains a challenging problem given our current tools. These issues become even more intricate when downstream users or organizations need to adapt black-box models using proprietary data. I am dedicated to designing algorithms that enable the extension and servicing of models, making them more functional and useful for real-world problems.

Composition of Language Models

During my time at Google Research, under the guidance of Dr. Partha Talukdar and Dr. Prateek Jain, I worked on a facet of this usability problem: efficiently extending general capabilities available through large models to specialized downstream knowledge. We first observed that conventional fine-tuning (training all or a subset of model parameters) for a specific capability often results in the degradation of other abilities, hence lacking robustness. Instead, we approached the problem of adaptation from a compositional perspective: a large language model (an anchor) can be augmented with new niche knowledge through composition with other, potentially smaller, specialized (augmenting) models. We found that general-purpose abilities of an anchor LLM could be extended for settings such as extremely underrepresented languages and code through composition with associated specialized LMs. In our recent ICLR’24 submission (Bansal et al., 2024), we grounded our framework on learning a few parameters over layer representations of the models, preserving their original capabilities.

Due to our emphasis on foundational design principles of modularity, model re-use, and efficiency in our work, we are now collaborating closely with Google DeepMind and product teams at Google. We are exploring a novel realm of serving foundation models to downstream users that allows them to adapt a model to their custom data in a compositional manner. I view this work as an initiation towards a line of research focused on adaptive, controllable, and hence more useful models.

Embracing Efficiency for More Practical Models

Moving forward, I am eager to delve deeper into the problem of efficiency—another critical pillar towards more useful models. While our current work enables new capabilities through the composition of multiple models, I am keen to explore the converse problem: Given a large general model, can we extract multiple specialized models that fit a specified computational budget or task requirements? I believe such methodologies can leverage the sparsity of existing models to create more practical and deployable versions. This is especially relevant in light of recent results where task-specific small models are shown to outperform large generic models (Raffel et al., 2023). Initial lines of work towards this end propose an inherently modified pre-training scheme (Kudugunta et al., 2023) as well as post-hoc algorithms (Zhang et al., 2022), both of which are exciting directions.

Enhancing Understanding of Language Models

As a step towards larger goals, it is essential to elevate our understanding of current systems. I believe that interpretability and analysis of models can provide a systematic way to understand the limitations and strengths of contemporary models. Unraveling a model's decision process into a human-comprehensible set of inductive biases could open avenues for attributing computation rules behind model predictions, discerning how a set of training examples are generalized to learn decision rules, and finally controlling undesirable behavior. I am especially excited about employing robust model understanding tools for use cases in evaluation and modeling.

Evaluating Explanations

What cannot be measured cannot be improved. Although numerous efforts have made considerable progress towards the overarching goal of model understanding, no proper means for evaluation (and hence measuring progress) exist. As a fundamental step towards enhancing model understanding, I joined Prof. Danish Pruthi at LTI CMU to ground and quantify the notion of explanation quality. Our formulation is based on the core use of explanations: A means of communication in any teacher-student setup. Imagine a student A is provided with some pairs of quadratic equations with their roots, while another student B is given an explanation for how each equation was solved. The relative ability of the two students to solve quadratic equations in the wild reflects the quality of explanations given to student B. Our TACL paper (Pruthi et al., 2022) formalizes this methodology to evaluate saliency attribution methods for text models.

Evaluating Generalization via Model Analysis

I have utilized model analysis tools to evaluate generalization attributes like reliance on spurious correlations and memorization of individual training examples. During my undergraduate thesis with Prof. Yonatan Belinkov at the Technion, I studied information distribution across neuron activations and discovered strong correlation patterns with distinct generalization behaviors. Our work, presented at NeurIPS’22 (Bansal et al., 2022), bypasses the necessity for specialized out-of-distribution evaluation sets and introduces intrinsic information-theoretic metrics such as mutual information and entropy as a way to perform model selection.

Similarly, my work with Dr. Naomi Saphra and Prof. Kyunghyun Cho at CILVR Lab NYU studied mode connectivity in the loss surface of real-world text models. For the first time, we discovered that distinct modes (as basins in the loss surface) exist in the loss surface, each containing a set of models that depict a particular generalization behavior. Our work at ICLR’23 (Juneja et al., 2023) challenged conventional beliefs about how individual models are connected in the loss surface, paving the way for rethinking several foundational aspects in weight averaging, model selection, and initialization.

Going forward, I wish to continue utilizing robust model analysis tools for foundational challenges in evaluation and modeling. For instance, I am curious to investigate the role of training dynamics (or transition patterns in the loss surface) to make optimal design choices for hyperparameters and dataset mixtures for large-scale training. Moreover, I aspire to work towards the ambitious goal of identifying intermediate computations behind model outputs. Popular methods in language models like chain-of-thought style reasoning are often misinterpreted as explanations; however, past work (Lanham et al., 2023) has established that these generations are frequently unfaithful to a model's output decision process. I believe that inductive biases grounded in intermediate representations can lead to more trustworthy model understanding, and hence enhanced use for debugging and control.
