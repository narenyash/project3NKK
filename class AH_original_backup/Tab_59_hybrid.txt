My academic passion resides at the confluence of data privacy and machine learning (ML), with a primary focus on federated learning (FL) and differential privacy (DP). Since my initial foray into this field during a summer research internship at the Institute for Mathematical Science (IMS) in 2021, I've been steadfastly pursuing this area, aiming to carve out a career as an academic, through research, teaching, and service. I am excited to apply for the EECS PhD program at MIT, which presents an unparalleled opportunity to collaborate with experts, further my research, and lay the groundwork for a fulfilling career in academia.

My journey into data privacy and ML began with a summer internship at IMS, where I led a team designing a vertical FL system for dual-party financial settings, tailored to meet our sponsor's requirements – Google. Our objective was to facilitate collaborative model training between organizations such as banks and fintech companies, without compromising privacy. To achieve this, I employed homomorphic encryption within our FL system, ensuring all exchanged data remained encrypted, and calculations were performed on ciphertext. This experience solidified my interest in FL, propelling me to further delve into this field as a research assistant under Prof Xiaokui Xiao.

In my junior year, I embarked upon a project investigating the privacy of split learning (SL), an emerging alternative to FL. My work critiqued previous attacks on SL, highlighting their reliance on unrealistic assumptions and vulnerabilities in simpler models. In response, I developed practical attacks that yielded significantly better results under realistic assumptions. This work resulted in me earning the Outstanding Computing Project Prize from NUS School of Computing, as well as a first-authored paper currently under review for WWW '24, which has received generally positive reviews and grades.

My research interests subsequently veered towards theoretical problems, particularly DP as a robust framework to quantify information leakage. Supervised by Professors Xiaokui Xiao and Vincent Tan, I completed my honors thesis on graph neural networks (GNNs) with link local privacy. Our aim was to ensure privacy during GNN training via local DP, where each node/user perturbs its data with noise before transmission to the server, thus ensuring that the server receives only differentially private information. To address the inherent privacy-utility trade-off, I innovatively proposed using Bayesian estimation to estimate the graph topology, striking a balance between privacy and utility with theoretical guarantees. This work has a broader potential impact, including privacy-preserving contact tracing systems. An abstract of this work was published and presented in SIGMOD '23, where I won first-place in the SIGMOD Student Research Competition (SRC), qualifying to compete in the ACM SRC grand finals. Additionally, a full research paper first authored by me was published and presented in CCS '23. This work also earned me the University-level Outstanding Undergraduate Researcher Prize, recognizing exceptional undergraduate research contributions.

In my post-graduate studies at NUS, I became curious about the limitations of DP as a notion to quantify information leakage on individual data points. Inspired by this curiosity, we explored data distribution leakage in differentially private FL. Our findings revealed that, despite DP guarantees, it is still possible to infer the data distribution of individual organizations – a concern in scenarios where such information could divulge sensitive business insights. This exploration opened new avenues in data privacy and underscores the need for stronger protection. The paper, co-authored by me, is currently undergoing revision at TKDE.

Upon graduating from NUS with double honors degrees in CS and math, both with highest distinction, I continued as a full-time research assistant (RA). While I continued working on FL and DP, my focus expanded to include ML theory. Currently, I am working on kernelized bandits, aiming to develop Gaussian process optimization algorithms in a federated setting, with the objective of theoretically bounding both the cumulative regret and communication cost.

As a graduate student, I aspire to make significant strides in privacy-preserving ML and explore various privacy notions. To this end, I am eager to work with Prof Srini Devadas, particularly intrigued by his recent work on improvements to DP-SGD via novel optimizer designs that reduce clipping bias (S&P 2023) and adding anisotropic noise across principal subspaces (CCS 2023), as well as PAC privacy (CRYPTO 2023), a new privacy notion which allows automatic privacy measure and control. Although my background is more aligned with algorithmic (differential) privacy, I am also interested in exploring the cryptographic side of privacy, such as homomorphic encryption, multi-party computation, and private information retrieval – areas Prof Devadas has extensively worked on. I have shared preliminary thoughts on related topics with Prof Devadas, and I've had enlightening discussions with his students, Simon and Kevin, at CCS to learn more about the group.

Additionally, I am passionately interested in theoretically studying the fundamental privacy-utility trade-off in ML, and I am intrigued by Prof Martin Wainwright’s work on information-theoretical bounds of local DP, as well as his recent work on federated optimization (NeurIPS 2020) and adversarial attacks (S&P 2020). Finally, data privacy is a field deeply rooted in the database community, and privacy can only be achieved via careful design and implementation of database systems. Hence, I am interested in Prof Samuel Madden’s recent project, ATLANTIC (VLDB 2021), on building efficient and accurate database systems with differential privacy guarantees.

Looking ahead, I am eager to continue my research as a graduate student. The presence of distinguished experts in the field and a culture that supports research make MIT an ideal setting for my academic pursuits. Moreover, the high regard with which my advisor and mentor, Prof Vincent Tan – himself an esteemed MIT alumnus – speaks of the Institute, further fuels my aspiration to embark on my PhD journey here. I am confident that my academic foundation and passion for research will enable me to make meaningful contributions and thrive in the challenging yet stimulating environment at MIT.
