Title: A Symbiotic Journey: Fusing Art and Science in Computer Graphics

Ava Pun

In pursuit of a PhD in Computer Science, I am driven by a passion for merging the worlds of art and technology, specifically in the realms of graphics, vision, and artificial intelligence. This multidisciplinary endeavor encompasses a myriad of applications, from digital illustration and animation to photography, all aimed at enhancing the creative tools available to visual artists. Inspired by recent research experiences, I am currently captivated by the potential of neural rendering, scene understanding, and image generation in an artistic context.

My personal journey as a user of artistic tools has fueled my ambition. Over the past eight years, I have been actively engaged in educational cartooning, spearheading projects like MathSoc Cartoons and Academy 118. My work as a cartoon artist underscores the transformative power of engaging visuals and compelling narratives in inspiring learning. By crafting more sophisticated image-creation tools, I aspire to empower more educators to engage their students through immersive visual storytelling.

My research background is grounded in scene understanding and neural rendering of virtual environments, a field that I have explored during internships at Uber ATG (later Waabi). In my first project, AdvSim, I devised an algorithm to seamlessly merge real and simulated LiDAR points. This innovation enabled the generation of safety-critical driving simulations by realistically perturbing vehicles within a LiDAR scene. In my second project, LightSim, I ventured into neural rendering, developing an urban scene relighting method that leveraged physically-based rendering (PBR) to relight a reconstructed digital twin of the scene. Although this initial model showed promise, it struggled to completely eliminate strong lighting effects like cast shadows. To address this challenge, I am currently refining the model by designing a NeRF-based inverse rendering technique to disentangle lighting effects from intrinsic material properties. This task is particularly complex due to the difficulties faced by current state-of-the-art methods when scaling with large outdoor scenes. My ultimate goal is to create a system capable of extracting 3D geometry, materials, and lighting from any urban scene, enabling the rendering of diverse virtual environments from any viewpoint and under any lighting condition.

Prior to delving into neural rendering, I explored other computer graphics topics with artistic applications. Collaborating with Prof. Christopher Batty at the University of Waterloo, I designed and implemented a hybrid method for enhanced artistic control of fluid animations. This method combined Eulerian (grid) and Lagrangian (vortex filament) animation techniques to reproduce effects such as viscosity and buoyancy forces while preserving intricate vortex details.

As a double major in Computer Science and Combinatorics & Optimization, I also possess a robust mathematical foundation. In addition to my computer science pursuits, I have undertaken several part-time research projects that delved deeper into the underlying mathematics. One project, under the guidance of Prof. Craig Kaplan, involved enumerating and visualizing polyform tilings, a task that led to the discovery of an aperiodic monotile, resolving a 60-year-old open mathematics problem! Another project saw me collaborate with Prof. Ian Munro to design a compact AVL tree representation. My findings demonstrated that my representation utilized less than one bit per node, coming remarkably close to the information-theoretic lower bound of 0.939 bits. These research experiences have cultivated a comprehensive understanding of computer science and its foundational mathematics.

In the future, I am intrigued by the prospect of investigating non-photorealistic neural rendering. While I have already worked on image relighting for real scenes, applying this concept to illustrations presents unique challenges. The ill-defined nature of shadowing in 2D drawings necessitates a focus on visual appeal over physical plausibility. Could a generative model, trained on an extensive dataset of aesthetic artworks, be fine-tuned to accomplish this task? As another example, novel view synthesis for cartoons presents a largely unexplored opportunity. Imagine the time I could save as an illustrator if I could generate new views of a character from a few input drawings! Although little research has been conducted in this area thus far, it holds immense potential.

I have chosen CMU because its faculty share my interest in creating intelligent systems to empower visual creators. I am particularly drawn to Prof. Jun-Yan Zhu’s Generative Intelligence Lab, where our goals align – developing AI to help storytellers bring their visions to life. My background as an artist uniquely positions me to contribute to this lab, as the increasing prevalence of generative AI in the creative sphere has sparked concerns among artists about control and authorship. Joining the lab would allow me to address these concerns directly. As a photography enthusiast, I am also eager to work with Profs. Srinivasa Narasimhan and Ioannis Gkioulekas in computational imaging. Ultimately, my career ambition is to research intelligent tools that foster creativity, whether as a professor or by joining a department like Adobe Research. I am excited to embark on this journey towards that goal with a PhD from CMU.
