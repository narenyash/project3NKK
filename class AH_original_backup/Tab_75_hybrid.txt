A Transformed Odyssey: Exploring the Frontiers of Computer Vision with a Ph.D. at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill

My pursuit of a Ph.D. in Computer Science at the prestigious University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill is fueled by an insatiable curiosity to delve deeper into the realm of label-efficient deep learning methods, particularly as they apply to complex computer vision problems. While semi/self-supervised learning paradigms have shown remarkable promise in classification tasks, their applicability to other areas such as video understanding, action recognition, and multi-modal learning has been limited, especially in fields where acquiring ground truths is laborious and demands expertise. This forms the foundation of my research ambitions, rooted in my background in learning with less supervision.

Embarking on this journey, I am a Bachelor’s degree candidate majoring in Information Technology from Jadavpur University, an institution renowned for fostering a positive academic attitude and strong work ethics. My academic prowess shines in courses such as Data Structures, Algorithms, Object-Oriented Programming, and various online courses on machine learning, image processing, and computer vision. These form the bedrock of my understanding, providing me with the necessary foundation to conduct advanced research.

My research journey commenced during my sophomore year under the mentorship of Professors Ram Sarkar and Pawan Kumar Singh at Jadavpur University. We focused on deep learning and evolutionary optimization for medical imaging, developing a local search-embedded hybrid meta-heuristic scheme to optimize deep features extracted from lung X-ray images for pneumonia screening. Witnessing the transformative power of deep learning in real-world applications served as a significant motivation boost, propelling me further into the realm of research. Our work was published in the International Journal of Intelligent Systems, Wiley (IF: 8.993).

During the summer of 2021, I had the privilege of working as a research assistant at SketchX Lab, University of Surrey, under the guidance of Professor Yi-Zhe Song. Our project centered around fine-grained sketch-based image retrieval, a task that required each image to be paired with a corresponding hand-drawn finely detailed sketch for annotation. This experience opened my eyes to the extensive annotation requirements of supervised models, a factor I previously overlooked. This revelation sparked my interest in exploring research avenues involving learning with limited labels, including semi/self-supervision and weak supervision paradigms. I delved into classical self-supervised algorithms, investigating how they could enhance representation learning from unlabeled data, initially focusing on observing their impact on supervised models. In collaboration with Professor Singh, we devised a hybrid framework that leveraged an image inpainting module along with a supervised pipeline for a histopathological screening task. Our model outperformed existing SOTA methods by significant margins, and through suitable ablation studies, we empirically determined the performance boost due to the additional self-supervision signal. Our work was accepted for publication at the International Conference on Machine Intelligence and Signal Processing (MISP ‘22), also winning the prestigious “Best Paper Award” (Track: Deep Learning) in the event.

Starting winter 2021, I joined the CVPR Unit at the Indian Statistical Institute, Kolkata as a research intern, under the supervision of Professors Umapada Pal (ISI Kolkata) and Saumik Bhattacharya (IIT Kharagpur). My project goal was to develop self-supervised models for writer-independent offline signature verification. Leveraging my prior experience with sparse sketches, the fine-grainedness of handwritten signatures proved an intuitive starting point, directing our focus towards local region-wise feature learning. In my first work, we employed a patch-wise 2D attention mechanism for the reconstruction of the signature image from its constituent patches as a self-supervision pretext task, followed by fine-tuning of the pre-trained model using a dual metric learning objective. We published our work at the International Conference on Pattern Recognition (ICPR ‘22). In another work, we developed a non-contrastive self-supervised framework that learns decorrelated stroke representation of the signature images, via information maximization among patch-level features. Our work was accepted as an oral presentation in the International Conference on Image Processing (ICIP ‘22).

The experiences gleaned from these projects have been invaluable. They involved rigorous literature survey and testing of prior works, enabling us to formulate different approaches to tackle the problem statement. They also taught me the importance of meticulously planning experiments that are feasible within time constraints, especially during review addressal, since conferences have shorter and stricter deadlines compared to journals. However, most importantly, my first-hand experience with self-supervision served as a tremendous boost to my motivation to develop annotation-efficient frameworks, and I became more enthusiastic than ever to delve deeper into its theoretical and practical aspects. Continuing our collaboration, I am currently working on a similarity weighting mechanism by temperature variation in contrastive learning for class imbalanced data setups; and an unsupervised image decomposition framework to unify all types of image restoration (denoising, dehazing, etc.). We aim to submit these works to an upcoming top-venue conference.

In the summer of 2022, I was selected as a research intern under the Mitacs Globalink Programme. I worked with Professor Jose Dolz at Ecole de technologie superieure (ETS), Montreal, on unpaired multi-modal medical image segmentation. Our project aimed to circumvent the registration step for multi-modal learning from CT and MR volumes and improve segmentation performance in each modality by fusing cross-modal information from the unpaired medical scan volumes. This offered me an opportunity to extend my medical imaging experience to a more practical and challenging setup involving raw medical volumes instead of carefully curated 2D slices. Adapting to handling 3D medical scans, especially their pre-processing, sampling, and visualization, was a steep learning curve, but the enriching discussions with senior PhD students and doctoral journeys, as well as the taste of global research collaboration, made the experience delightful. Furthermore, it instilled in me the confidence to collaborate effectively with international students in diverse lab environments – an essential skill for success in a Ph.D. program.

Currently, Professor Jose and I are working on extending our project to a semi-supervised setting via an uncertainty-based approach. Our work would be one of the first to tackle unpaired multi-modal segmentation with limited annotations. We aim to publish this project soon at an upcoming venue.

The process of conducting research – from brainstorming ideas, designing proof of concept, experimental validation, and finally disseminating the findings through a compelling manuscript – has been incredibly rewarding, fueling my passion to conduct research at the highest level. Through my various research experiences, I have come to appreciate the significance of label-efficient methods such as semi-supervision and self-supervision paradigms in developing more sustainable ways of representation learning. Although my research background has spanned diverse topics, my objectives have primarily been to reduce supervision needs and instead develop weakly/semi/self-supervised frameworks, as evident from my research outputs and directions. In my Ph.D., I intend to work further on label-efficient representation learning and contribute to the field of computer vision.

At UNC, I am particularly interested in the works of Professors Gedas Bertasius, Soumyadip Sengupta, Tamara Berg, and Mohit Bansal. My research interests align with Professor Bertasius’ works on video understanding tasks under weakly/self-supervised paradigms. Specifically, I would like to explore along the lines of his paper “Long-Short Temporal Contrastive Learning of Video Transformers,” possibly extending into non-contrastive approaches as well. I am also highly motivated towards Professor Sengupta’s multimedia retrieval and VQA research, for which his guidance would be invaluable. I feel my research interests make me a great fit for each of their respective research groups. Additionally, I am eager to collaborate with other faculties on problems of mutual interest.

My decision to pursue a Ph.D. is driven by a passion for scientific pursuit and a career in academic research. The high degree of alignment between my research motivations and the eminent faculty at the University of North Carolina has made it an ideal choice to further my research aspirations, ultimately contributing to my long-term goal of being a researcher in academia. I am confident that I would thrive in the vibrant student community at UNC and look forward to engaging in fruitful discussions with my peers and contributing to collaborating research groups.
