Title: Formal Methods in Software Analysis and Systems Research: A Journey Towards Dependable Systems

My fascination lies in the realm of formal methods in software and systems analysis, encompassing static verification to run-time monitoring. I am drawn to projects that involve the redesign or analysis of a software system using a meticulously crafted and adaptable formal model. This process can lead to proving robust properties about a system, such as being correct by construction, which significantly aids in understanding its behavior. In such contexts, developing a versatile model often necessitates the creation of a custom-designed language or tool tailored to fully describe the model and be user-friendly for its implementation. Overcoming these challenges is intellectually stimulating; I find immense satisfaction in designing systems with robust guarantees about their behavior or languages that facilitate the specification of such systems more effortlessly.

Transitioning towards run-time verifications, I am equally captivated by the challenges posed by monitoring software systems at runtime. Run-time monitoring is particularly intriguing due to its limitations—we are confined to a single, finite run of the system, and not all properties can be monitored with this limited data. However, combining the original property with an astute assumption about the system structure can make more properties monitorable. In quantitative monitoring, statistical approaches such as modeling with random variables and utilizing confidence bounds can offer assistance. The run-time verifier must also be highly efficient, imposing minimal overhead on the existing configuration of systems. Engaging with problems in this domain is rewarding, given the variety of tools and concepts required to design an effective monitoring mechanism.

The allure of confidently understanding complex program behavior has long fascinated me. In my undergraduate years, I developed an eagerness to learn about formal reasoning related to programs and systems. I am particularly intrigued by seeing the tangible outcomes of a research project being applied in the real world—these advancements can contribute significantly to building safe and dependable real-world systems. The implementation phase of research work is equally captivating, as it introduces new problems that demand a deep understanding of hardware constraints and software design practices. Overcoming these challenges leads to solutions that enhance the tool's efficiency and maintainability.

My first foray into research was collaborating with Dr. Hossein Hojjat and his graduate student Amir Hossein Seyhani on applying causality, primarily introduced by Halpern and Pearl, to concurrent systems modeled as transition systems or event structures. Employing a robust framework of causality in real-world systems can aid in analyzing the root causes of failures in complex settings and building systems that are more secure and less error-prone. During this project, I implemented event structure composition and translation to a causal model in a prototype tool for defining and verifying causes in event structures. I also translated classic causality problems, as well as simple computer network configurations, into our desired models. Currently, I am focusing on optimizing the cause-verifier module, which checks whether a given cause, accompanied by a witness, can satisfy the Halpern-Pearl causality criteria. For this problem, I am leveraging ideas from previous publications in this area (such as search space reduction and special-case solutions).

I continued my exploration of formal methods during my summer internship at the Institute of Science and Technology Austria. Under Prof. Thomas Henzinger’s guidance, I collaborated with two group members to design run-time monitors for verifying fairness properties on black-box systems. In this setting, we assumed the underlying system was a Markov chain, and our monitors provided a confidence level for the satisfaction of a fairness property, given an error threshold. We employed both frequentist and Bayesian statistical approaches in our designs. For comparison, we focused on well-known problems of fairness—specifically, the bank lending and college admission problems, with equal opportunities and social burden as our fairness metrics, respectively. During this internship, I developed a Rust codebase to define and examine our monitors. Additionally, I mapped the aforementioned classic problems to Markov chains, contributed to developing theories, discovered that Bayesian monitoring is computationally inexpensive when our fairness expression is in polynomial form, and demonstrated that monitoring fixed-size regular expressions is achievable with our existing monitors. Our results for this project have been submitted to TACAS 2023.

The scholarly prowess of ISTA faculty members, combined with a community of motivated students, offers an ideal environment for me to pursue my research journey as a Ph.D. student. My interests align well with Prof. Thomas Henzinger’s areas of research, particularly his work on monitoring techniques and run-time verification, which has piqued my interest. Furthermore, the work of Prof. Krishnendu Chatterjee on static analysis of programs and automata theory—such as the paper Proving Non-termination by Program Reversal—has caught my attention, and I aspire to pursue a Ph.D. under his supervision for my future endeavors.
