My research journey revolves around privacy-preserving machine learning, with a focus on federated learning (FL) and differential privacy (DP), and a broader interest in developing and understanding ML methods that can be deployed ethically and effectively in the real world. This passion has led me to two primary avenues I aim to explore at the University of California, Berkeley:

1. Advancing perspectives on ML privacy: What types of privacy (differential) are suitable for different scenarios, and when can they be relaxed? In the era where training examples may not align perfectly with sensitive information we wish to protect, what constitutes "private" machine learning?

2. Practical privacy solutions: Can we create methods that significantly improve the privacy-utility tradeoff, making privacy the default for ML? How do these methods interact with other desirable properties of ML, such as efficiency and generalization?

Evolving Notions of ML Privacy

My initial foray into this domain came through my work on distributed differential privacy at Google, published at ICML'21 [4] and NeurIPS'21 [5] and deployed at scale for applications like Gboard. Distributed DP addresses the question: can we achieve central DP-like guarantees under a local DP setup, where n clients each noise their vector locally to minimize trust in the central aggregator? Prior research had bridged the O(√n) local/central utility gap assuming trusted shufflers or execution environments; however, it remained unclear whether we could do so using cryptography, specifically secure aggregation (SecAgg). Working with my colleagues, I developed the first working system for distributed DP with SecAgg using distributed discrete Gaussians (DDG)—client vectors are appropriately clipped, scaled, rotated/reflected, rounded, locally noised, and securely summed—and demonstrated that it matches the performance of the central, continuous Gaussian even under low bitwidths [4]. However, DDG noise is difficult to sample and not closed under summation, and fine-tuning the discretization factor γ, which impacts privacy, communication, and utility, can be challenging. To address these issues, we also analyzed Skellam noise as a practical alternative [5]: it is closed under summation, easy to sample, and has an RDP overhead over Gaussians shrinking quickly at 1/poly(variance)—a small compromise from DDG’s 1/exp(variance) in practice—and it thus offers matching performance in production. Moreover, I proposed a simple method leveraging private quantiles of client vector `2-norms to automatically tune γ, significantly simplifying the deployment of our distributed DP algorithms. Engaging with cryptography, compression, empirical heuristics, and engineering challenges has shown me that there is significant potential for better privacy tools if we view privacy as a multi-faceted problem rather than a standalone statistical discipline.

In another project at CMU, I investigated the application of DP in cross-silo federated learning. Previous work on differentially private FL, including my distributed DP efforts, has primarily focused on client-level DP. However, in cross-silo FL there are fewer clients with large local datasets and varying privacy targets, and importantly, the entities deserving protection (e.g., institutions) may not align with those we wish to protect (e.g., people). In my NeurIPS'22 [6] paper, I found that the need for granular protection and model personalization in cross-silo FL points towards a relaxation—silo-specific sample-level privacy—where each client caters to the privacy of its own samples as it participates in FL protocols. I delved into many intriguing implications, such as the removal of trust on the server, the breakdown of local fine-tuning for effective personalization, and the emergence of a privacy-heterogeneity cost tradeoff and the role of personalization in navigating it. I demonstrated that mean-regularized multi-task learning forms a strong baseline under these conditions but also explored its practical complications in terms of hyperparameter tuning. This project underscored my understanding that privacy is interwoven with various aspects of learning, and effective privacy algorithms should not be developed in isolation.

Practical Privacy Solutions

A major obstacle to the widespread adoption of differential privacy in ML is its practicality, particularly regarding the significant privacy-utility tradeoff. Currently, I'm leading a project at CMU exploring the mitigation of per-example clipping bias of DP training through various generalization methods. I noticed that the recent empirical success of [10] suggests an outsized role of data augmentation in boosting DP-SGD performance that correlates to smaller augmentation-averaged gradient norms. Drawing connections to the body of work on sharpness-aware optimization, which (debatably) links generalization to flatter minima, I'm investigating whether various generalization methods would remain effective across private and non-private optimization; one intriguing observation is that while both augmentation and gradient norm penalty reduce the magnitude part of clipping bias, only the latter seems to reduce the direction part, which manifests as smaller utility drops from clipping. Understanding and mitigating the clipping bias would be a crucial step towards making DP practical, and I aim to delve deeper into this area with both empirical and theoretical analysis.

I'm also interested in private adaptive optimization and am crucially involved in the development of DP2 [7], a family of adaptive optimizers leveraging delayed preconditioners to drastically improve privacy-utility tradeoffs. DP2 is based on the simple idea that in many tasks, the underlying loss geometry doesn't change much over training, and we can learn less noisy but stale preconditioners using averages of past private gradients. DP2 is highly practical and efficient as it does not require public data or 2nd-order computation; I performed the empirical analyses in our ICLR'23 submission and OPT'22 talk [7] and found it promising to see DP2 excelling on real-world tasks. Through this work, I also discovered a latent interest in efficient private ML systems—I wrote our code from scratch with auto-vectorization and JIT compilation and significantly accelerated our research with over 10× speedup over our initial prototype. I'm curious to explore other higher/lower-level efficiency methods too.

At Berkeley, I'm eager to continue exploring these directions and beyond in the broader area of trustworthy ML. For example, I'm interested in relaxations of (differential) privacy for ML and FL, such as fine-grained analyses and label-only protection. Moreover, what if a person's data are distributed across multiple clients in federated learning? How can we protect their privacy while maintaining the benefits of this collaborative approach? I'm also excited to collaborate on open-source efforts on private ML systems and benchmarking to facilitate future research in these areas.
