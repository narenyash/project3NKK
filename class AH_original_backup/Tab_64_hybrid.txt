Nurtured by the burgeoning mobile internet during my middle school years, I found myself deeply captivated by the design innovations of electronic products such as the iPhone, igniting a passion within me to create next-generation spatial user interfaces and intelligent tools. This ardent desire led me to pursue a multidisciplinary education at ShanghaiTech University, where I honed my skills in computer science, design, and humanities. My thirst for knowledge was further quenched through research at UIUC and Harvard. I am firmly convinced that designing human-centered interfaces is crucial for effective technology application, and exploring design challenges through various research methods is an enthralling pursuit. Consequently, I am resolute in my decision to pursue a Ph.D. in HCC at Georgia Tech.

The emergence of Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) and spatial computing platforms presents an expansive design space for Human-Computer Interaction, with three specific challenges captivating my attention. First, spatial computing platforms transcend traditional screen-based interfaces, necessitating the development of tailored information input and display methods to maximize the high interaction freedom in 3D space. Second, as Large Language Models (LLMs) exhibit AGI capabilities, the line between human and AI abilities is increasingly indistinct, requiring a more precise division of roles to support human values such as creativity. Lastly, rapid technology advancement imposes higher demands on people's literacy and adaptability to new things, necessitating the design of user interfaces for technologically underprivileged groups—transforming technology into new opportunities for personal growth rather than barriers. Over the past few years, I have conducted research on several projects spanning these themes, primarily focused on human-centered user interface design. For my Ph.D. studies, I aim to take a broader perspective while still addressing the design challenges around the above three themes.

Delving into intuitive interaction design for spatial computing, I recognized that human gaze reveals spatial attention during interaction. Building upon this insight, I led a research project on VR gaze interaction, exploring the use of eyes as an input source for spatial computing headsets. Existing gaze interaction methods primarily employ gaze direction for target pointing but struggle with the Midas Touch problem for target selection—distinguishing between 'looking' and 'choosing' intents. To address this challenge, we discovered that visual depth calculated from binocular vision is a promising dimension. By enabling users to focus on objects in 3D space, we can selectively observe different objects with intention—much like looking through a window at a distant landscape or focusing closely to observe dust on the windowpane. Inspired by this, we developed FocusFlow, a hands-free gaze selection method using visual depth shift for VR headsets, providing an intuitive and efficient interaction process that has demonstrated application value in both general and professional scenarios. In my pursuit to support human creativity in future human-AI interaction, I collaborated with researchers at the Harvard Visual Computing Group during the summer of 2023. Our goal was to leverage the LLM’s fuzzy matching capability to translate UI designers' design principles into environment-customized visualization parameters, thereby enhancing UI adaptability to context. This research highlighted a dual ambiguity in describing environmental conditions and human needs, posing challenges in communication between LLMs and designers. While vague design principles ease the burden on designers, they may lead to unstable model decisions. In contrast, specific descriptions heavily rely on preset conditions provided by the designers, diminishing the LLMs' inferential value. To address this challenge, we proposed a JSON-structured declarative natural language grammar for conveying design constraints to LLMs, and explored ways to define and balance the ambiguity in human-AI natural language communication. This research experience revealed the need for a more human-centered mindset when it comes to AI applications. [project page]

Approaching HCI artifact values from the creativity perspective, I engaged in qualitative research on the roles of student documentation in Physical Computing education under Professor Sarah Sterman's newly formed lab. We first analyzed course syllabi across various majors and educational levels using open coding method, developing an understanding of the motivations, tools, content, and learning goals for student-created documentation. Further, we conducted semi-structured interviews with course instructors from various majors to delve into their values on documentation and challenges faced in education practice. This hands-on research experience not only opened me up to qualitative research methodologies but also enhanced my ability to derive insights from textual data and learn directly from our users.

Reflecting upon my upbringing in a small city in China with limited resources, I have witnessed firsthand the transformative power of HCI artifacts symbolized by digital devices and mobile internet in the 2010s. While appreciating the opportunities technology has provided, I also recognize its challenges. During an educational outreach experience in Southwest China, I realized the complex impact of technology—despite widespread Internet access enabled by government initiatives, the information divide persists in underdeveloped areas. Children are often dangerously exposed to low-quality sensory-charged online content, and local teachers struggle to integrate multimedia technology effectively in education. These experiences have highlighted the limitations of current HCI in marginalized communities, fueling my motivation to develop more inclusive technological solutions.

During my doctoral studies, I aim to integrate the above three topics to explore user interface and intelligent tool design in spatial computing scenarios. Specifically, I hope to research telepresence technology in mixed reality, enabling users physically in different spaces or times to communicate and collaborate as if they were in the same place. This involves fundamental research questions about how to reconstruct the spatial and temporal context of immersive communication, and interact with objects in virtual environments. Furthermore, spatial computing transforms human-AI collaboration space from 2D screens to 3D environments. Investigating the creation of more context-aware AI systems, and enabling more end-users to intuitively create customized and flexible virtual tools in mixed reality excites me greatly. Georgia Tech is an ideal research environment for me, where its extensive HCI community can offer a solid foundation for my academic journey. In particular, the research of Professor Yalong Yang in display and interaction techniques between 2D and 3D user interfaces, along with Professor Thad Starner’s work in mixed reality interactive systems and multi-sensory interfaces greatly inspire my spatial computing research. Additionally, the research conducted by Professors HyunJoo Oh and Cindy Xiong in the fields of human-centered design, creativity, and cognitive processes also offers wonderful perspectives for my artifact research. I hope to design intelligent user interfaces to address challenging problems in spatial computing within the School of Interactive Computing’s interdisciplinary research context, combining different technologies and research methods, and thus contributing new possibilities to the field of Human-Computer Interaction.
