Brown University Computer Science PhD Program: A Symbiosis of Computation and Law

In an era where legal structures are struggling to keep pace with increasingly sophisticated technological systems, computational tools are being employed in the name of upholding legal principles. Witnessing this feedback loop during my undergraduate studies in computer science and mathematics, I was compelled to hone research skills at MIT's Technology and Policy program, situated at the nexus of computation and law. As a PhD candidate in Computer Science at Brown University, I aspire to explore how computation and law can collaborate to steer the design and development of technical systems towards equitable and intended outcomes. I would relish opportunities to:

1) Fuse domain expertise to co-design models that support legal processes and frameworks;
2) Identify failures in computational systems and their development cycles; and
3) Propose and advocate for system standards and technology regulation.

Research Motivation

During my engineering internship at Google in 2020, I worked on open-source differential privacy (DP) libraries under the guidance of Chris Jones, Raine Serrano, and Miraç Basaran. Differential privacy strategically adds noise to data to guarantee privacy while retaining information. However, for health survey data with rare and sensitive responses linked to individual participants, adding noise is insufficient. Consider a scenario where a participant's response appears in a histogram partition, regardless of the noise applied to that partition's contribution—this breaches DP guarantees. To prevent such leakages, Google implemented δ-thresholding across its codebase. This approach, however, led to needless elimination of partitions with small contributions that were predetermined and public. I devised a solution by modifying the aggregation functions in the codebase, enabling developers to specify and maintain public, predetermined partitions beforehand. This modification involved reconfiguring the distribution of noise across partitions and ensuring that any function changes would not disrupt existing function calls. As a result, developers could achieve stronger privacy guarantees of (ε, 0)-DP.

Curiosity piqued, I delved into the legal frameworks that shaped this work. In 2020, the same year I joined Google's anonymization team, the EU GDPR enacted carve-outs for anonymized data. Interestingly, anonymization techniques such as differential privacy are not typically employed to enhance privacy protections for already-collected data but rather serve as tools to access new forms of data. This dichotomy between technical and policy values left me disheartened, and I began contemplating how legal principles could be more meaningfully integrated into technological systems.

Models for Legal and Policy Frameworks

I bridged the gap between legal values and technical systems through an undergraduate research project that modeled ethical dilemmas in strategic games. In this project, I framed and justified the system's modeling of autonomous vehicle dilemmas, designed a sacrifice function, and demonstrated how the logical system could be fine-tuned to emulate various types of ethical and policy frameworks. These contributions culminated in a co-authored AAAI 2021 publication titled "Ethical Dilemmas in Strategic Games." Models can capture significant aspects of a phenomenon but not every aspect. Our model forced a dilemma between actions, potentially omitting others. We simplified legal and ethical frameworks by assigning values to each action and ordering them—a necessary step for computability, though heuristics like ordering can sometimes be surprisingly effective. I was driven to explore the aspects of AI and technology that were relevant and pertinent to law.

Uncovering Legal Tensions in the Development of Computational Systems

To gain insights into relevant technical properties in legal assessments, I conducted research on technology case law alongside Senior Research Scientist Alice Xiang at Sony AI. My investigation revealed that nearly all United States litigation involving facial processing technologies (FPT) stemmed from a specific Illinois law—the 2008 Biometric Information Privacy Act (BIPA). This led me to scrutinize the legal properties of BIPA and the technical properties of FPT that combined to spark an explosion of litigation. In certain cases, the application of BIPA to FPT underscored the limitations of technical methods in achieving legal privacy values. However, the policy did not always delve into the heart of technical approaches either. For instance, agnostic of use case, BIPA protects the collection of scans of face geometry, while excluding facial photographs from privacy protections. This could misguidedly discourage privacy-preserving face blurring in image datasets where facial landmarks are collected to detect and blur faces. These tensions are discussed in my first-author paper, "Regulating Facial Processing Technologies: Tensions Between Legal and Technical Considerations," published in the proceedings of ACM FAccT 2022. After its publication, the American Civil Liberties Union successfully wielded this policy to sue Clearview AI, while Meta faced BIPA lawsuits regarding its augmented reality filters. These lawsuits address several points I raise in my paper, and I was heartened to see BIPA enforce surveillance and discriminatory FPT deployment. Simultaneously, after uncovering the policy's technical misunderstandings through this research project, I became motivated to consider how written regulation could be improved through a more comprehensive incorporation of technological guidance.

Motivating and Proposing Technology Regulation and System Standards

In my first year at MIT CSAIL, I embarked on research concerning regulatory design for AI systems under the tutelage of Professor Dylan Hadfield-Menell. The AI research community acknowledges the significance of transparency regarding potential harms caused by AI systems. However, creating legal and organizational incentives to prioritize investigating these harms remains an open and pressing issue. I discovered the potential of regulatory penalty defaults to address this challenge as I synthesized several bodies of literature: AI-related policies in the United States, the legal literature on regulatory design, and domain-specific properties of recommender systems and autonomous vehicles. My primary contributions were in the proposal of regulatory penalty default approaches for AI systems. I am the lead author on the corresponding AAAI/ACM AIES 2022 publication "A Penalty Default Approach to Preemptive Harm Disclosure and Mitigation for AI Systems." The domain-specific, top-down approach I took to this policy problem led me to explore bottom-up approaches such as developing technical standards. I am currently pursuing this work through a part-time research internship at the Center for Trustworthy and Responsible AI within the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), where I am developing and comparing measurable standards for the AI Risk Management Framework.
