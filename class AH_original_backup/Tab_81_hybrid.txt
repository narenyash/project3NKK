**Background:**

My journey in Computer Science research began with an opportunity to collaborate with Prof. Haryadi Gunawi from the University of Chicago. Our mission: to investigate the latency predictability of unpredictable flash storage, a challenge that had eluded researchers due to vendors' unwillingness to modify device interfaces and the high cost associated with "black-box" approaches involving extra I/Os. Our group pioneered a novel technique—letting the device act as a "black box," learning its patterns without altering file systems or applications, thereby minimizing overhead and enhancing accuracy using a lightweight neural network, which I designed and implemented.

**LinnOS Project:**

In the LinnOS project, my role was pivotal in creating a neural network model to predict incoming I/O speeds for SSD applications. This task proved daunting due to the need for high accuracy without compromising on inference speed or incurring excessive costs. Through meticulous design iterations, I constructed a 3-layer light neural network and focused on crucial input features, ultimately uncovering that features commonly considered significant, such as block offsets, read/write flags, or the long history of writes, played minimal roles. Instead, I discovered that the most critical input features were the latencies of recently completed I/Os and the number of pending I/Os when those I/Os and the current, to-be-inferred I/O arrived. This model surpassed state-of-the-art techniques employed by leading tech companies with minimal inference costs, reducing average I/O latencies by 9.6–79.6% while maintaining 87–97% accuracy and a 4–6 microsecond inference overhead for each I/O. Our work was published on OSDI 2020, marking a significant stride forward in the field of storage systems research.

**Transition:**

Propelled by this success, I decided to venture into AI-related research, joining Prof. Derry Wijaya from Boston University for my next project—a multimodal analysis of news framing. Our goal was to develop a web-based system, OpenFraming, for analyzing and classifying news frames in text documents, making it accessible to researchers from diverse disciplines with or without computational backgrounds. I derived immense satisfaction during the development of this demonstration, envisioning future projects involving online demos for select research outcomes. This demo was published on EMNLP 2021.

**Multimodal News Framing Analysis:**

In collaboration with Prof. Derry Wijaya, I proceeded to utilize BERT and ResNet to predict news frames from a Gun Violence dataset. Our work represented the first foray into computational multimodal framing analysis. Initially, the ResNet-50 model struggled, yielding only 42.8% accuracy when trained and tested on images from our dataset. To enhance its performance, I extracted information from the images using Google Visual API and combined these features with fully connected layers derived from ResNet. The resulting model outperformed the original ResNet model by achieving an 87% accuracy on articles containing relevant images, underscoring the importance of background knowledge in building smarter AI agents. This work was also accepted at EMNLP 2021.

**Future Plan:**

Modern computing devices are plagued by data movement bottlenecks, which consume a significant portion of total energy consumption and negatively impact workloads such as machine learning, computational biology, and more due to low cache utilization and large datasets. I envision adopting a new paradigm of near-memory processing as the key to improving the performance and efficiency of future computer systems. While my focus lies in exploring near-memory processing, memory disaggregation, and MLSys interaction, I remain open to other exciting research problems in Computer Science.
