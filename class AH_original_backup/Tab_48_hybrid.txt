Title: Pioneering AI Research: My Journey, Vision, and Future Aspirations

In my academic pursuits, I am passionately driven to delve into fundamental research, specifically focusing on machine learning and natural language processing (NLP). This fascination is rooted in understanding the intricate process of learning and leveraging this knowledge to create robust and efficient models, particularly in curiosity-driven research. Instead of the conventional approach of scaling up, I am captivated by research work that emphasizes scaling down, as it presents a challenging technical landscape while pushing the boundaries of our understanding (Wang & LeCun, 2018).

My transformative journey as a Research Scholar at Cohere For AI spanned eight months and culminated in two first-author papers accepted to EMNLP 2023 [Pozzobon et al., 2023a, b]. I also contributed to a colleague's paper that was featured in the ATTRIB Workshop at NeurIPS 2023. My current research interests intersect data-centric modeling, interpretability, and safety.

Data-centric modeling is about developing robust, efficient, and generalizable models. A question that piqued my interest during this period was the role of a datapoint in training dynamics. Could models trained with dissimilar subsets of data achieve comparable downstream performances? These intriguing findings emerged from our work on Data Pruning [Marion et al., 2023], where we demonstrated that, using only 30% of the pretraining dataset, we could match or even surpass the performance of a model trained with 100%. Remarkably, this was consistent across various subsets of data. However, this similarity held true only when the number of training steps remained constant, suggesting that perhaps in certain data domains, model performance may be more closely related to the number of gradient update steps than the number of unique instances (LeCun et al., 2015). This discovery raises other intriguing questions: can we quantify a dataset's bottleneck capacity a priori? Can we learn from which data to learn from? How can we synthesize data reliably so that smaller models achieve comparable performances as larger ones? Furthermore, what is the optimal balance between learning generalizable features with few datapoints and abiding by scaling laws (Suttorp et al., 2018)?

My curiosity in generalization was sparked during my master's program. I delved into the generalization of reinforcement learning algorithms in Contextual Markov Decision Processes (CMDPs), often referred to as zero-shot policy transfer (Sutton & Barto, 1998). Although I didn't have the opportunity to explore these topics further at the time, the questions surrounding out-of-distribution generalization and uncertainty estimation remain compelling. For language modeling, clear definitions, evaluations, and a taxonomy of types of generalization are only recently emerging (Brown et al., 2020). However, it's still unclear what generalization means as datasets continue to grow exponentially. I also believe that uncertainty estimation is currently under-explored in NLP. Recent advancements in self-improvement and self-correction suggest that this field will gain more attention in the coming years (Raffel et al., 2020). Coupled with effective synthetic data, these developments could lead to high-performing models requiring fewer annotated datapoints while making a significant impact on the world.

Interpretability and safety are two aspects of AI research that continue to captivate me, particularly in the context of language modeling. A question I find intriguing is whether the "in-context learning" property of large language models, which enables zero or few-shot transfer, can be replicated in smaller models (Rojas et al., 2021). This relates to my interest in efficiency but also delves into model understanding. I believe that research in this area, aimed at investigating unexpected model behavior, will be incredibly exciting.

During my time as a Research Scholar, I discovered unexpected behaviors from black-box APIs backed by machine learning models [Pozzobon et al., 2023a]. The unexpected finding was the non-reproducibility of research caused by changes that were not well-informed to users. This is an influential discovery as ML algorithms are increasingly integrated into general applications, and unexpected behavior can lead to system failures (Gunning et al., 2017). I encountered this issue while working on toxicity mitigation with retrieval-augmented models [Pozzobon et al., 2023b], both of which were accepted to EMNLP 2023. Currently, I am expanding this work to multilingual toxicity mitigation. Harm and bias in a multilingual setting are vastly under-explored, with most research focusing only on high-resource languages and classification tasks (Hovy et al., 2006). My aim is for this work to serve as a starting point for measuring harm before deploying multilingual models.

As an underprivileged student, both LGBTQ+ and Latina, I am acutely aware of the value that mentors can provide in shaping one's life. I have been fortunate enough to encounter mine, and I aspire to help unlock paths for other students by collaborating with juniors and through teaching assistant positions. In the spirit of Richard Hamming, who noted that the most successful researchers he knew kept their office doors open (Hamming, 1986), I envision these collaborations and mentorships as a means to keep my "doors" open, not solely for personal success but to foster an active, engaged, and caring research community.

At UW, I look forward to working with professors Luke Zettlemoyer, Ludwig Schmidt, and Noah Smith. With Prof. Luke, I aim to learn how to be an effective empiricist while exploring the intersection of NLP and decision-making under uncertainty. I am particularly drawn to his recent work on deciphering large language model behavior (Zettlemoyer et al., 2021). With Prof. Ludwig, I aspire to unravel the mysteries surrounding data in various learning stages, using that knowledge to build reliable and generalizable models (Schmidt & Schraudolph, 2004). Finally, with Prof. Noah, I look forward to embarking on socially-aware research, seeking to understand how algorithms truly work and impact society (Smith et al., 2017). More broadly, I am eager to be part of the collaborative and friendly community that UW is renowned for.
