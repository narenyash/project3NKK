**Research Symphony**

**TU Wien Internship**
My debut on the research stage unfolded during a 7-month internship at the Automated Program Reasoning group at TU Wien, under the guidance of Prof. Laura Kovács and Prof. Ezio Bartocci. My contributions to two research projects culminated in valuable results and publications.

The first project was an exploration into polynomial invariant generation, a topic that is well understood for solvable loops but remains shrouded in mystery outside this restricted computational model. Traditionally, the approach has been limited to linear combinations over loop variables. However, I elevated the state-of-the-art by introducing an innovative method to automatically synthesize invariants for unsolvable loops, not just relying on linear combinations but also considering polynomial relations among them. This approach applies to probabilistic loops as well. Our collaboration led to a conference publication at the 29th Static Analysis Symposium (SAS'22), winning the Radhia Cousot Young Researcher Best Paper award. My role extended beyond code implementation, encompassing the design of a polynomial-time graph algorithm to characterize the program variables responsible for unsolvability and the introduction of a novel technique for synthesizing invariants in unsolvable loops.

During the publication process, I recognized the vital importance of articulating key concepts and ideas to a broader audience of computer scientists.

In the second project, I embarked on an expedition into algebraic reasoning for loop synthesis. This research charted a sound and complete SMT-based approach for synthesizing loops that satisfy a set of polynomial loop invariants, seamlessly combining symbolic computation techniques over algebraic recurrence equations with polynomial constraint solving. In this project, I took the lead, managing the construction of experiments and discovering a potential application in compiler optimization – the synthesis of equivalent single-path programs with less costly operations. The paper was published in the ACM journal Formal Aspects of Computing. This project marked my successful demonstration of independent research capabilities, requiring minimal oversight.

**ETH Zürich Internship**
Following my internship at TU Wien, I embarked on another research journey at ETH Zurich to delve deeper into the field of formal methods. My mission at ETH was to verify initialization code in Golang, ensuring that packages or libraries were in a desired state at the beginning of program execution. In Golang, verifying initialization code regardless of the order of files passed to the compiler presents a formidable concurrency challenge. I devised a non-trivial solution (not considering all possible file orderings) by employing the Viper intermediate verification language and separation logic. The foundation of my approach was finding a preserving invariant for each initialization block. Additionally, I worked on translating our problem to a programming model introduced by Peter W. O'Hearn by statically analyzing the source code of the Go program. However, designing a scalable approach applicable to real-world programs with thousands of lines of code and hundreds of files is a daunting task due to nested package dependencies and the high number of possible interleavings, an endeavor yet to be undertaken as far as I am aware. I aspire to develop a robust generalized framework for addressing concurrency problems in distributed systems in a scalable manner.

**EPFL Internship**
Having savored the theoretical side of formal methods, I decided to immerse myself at the Dependable Systems Lab (DSLAB) at EPFL under the guidance of Prof. George Candea to witness the practical application of these methods in real-world computer systems. Ensuring the reliability and correctness of computer systems becomes a challenging feat when confronted with real-world applications. Automated verification serves as a potent tool for this purpose. At DSLAB, they had already made significant strides in verifying network functions (NFs) using symbolic execution. However, a formidable obstacle with symbolic execution is the path explosion problem, particularly when dealing with larger programs, especially those with loops being a primary source. I mitigated this by abstracting loops in 9 widely used string functions in the standard C library using quantifiers in first-order logic. This was achieved by integrating Z3's support for quantifiers into KLEE's symbolic execution engine source code. The result of my work not only improved the scalability of verifying NFs but also mitigated the path explosion problem when conducting symbolic execution on almost any C code utilizing string operations from the standard C library. Although this was just a prototype, the implications of this work were profound, and for the first time, I felt the exhilaration of the applicability of my work to software with high impact.
