Title: Unraveling the Intricacies of Natural Language Processing: A Causal Perspective

The advent of large-scale, unsupervised pretrained language models has brought about significant strides in natural language processing (NLP), narrowing the gap between human and machine performance on numerous tasks. Yet, these advancements have also raised questions concerning the accurate measurement, evaluation, and explanation of progress within the field. Models have been found to be brittle (Jia & Liang, 2017), high-quality datasets are costly to construct (Marcus et al., 1993), and understanding the behavior of pretrained language models remains challenging. Moreover, as NLP technologies become more accessible, addressing practical concerns becomes imperative. Modern NLP is expensive (Strubell et al., 2019) and the current modeling choices are sub-optimal. I am convinced that evolution in evaluation and explainability techniques goes hand in hand with advancements in modeling techniques. Looking ahead, a causal perspective could serve as a unifying force propelling progress on both fronts.

During my Ph.D., I aspire to explore new avenues in NLP, particularly in relation to evaluation, explainability, and modeling. My early work has provided me with a strong foundation in these areas, and I aim to delve deeper, focusing on projects that take a causal angle. Enhanced modeling leads to improved evaluation and explainability; the latest large-scale pretrained language models have dramatically altered the NLP landscape (Devlin et al., 2019; Brown et al., 2020). As these models are deployed into more practical scenarios, new demands for evaluation and explainability techniques emerge. One critical challenge is understanding the robustness of these models across a wide range of conditions. However, creating datasets to evaluate models poses a substantial obstacle (Gardner et al., 2020).

My experience with downstream tasks has revealed that generating contrast sets can aid in overcoming this barrier. By employing linguistically-informed transformations (LIT), we automatically generate contrast sets that help evaluate the robustness of models, as demonstrated at EMNLP BlackboxNLP Workshop (Liu et al., 2020). This method has proven to be an effective approach in probing across time, uncovering what models know and when (Liu et al., 2021).

My journey in NLP began with the study of attention mechanisms within transformers. Specifically, I investigated how BERT's attention functions and discovered that it focuses primarily on key phrases rather than individual words (Clark et al., 2019). This revelation has significant implications for understanding and improving reading comprehension systems.

A major turning point in my career occurred when I co-authored the paper "Probing Across Time: What Does Roberta Know and When?" at EMNLP Finding (Liu et al., 2021). This work provided insights into how different layers of the transformer network process information over time.

As my research progresses, I am increasingly drawn to the idea of causal abstractions of neural networks (Geiger et al., 2021) and their potential for explaining the behavior of complex models. In collaboration with other researchers, I aim to leverage these insights to develop more robust and interpretable NLP models.

In conclusion, I am excited about the future of NLP research, particularly from a causal perspective. The challenges faced in understanding and improving evaluation, explainability, and modeling techniques are numerous, but I believe that with perseverance and collaboration, we can continue to make significant strides in this field.
