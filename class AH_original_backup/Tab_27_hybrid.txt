A TALE OF HORRORS AND HEART: THE COLLEGE ESSAY REVISITED
"His head brims with a tale, teetering on the edge of fearful disclosure. It is unnerving, this insatiable urge to spill forth. He senses if his feverish quill cannot scribe the narrative swiftly enough, it may burst from his eyes in its desperate bid for freedom and tangible form." Bill Denbrough's words echoed persistently within my mind like an indelible imprint. Stephen King has a penchant for incorporating author characters into his tales—his alter egos. Yet, in my imagination, Bill was more than just a character; he was me, as much as he was King. Though Bill bore a distinct persona and unique motivations, we shared an undying bond: storytelling.

In the 6th grade, I immersed myself entirely in Stephen King's It over the course of three months. That blood-stained brick of a novel became my constant companion—in the car, during math class (apologies to Mrs. Bailey), even on the commode. The enchantment I found within that story was unparalleled, a realm I had never before encountered. My previous notion of horror stemmed from films; it was garish, sensational, and gratuitous. However, horror in literature—especially King's—exhibited a striking semblance of emotional authenticity.

Following my connection to the Loser's Club, Bill Denbrough's friendship circle, I embarked on a mission to assemble my own brotherhood. I marveled at the group's camaraderie blossoming amid adversity. To foster this bond authentically, I endeavored to persuade my friends to delve into King’s masterpiece through my personal narration. During lunch, I'd regale them with accounts of the chapters I had just devoured. After school, I'd send textual soliloquies in group chats and elucidate the significance of each scene to anyone who would listen. On weekends, I'd reenact my favorite moments for friends, explaining the symbolism behind each action.

Lo and behold, I proved to be an engaging storyteller. My impassioned discourse inspired a few friends to purchase the book, with two even reading it cover-to-cover. Others, having heard my renditions, read summaries or watched YouTube analyses (opted out of reading the book out of apprehension). I felt a sense of accomplishment. Witnessing the spread of my "It" ardor helped me recognize that I harbored a desire to share stories.

Though middle school may now be but a memory, horror remains a cherished pastime. Writing in this genre has become one of my favorite hobbies. One of my short stories, Goodbye Horses, chronicles a schizophrenic, body-dysmorphic girl who removes her neighbor's face and wears it like a mask, seeking validation for her self-worth. Another piece delves into the murderous impulses of a young Victorian boy's best friend's mother, told from his perspective. One of my favorites is about a discontented college student who acquires an enigmatic garment from an ancient Russian woman and eventually drives herself mad abusing its powers.

I write with the intention of conveying a message. Goodbye Horses serves as a critique of the detrimental beauty standards imposed upon women. My Victorian murder story explores the effects of early childhood trauma and neglect. Horror can be particularly enlightening when handled deftly. The genre achieves a shocking empathy that I believe most effectively conveys the gravity of an issue.

Another facet of horror writing that captivates me is its humor. Even the goriest details can elicit laughter. The first time my friend Jason encountered Goodbye Horses, he chuckled in astonishment at the line, "Her body was spewing blood like a burst water balloon." Witnessing Jason's awestruck laughter evoked a feeling of delight within me—the realization that horror can be both entertaining and thought-provoking. And while it's fun to repulse people, these humorous details also serve to underscore the seriousness of a horror story’s message. When individuals read my work, I hope they uncover new insights into issues that resonate deeply with me.

After all, tales have the power to transform lives. It reshaped mine.
