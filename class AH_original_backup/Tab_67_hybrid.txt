Title: A Journey through NeuroAI: Unraveling the Mysteries of Brain Learning

Yash Mehta

Sparked by my first taste of research, towards the tail end of my undergraduate studies, I discovered a passion that ignited within me. My final-year thesis at NTU Singapore, focused on deep learning-based personality prediction [6-9], ignited an ardor for the research process that I had longed for. The logic of code was intriguing, but the adaptability and broad applicability of this tool captured my heart. It became clear that research was my true calling, and I left behind the comfortable confines of my position as a software developer at Amazon to embark on an adventure in research. My aspirations lie in becoming a group leader, collaborating with like-minded researchers to leverage advancements in deep learning and unveil fundamental principles that govern learning in the brain.

Drawn towards the fundamental mechanisms of learning within neural networks, I reached out to Dr. Peter Latham at the Gatsby Computational Neuroscience Unit to work on biologically plausible credit assignment algorithms. During my 1 year and 6-month tenure (Gatsby), collaborating with Dr. Tim Lillicrap from DeepMind, I sought to explore the potential of perturbation-based algorithms as candidates for learning within the brain. I refined an efficient implementation of the node perturbation algorithm and compared its learning dynamics to backpropagation when the network was trained on object classification tasks. Together with Drs. Latham and Lillicrap, we developed a novel method that harnesses the auto-differentiation functionality of modern deep learning libraries, allowing for large-scale experiments with node perturbation on convolutional architectures for the first time. We also encountered an intriguing failure mode during training where the network's performance suddenly and stubbornly declined. Our findings suggested that node perturbation may not be fast enough to effectively learn complex goal-oriented tasks that animals perform. We shared these discoveries in two papers, one of which was accepted at NeurIPS’22 [4,5], and the other at NeurIPS’21 [3].

Intrigued by the role of underlying network topology, I pondered whether we could identify neural architectures tailored to specific tasks. Can we create intelligent algorithms that evolve an optimal underlying neural circuit themselves? I then joined Dr. Frank Hutter’s Machine Learning Lab (1y 3m) to explore these questions and worked on developing efficient algorithms for automated neural network design. This journey at the University of Freiburg broadened my understanding of recent advancements in deep learning. I crafted an open-source library that empowered researchers to innovate novel evolution-based Neural Architecture Search (NAS) algorithms, as well as facilitated fair benchmarking comparisons of existing NAS algorithms – culminating in a first-author publication at ICLR’22 [2].

To apply my deep learning expertise to neuroscience modeling and bridge the gap between model and experimental design, I joined Drs. James Fitzgerald and Jan Funke's labs at the HHMI Janelia Research Campus (1y). Currently, I am using recent advancements in deep learning, specifically meta-learning, to infer the underlying synaptic plasticity rules solely by observing neuronal activity trajectories during learning. In silico, I modeled this with an artificial neural network constrained by the Drosophila Mushroom Body connectome. I collaborate closely with the experimental labs of Drs. Yoshi Aso and Glenn Turner, aiming to fit the model to recordings from Kenyon Cells (KCs), Mushroom Body Output Neurons (MBONs), and cross-compartment dopamine measurements as flies undergo first-order conditioning. As a first step, I am conducting an initial exploratory study of this approach using simulated data, with the aim of recovering known plasticity rules such as Oja's rule. To assess the robustness of this method to noise and sparseness in neuronal recordings, I collaborate with Dr. Larry Abbott’s lab as a visiting researcher at the Zuckerman Institute. In addition to noise and sparseness, we have found that our approach is also robust to other factors, including length of measured trajectory, number of trainable parameters, weight initialization, regularization, and network size. We have submitted these promising results on synthetic data to Cosyne [1].

In both academic and non-academic settings, I find joy in teaching, mentoring, and collaborating with others. During my undergraduate studies, I served as the head Teaching Assistant for the Design and Analysis of Algorithms course where I was responsible for creating and teaching tutorials for a class of 250 students. At the University of Freiburg, I volunteered as a Teaching Assistant for the Deep Learning Lab and Deep Learning courses. Together with Dr. Frank Hutter, I designed and created a new Attention and Transformers course module from scratch. I also co-supervised 2 MSc. theses and 3 research technicians. Presently, I teach an introductory machine learning class at a local high school close to Janelia, which I helped design. I believe that science flourishes when researchers teach and learn from each other, fostering interdisciplinary insights.

I am enthralled by the burgeoning field of "NeuroAI". During my graduate studies, I aspire to make strides towards reverse engineering the brain to uncover the fundamental principles that underpin learning and sensorimotor intelligence. By gaining a deeper understanding of biological learning, we can bring machine-learning algorithms closer to those in the brain – leading to better AI systems as well as more powerful theoretical paradigms to understand human cognition. My goal is to work at the interface of academia and industry to foster fruitful collaborations while taking an active role in mentoring the next generation of scientists. I am confident that the Cognitive Science Program's focus on interdisciplinary approaches, combined with my active collaborative style and enthusiasm for research, would create an environment where I can thrive. Given my experience with Drosophila connectome-constrained modeling, I would like to collaborate with xxx to uncover links between variations in circuit features and variations in behavior.
