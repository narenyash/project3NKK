Growing up in a Chinese restaurant, I developed an early revulsion for potato skins. When the restaurant stove finally cooled, I would sit by a bucket of water and skin each tater with an apple peeler, which soon got clotted with the bruised epidermal crust of the vegetable. But underneath the skin is that marvelous, milky-golden vegetable that my family and I find quite enduring.

In most American families, dessert consists of sweet pastries and chilly confections, but in mine, our dessert tradition revolves around the loaded baked potato. Indeed, on sleepy Monday evenings, we hopped in the family Nissan Pathfinder, winding our way through the hilly middle Tennessee landscape to the nearest O’Charley’s, which had the most delightful baked potatoes.

The beauty of the baked potato lies in its customizable nature. The toppings are endless: from butter to chives to even dried pork floss, each ingredient offers its own solo in this symphony of flavors. Plus, with the amount of chives that I add, I can say that I am eating my recommended daily serving of vegetables.

As my palate evolved, I found myself yearning for a culinary experience beyond the ordinary. This quest for flavor, however, occasionally veered into excess, with an almost obsessive desire to amplify every dish. The addition of copious amounts of bacon bits to my potato seemed extravagant, their marginal economic utility diminishing rapidly after the sixth spoonful. The allure of buttery goodness led me to question whether I was drawn to the humble potato itself or its rich accompaniment. Yet, in my pursuit of a multisensory feast, I neglected a fundamental truth: a dish's toppings cannot truly shine without their underlying base. The age-old adage that simplicity is beauty became lost amidst my flavor-seeking journey, and it took a while for me to rediscover the wisdom in appreciating the harmonious balance between taste and minimalism.

In the throes of adolescence, I found myself grappling with a complex brew of emotions that ultimately manifested in a decline in my academic performance. This transformation was not solely attributable to the turbulence of teenage years, nor was it entirely fueled by the insidious influence of social media, which fostered an unhealthy tendency towards self-comparison. Rather, I believe it was rooted in a deeply ingrained sense of discontent, a simmering resentment stemming from my nomadic childhood and the financial constraints that limited our family's opportunities. This resentment, compounded by a persistent desire to overcompensate for what we lacked, inadvertently led me into a cycle of oversleeping, apathy, and an overall disengagement with daily life.

In the crucible of adversity and resentment, I discovered transformation. Each potato, scarred with unsightly blemishes and protrusions akin to moles, served as an object of intrigue when I stumbled upon one bearing sprouts - aptly named "potato eyes." These eyes signified regeneration, symbolizing the endless possibility of growing numerous potatoes throughout my lifetime. They were a poignant reminder that even when facing imperfect circumstances, there is always room for growth and change.

This simple vegetable, a New World commodity facilitated by the Columbian Exchange, offers an enlightening perspective on resilience and adaptation. Initially slow to thrive in European conditions, potatoes eventually took root in the cool, continental lowlands. Similarly, I have learned that setbacks do not define my journey, but rather serve as stepping stones towards growth and self-discovery.

Born in humble circumstances, I was delivered feet-first and remained silent until the age of three, learning early lessons in resilience and adaptability. My upbringing was characterized by simple pleasures, such as poring over worn copies of National Geographic, captivated by the intricate cuneiform etched onto clay tablets and the grandeur of Mesoamerican temples. My life's journey has been marked by significant geographical transitions, from the bustling streets of Coney Island to the lush palmetto groves of the Carolinas, and the verdant fields of Middle Tennessee - even navigating the occasional pothole along the way. A shared meal of orange chicken and white rice at our family restaurant after the daily hustle offered a poignant reminder of the enduring values that anchor me - a testament to my roots and the importance they continue to hold in my life.

People often joke about those who order the same thing off the menu, or those who choose level one on a five-point scale of spiciness at the local Thai restaurant. Yes, I am that person. But I’ve learned to appreciate the nuance of simplicity. Sweet potatoes are tempting, but that familiar love and warmth will remain in my baked Russet potato.
